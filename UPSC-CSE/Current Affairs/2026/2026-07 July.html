◆ What this file is, and what it deliberately is not

The eight current-affairs questions traceable in Prelims 2025 asked for: BIMSTEC's member count and founding declaration; the Gandhi Peace Prize jury; who co-chaired the Paris AI Summit; the BRICS chair, host city and theme; and the scoreline of a Kho Kho final. Not one asked for analysis. Every one asked a verifiable identity fact about a named entity.

So these cards carry facts you can be tested on — founded when, how many, who chairs, what changed, which number. The argument lives separately, in the Mains tab, where it earns marks. That separation is the whole design.

Honest scope: this file covers the July 2026 legislative and Cabinet cycle, which is the densest and most reliably-examined part of the month. It is not a complete record of everything that happened in July, and no note can be — see Part 1 of the method file on why chasing completeness costs more than it returns.

Legislation — Monsoon Session 2026

The session began 20 July 2026 and was scheduled for 19 sittings. Five Bills were introduced and two passed in the period covered here. Session facts — start date, number of sittings, Bills introduced versus passed — are themselves a standard question type.

Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026

Legislation · Judiciary · ★★★★★
What
Raises the sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court.
The numbers
From 33 to 37 judges excluding the Chief Justice — so total strength becomes 38.
Amends
The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956.
Route
Replaces the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026, promulgated 16 May 2026. Introduced in the Lok Sabha, Monsoon Session.
Constitutional hook
Article 124(1) — Parliament may by law prescribe a larger number of judges. The Constitution fixed only 7 plus the CJI originally.
Why it matters
A live example of Parliament exercising Art 124(1), and of the ordinance-then-Bill route you studied under Art 123.
Confusable with: The collegium question, which is about who appoints (Art 124(2), the Fourth Judges Case). This Bill is only about how many.

Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Amendment Bill, 2026

Legislation · Governance · ★★★★☆
Status
Passed by Parliament on 30 July 2026; introduced in the Lok Sabha 27 July 2026.
Amends
The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024.
Who it covers
Examinations by UPSC, SSC and the National Testing Agency, among specified authorities.
Penalties raised
Individual use of unfair means: from 3–5 years and ₹10 lakh, to 5–10 years and ₹50 lakh. Service-provider fine: ₹1 crore → ₹5 crore. Debarment: 4 → 8 years.
New machinery
A Special Task Force for investigation, to be completed in 2 months; Special Fast Track Courts; appeals to a two-judge High Court bench, disposed of within 3 months as far as possible.
Confusable with: The 2024 parent Act — questions may ask which provision is new. Everything above is the 2026 amendment.

The distinction that decides half of these questions: introduced versus passed. Passed by Parliament: Public Examinations (Unfair Means) and Prevention of Insults to National Honour. A statement saying a Bill "was enacted" when it was only introduced is the commonest CA trap in Polity.

Static linkage map

◆ Why this section exists

UPSC rarely asks a current-affairs fact naked. It asks the fact riding on a static hook — the Article the Bill amends, the List the subject sits in, the constitutional body the scheme runs through. A candidate who read the news but not the Constitution gets these wrong; so does a candidate who knows the Constitution but not the news. The question is engineered to need both.

Your own Week 3 paper proved it. Q19 asked what happens when the Budget fails to pass — pure static Polity, triggered by a budget-season stem. Q30 needed the RPA. Q38 needed Article 163. So this map is not decoration: it is where roughly half of this file's questions come from.

July 2026 itemThe static anchor a question will ride on

The Bill also replaces an Ordinance — so Article 123: promulgated only when both Houses are not in session, and it must be laid before Parliament and ceases six weeks from reassembly.
Public Examinations AmendmentThe Act covers UPSC, SSC and NTA — but only the UPSC is a constitutional body, under Articles 315–323. SSC and NTA are executive creations. A statement calling all three constitutional bodies is a ready-made trap.
Insults to National Honour AmendmentArticle 51A(a) lists the duty to respect the National Flag and the National Anthem — it does not mention the national song. The three hooks that carry most linkage questions. (1) Which List? Every scheme and Bill sits somewhere in the Seventh Schedule, and a State-List subject funded by the Union means Article 282. (2) Constitutional or statutory or executive? UPSC is constitutional; the Bar Council is statutory; NITI Aayog and NTA are executive. (3) Which Article does it amend or rest on? Learn the anchor, not just the headline.

Mains material

Four answers, written to the full MAINS_ANSWER_CRAFT.md standard — a real question with a directive, a skeleton, a model answer inside its word budget, the value-additions named, the cut/bought trade shown, and the trap that would cost marks. C1Practice · GS-II Judiciary15 marks · 250 words

"Raising the sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court treats a symptom while leaving the disease untouched." Critically examine in the light of recent legislative action.

Directive: "Critically examine." Take the claim apart and deliver a verdict. An answer that only defends the increase, or only attacks it, is capped however well written.
Skeleton
  1. The move, with the constitutional hook
  2. The case that it helps
  3. Why the claim has force — the real constraint
  4. Verdict and way forward
Enriches: M11 (NJAC) — use the unfinalised MoP link · M20 (parliamentary system) — judicial capacity as an accountability question.

Model answer

Article 124(1) fixed the Supreme Court at seven judges besides the Chief Justice and left the rest to Parliament. The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026 exercises that power, raising strength from 33 to 37 excluding the CJI — replacing an Ordinance of 16 May 2026 and amending the 1956 Act.

The case for

Pendency runs into tens of thousands of matters. More judges allow more benches to sit in parallel — which matters because Article 145(3) requires five judges for any substantial question of constitutional interpretation, and references are queued: the Money Bill reference from Rojer Mathew (2019) before a seven-judge bench, among others.

Why the claim has force

Strength has been raised repeatedly since 1956 without pendency falling, which suggests the binding constraint lies elsewhere. Three candidates: vacancies persist for months against existing strength, because the Memorandum of Procedure has remained unfinalised for a decade after the Fourth Judges Case (2015); the Court's docket composition is dominated by routine appeals rather than constitutional questions; and there is no structural filter between the High Courts and the apex court.

Verdict

The increase is necessary but not sufficient — a larger sanctioned strength is meaningless if appointments do not follow. The sequence should be a settled MoP with time-bound appointment, then a serious examination of the National Court of Appeal proposal, so that added capacity is spent on constitutional adjudication rather than absorbed by the appellate backlog.

Value additions used: Art 124(1) and the original seven-judge figure · Art 145(3)'s five-judge rule · Rojer Mathew (2019) pending reference · Fourth Judges Case (2015) and the unfinalised MoP · vacancy-versus-strength distinction · National Court of Appeal.
Budget check: 258 / 250 words. Cut: the Ordinance's Article 123 mechanics and a sentence on historical strength increases. Bought: the three named constraints, which is what converts a description into the "critically" half.
What would cost you marks: describing the Bill without engaging the claim — "critically examine" is not decorative; and omitting the vacancy-versus-sanctioned-strength distinction, which is the entire argument.
C2Practice · GS-II Governance15 marks · 250 words

"Escalating penalties has become the default response to examination malpractice, but deterrence without capacity does not prevent leaks." Discuss with reference to recent legislation.

Directive: "Discuss." Present both sides and arrive at a position. The tail ("deterrence without capacity") tells you where the weight lies.
Skeleton
  1. What the 2026 amendment does
  2. The case for deterrence
  3. Why capacity is the binding constraint
  4. Way forward
Enriches: any GS-II answer on recruitment integrity, institutional trust, or regulation of private service providers in public functions.

Model answer

The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Amendment Bill, 2026, passed on 30 July 2026, amends the 2024 Act covering examinations by the UPSC, SSC and NTA. Individual penalties rise from 3–5 years and ₹10 lakh to 5–10 years and ₹50 lakh; the service-provider fine from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore; debarment from four to eight years. It adds Special Task Forces with a two-month investigation limit, Special Fast Track Courts, and appeals to a two-judge High Court bench.

The case for deterrence

A leak harms lakhs simultaneously and corrodes the belief that selection is merit-based — the foundation of a constitutional civil service under Articles 315–323. Penalties should be proportionate to that scale. The procedural additions matter more than the numbers: prosecutions have historically collapsed through delay, and a statutory time limit attacks precisely that.

Why capacity binds

The conduct was already criminal under the 2024 Act, so escalation alone changes little. Leaks are a systems-security failure: question-paper custody, transmission, printing and storage are largely outsourced to private service providers, and the point of compromise is usually logistical rather than judicial. Deterrence acts after the harm; process design prevents it.

Way forward

Treat examination integrity as a security architecture — end-to-end custody audit, encrypted staggered digital delivery, biometric verification, accreditation of service providers, and a published accountability report after every affected examination.

Value additions used: the 2024 Act and its 2026 amendment with exact penalty figures · UPSC/SSC/NTA as covered authorities, only the first constitutional · Arts 315–323 · the deterrence-versus-capacity framing · outsourcing as the point of compromise.
Budget check: 262 / 250 words. Cut: the appeal-bench detail and the debarment period from the body — both survive in the fact card. Bought: the systems-security framing and the five-item way forward, which is where "discuss" is actually satisfied.
What would cost you marks: listing the new penalties and stopping; and missing that only the UPSC among the three named bodies is constitutional — an easy line that signals precision.
C3Practice · GS-III Economy15 marks · 250 words

"India's semiconductor push must sequence its ambitions rather than pursue them simultaneously." Examine in the light of Semicon 2.0.

Directive: "Examine." Investigate the claim and its basis. Less adversarial than "critically examine", but a position is still required.
Skeleton
  1. The scheme and its architecture
  2. Why the ambition is defensible
  3. Why sequencing matters
  4. Position
Enriches: GS-III answers on industrial policy, supply-chain resilience and PLI-type subsidy design.

Model answer

Semicon 2.0, approved on 15 July 2026 with an outlay of ₹1,27,500 crore, builds the ecosystem on six pillars — Design, Machines and Materials, Fabs, ATMP/OSAT, R&D and Talent — alongside a ₹62,500 crore Mobile Phone Manufacturing Scheme. Parliament's competence rests on Entry 52 of the Union List, industries declared expedient in the public interest.

Why the ambition is defensible

Semiconductors are a strategic input to defence, telecom, automotive and digital payments; the post-2020 disruptions exposed the risk of concentrated supply. India already has a design base — the scheme's own Design pillar cites 105 startups developing chips — so the capability gap is in manufacturing, not in engineering talent at the front end.

Why sequencing matters

Leading-edge fabrication is capital-hungry, cyclical and dominated by incumbents with decades of yield learning; subsidy-led entry can create capacity without competitiveness. ATMP/OSAT — assembly, testing, marking and packaging — is labour-intensive, lower-capital and the segment where India can compete now. The genuine constraint is talent: India designs a large share of the world's chips but has almost no manufacturing workforce, and a fab without operators is an idle asset.

Position

The claim holds. Sequence talent and ATMP first, leading-edge fabs later, and tie disbursement to output milestones rather than announced investment — the design flaw that has weakened earlier incentive schemes.

Value additions used: ₹1,27,500 crore and the six pillars named · 105 design startups from the PIB release · ATMP/OSAT explained, not just cited · Entry 52 of the Union List · milestone-linked disbursement.
Budget check: 261 / 250 words. Cut: the Mobile Phone Manufacturing Scheme's own rationale, which is a separate answer. Bought: the 105-startups figure and the ATMP expansion — both come from the primary release and neither appears in news summaries.
What would cost you marks: writing "semiconductors are strategic" without naming a single pillar or figure; and treating fabs and ATMP as one thing, which is the distinction the question turns on.
C4Practice · GS-II Polity10 marks · 150 words

"Shifting statutory thresholds to executive notification improves responsiveness but weakens parliamentary scrutiny." Comment with reference to a recent Bill.

Directive: "Comment." Form a judgement and support it. Shorter and more opinionated than "examine" — but the opinion must be evidenced.
Skeleton
  1. The move
  2. The case for delegation
  3. The scrutiny cost
  4. Verdict
Enriches: M29 (privileges and codification) and any answer on delegated legislation — it pairs directly with the Committee on Subordinate Legislation from Topic 5, Note 03.

Model answer

The MSME Development (Amendment) Bill, 2026, introduced in the Rajya Sabha, removes the investment thresholds written into the 2006 Act and empowers the central government to notify classification criteria based on investment and turnover.

The case for delegation

Thresholds fixed in primary legislation go stale with inflation and require an amendment each time — a slow instrument for a fast-moving definition. Delegation permits periodic revision without occupying parliamentary time.

The scrutiny cost

That definition determines eligibility for priority-sector credit, procurement preference and tax treatment. Moving it to notification shifts a consequential distributive choice from Parliament to the executive — the classic delegated legislation problem the Committee on Subordinate Legislation exists to police, alongside the requirement that rules be laid before Parliament.

The trade-off is real but manageable. Delegate the numbers, retain the criteria in the statute, and require every notification to be laid — responsiveness without surrendering the scrutiny that makes it legitimate.

Value additions used: the 2006 Act and its 2026 amendment · Rajya Sabha origin as evidence it is an ordinary Bill · the three consequences of MSME classification · Committee on Subordinate Legislation · the laying requirement.
Budget check: 168 / 150 words. Cut: the TReDS mandate for CPSEs — a genuinely good fact, but it belongs to the delayed-payment question, not the scrutiny one. Bought: the "delegate the numbers, retain the criteria" formulation, which is the answer's whole payload.
What would cost you marks: describing the Bill without taking a position — "comment" demands one; and omitting the Committee on Subordinate Legislation, which is the institutional anchor that turns opinion into constitutional argument.

Linkage questions — current affairs riding on a static hook

These are the ones that decide the section. Each needs both the July fact and a static provision; knowing only one gets you nowhere.

Linkage · Direct

The number of judges of the Supreme Court, other than the Chief Justice of India, is determined by:

  • (a) A provision of the Constitution that can be changed only by amendment
  • (b) A law made by Parliament
  • (c) An order of the President on the Collegium's recommendation
  • (d) A rule framed by the Supreme Court itself
Answer: (b) — a law made by Parliament
  • Article 124(1) fixed the original strength at seven judges besides the CJI and left the rest to Parliament — which is why the 2026 Bill raising it from 33 to 37 is an ordinary law, not a constitutional amendment.
  • (c) confuses how many (Art 124(1)) with who (Art 124(2) and the collegium). That is the trap.
  • The static hook is what makes this answerable without having read the news at all — and the news is what makes it topical.
Linkage · Statements

The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026 replaces an Ordinance. With reference to the ordinance-making power, consider the following statements:

  1. An ordinance can be promulgated only when both Houses of Parliament are not in session.
  2. It must be laid before both Houses and ceases to operate six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament.
  3. An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three

Linkage · Direct (the highest-value question in this file)

Agriculture is a State List subject and sports is a State List subject. The constitutional provision that nevertheless enables the Union to fund PM-KISAN and Khelo India is:

  • (a) Article 246 read with the Union List
  • (b) Article 249
  • (c) Article 282
  • (d) Article 293
Answer: (c) — Article 282
  • Article 282 lets the Union or a State make grants for any public purpose, even on subjects outside its own legislative competence. It is the constitutional basis of every Central Sector and Centrally Sponsored Scheme operating in a State subject.
  • Agriculture is Entry 14 and sports Entry 33 of the State List — so without Art 282 these schemes would be constitutionally awkward.
  • This is also a live federalism argument: States and the Punchhi Commission have criticised scheme-based spending as encroachment on State autonomy through the purse.
  • (b) Article 249 is the Rajya Sabha resolution route to legislate on a State subject — a different mechanism entirely. (d) Article 293 is State borrowing.
