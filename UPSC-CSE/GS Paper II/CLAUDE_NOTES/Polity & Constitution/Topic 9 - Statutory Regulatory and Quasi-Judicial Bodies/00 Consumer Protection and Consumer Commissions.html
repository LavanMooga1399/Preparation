Consumer Protection & Consumer Commissions

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 — a three-tier adjudicating pyramid, a new regulator with suo motu powers, and the first statutory scheme of product liability in Indian law. None of it is constitutional, and the exam's first question is always exactly that.

Act of 2019 CCPA · 2020 ₹50 lakh / ₹2 crore Product liability Statutory, not constitutional
3tiers · and 0 constitutional status
◆ Where the marks are, and where they are not

This note opens Topic 9 — Statutory, regulatory and various quasi-judicial bodies. Read the syllabus line carefully: the topic is defined by what these bodies are not. Everything here sits outside the Constitution, and this setter's single most repeated question in the whole area is “how many of the following are constitutional bodies?” — asked in 2023 with the NCDRC among the options, and in 2025 with the Inter-State Council. Both are reproduced below.

Do not learn consumer law. Learn the institutional architecture: who adjudicates at what value, who regulates, who appeals where, and what the 2019 Act added that the 1986 Act lacked.

Week 6, Days 1–2. The tribunals, Lok Adalats, Gram Nyayalayas and Article 39A also listed for those days are already covered in Topic 6, Note 07 — read that alongside this rather than expecting it repeated here.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

A consumer who has been sold a defective product has a real problem: the loss is small, and an ordinary civil suit costs more than the loss. So Parliament built a parallel, cheap, quick forum — no court fee worth the name, no requirement of a lawyer, and a decision in months rather than years.

The 1986 Act created three tiers of that forum. The 2019 Act replaced it and added three genuinely new things. First, a regulator — the Central Consumer Protection Authority — which can act on its own motion, without waiting for anyone to complain. Second, product liability, letting a consumer sue the manufacturer, the seller or the service provider for harm caused by a defective product. Third, express coverage of e-commerce and of unfair contracts.

The vocabulary changed too, and the exam uses the new words: the 1986 Act had Forums; the 2019 Act has Commissions. A question calling the district body a “District Forum” is describing the repealed Act.

01

The three tiers — and the money

TierPecuniary jurisdictionComposition & appointment
District CommissionConsideration paid up to ₹50 lakhA President and at least two members, appointed by the State Government.
State CommissionAbove ₹50 lakh and up to ₹2 croreA President and at least four members, appointed by the State Government.
National Commission (NCDRC)Above ₹2 croreA President and at least four members, appointed by the Central Government.
!
The limits you may have memorised are the old ones. The 2019 Act as enacted set ₹1 crore / ₹10 crore as the thresholds. Those were revised downward by rules notified on 31 December 2021 to ₹50 lakh / ₹2 crore, because the original figures had pushed a flood of cases down into the District Commissions and created pendency. Any source written between 2019 and 2021 carries the superseded numbers — and this is precisely the kind of quietly-changed figure this setter likes.
!
Jurisdiction is decided by the CONSIDERATION PAID, not by the compensation claimed. This is the sharpest change from the 1986 Act, under which the value of the goods plus the compensation claimed set the tier. Under the 2019 Act only the consideration paid counts — so a ₹40 lakh purchase with a ₹5 crore damages claim is still a District Commission matter. The Supreme Court has upheld the consideration-based test. If a stem gives you two figures, the one to use is what the consumer paid.
Procedural pointDetail
LimitationA complaint must be filed within two years of the cause of action; delay may be condoned for sufficient cause recorded in writing.
AppealDistrict → State → National → Supreme Court, 30 days at each stage. An appeal from the NCDRC lies to the Supreme Court only where the NCDRC exercised original jurisdiction.
Pre-depositA person ordered to pay must deposit 50% of the amount before appealing — the 1986 Act's varying caps were replaced by this flat rule.
Where a complaint may be filedA significant 2019 change: the consumer may file where he ordinarily resides or works, not only where the opposite party carries on business.
Nature of the bodyQuasi-judicial. The Commissions have the powers of a civil court on specified matters and their orders are enforceable as decrees.
02

What the 2019 Act added — regulator, liability, e-commerce

The Central Consumer Protection Authority

PointDetail
Established24 July 2020, under the 2019 Act. A regulator, not an adjudicator — this is the cleanest way to hold it apart from the Commissions.
CompositionA Chief Commissioner and Commissioners, appointed by the Central Government. Under the Ministry of Consumer Affairs.
Investigation wingHeaded by a Director-General, who may inquire or investigate into violations.
PowersAct suo motu; order recall of goods; order reimbursement of the price paid; cancel licences; file class action where more than one consumer is affected; and issue directions against false or misleading advertisements, with penalties on manufacturers and on endorsers.
Why it mattersThe Commissions can act only when someone complains. The CCPA can move before anyone does — which is the entire point of adding a regulator to an adjudicating system.
!
Endorser liability is a favourite. Under the 2019 Act a celebrity who endorses a false or misleading advertisement can be penalised and prohibited from making any endorsement for up to one year, extending to three years for a repeat offence. It is a defence that the endorser exercised due diligence to verify the claim. This is new law with no equivalent in the 1986 Act, and it is exactly the sort of provision a current-affairs-minded setter reaches for.

Product liability — the second addition

◆
A product liability action may be brought against the product manufacturer, the product service provider or the product seller — all three are exposed, which is the change. The claim lies for harm caused by a manufacturing defect, a design defect, a deviation from manufacturing specifications, a failure to conform to an express warranty, or inadequate instructions or warnings. Note the defences: a seller is not liable if he merely sold the product without altering it and exercised reasonable care; and no liability attaches where the product was misused, altered or modified by the consumer.

E-commerce and unfair contracts — the third

ItemDetail
E-Commerce Rules, 2020Made under the Act. Require disclosure of the seller's identity, country of origin, return and refund policy and grievance-officer details; prohibit cancelling an order unilaterally in a way that causes loss, and manipulation of price.
Unfair contractA wholly new statutory concept — a contract causing significant change in consumer rights, including excessive security deposits, disproportionate penalties, unilateral termination, and refusal to accept early repayment.
MediationA consumer mediation cell is attached to each Commission at every tier. A Commission may refer a dispute to mediation where an element of settlement exists and both parties agree. A settlement so reached is not appealable.
Six consumer rightsSafety · information · choice · to be heard · redressal · consumer education. Carried over from the 1986 Act and still asked as a list.

Pass 2 — the adjacent surface

◆ Why this section exists — and why it is unusually large here

For this topic the Pass-2 surface is the topic. The syllabus line reads “Statutory, regulatory and various quasi-judicial bodies”, so a question can come from any body in the Indian state that is not constitutional. The consumer commissions are one worked example; the classification skill is the actual deliverable.

The three words in the syllabus line are not synonyms, and telling them apart is the whole game.

The three categories the syllabus names

CategoryMeaningExamples
StatutoryCreated by an Act of Parliament or a State legislature.NHRC (1993) · NCW (1990) · CVC (2003) · CIC (RTI Act, 2005) · Lokpal (2013) · NCM (1992) · Consumer Commissions · NCDRC
RegulatoryA statutory body whose function is to license, set standards and police a sector — usually with rule-making power of its own.RBI (1934) · SEBI (1992) · TRAI (1997) · IRDAI (1999) · PFRDA (2013) · CCI (2002) · FSSAI (2006) · CCPA (2019)
Quasi-judicialA body that adjudicates — hears parties, takes evidence, issues enforceable orders — without being a court.NGT (2010) · NCLT / NCLAT · SAT · TDSAT · CAT (Art 323A) · Consumer Commissions · and the regulators when exercising adjudicatory powers
!
The categories overlap, and that overlap is examinable. A body can be all three at once: SEBI is statutory (Act of 1992), regulatory (it licenses and sets standards) and quasi-judicial (it adjudicates and penalises). So a question asking whether SEBI is “a regulatory body” and whether it is “a quasi-judicial body” can have both answers correct. Do not treat the three labels as mutually exclusive — the mistake is to assume one excludes the others.

◆
Three pairs that are built to be confused, and are worth learning as pairs. (1) Inter-State Council (Art 263, constitutional) versus Zonal Councils (States Reorganisation Act 1956, statutory) — and note the North Eastern Council is statutory too, under its own 1971 Act. (2) NCBC (constitutional since 2018) versus NCM, NHRC, NCW (all statutory). (3) CVC (statutory since 2003, executive before) versus CAG (constitutional, Art 148) — one audits, one investigates vigilance, and their statuses differ.

(A) Genuine previous-year questions

2023UPSC Prelims · How many

Consider the following organizations/bodies in India:

  1. The National Commission for Backward Classes
  2. The National Human Rights Commission
  3. The National Law Commission
  4. The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission

How many of the above are constitutional bodies?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (a) — Only one
  • Only the NCBC, under Art 338B since the 102nd Amendment, 2018.
  • The NCDRC — this note's subject — is statutory, under the Consumer Protection Act. So is the NHRC (1993). The Law Commission is merely executive, constituted by resolution.
  • This is the single most repeated question shape in Topic 9. Before learning what a body does, fix what it is.
2025UPSC Prelims · How many

With reference to India, consider the following:

  1. The Inter-State Council
  2. The National Security Council
  3. Zonal Councils

How many of the above were established as per the provisions of the Constitution of India?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All the three
  • (d) None
Answer: (a) — Only one
  • Only the Inter-State Council, under Article 263.
  • Zonal Councils are statutory — created by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, not by the Constitution. They feel constitutional because they sit in the federal chapter of every textbook, which is exactly why they work as a distractor.
  • The National Security Council is executive, created by government decision in 1998.
  • Three bodies, three different statuses, one question. This is the classification drill in its purest form.
2015UPSC Prelims · Direct

The Government of India has established NITI Aayog to replace the

  • (a) Human Rights Commission
  • (b) Finance Commission
  • (c) Law Commission
  • (d) Planning Commission
Answer: (d) — Planning Commission
  • Both are executive bodies created by resolution — neither has ever been constitutional or statutory.
  • The trap in option (b): the Finance Commission is constitutional (Art 280) and was never replaced by anything. NITI Aayog took over the Planning Commission's role, not the Finance Commission's.

(B) Practice questions

Practice · How many

Consider the following statements about the Consumer Protection Act, 2019:

  1. The District Commission has jurisdiction where the consideration paid does not exceed fifty lakh rupees.
  2. The pecuniary jurisdiction is determined by the compensation claimed by the complainant.
  3. A complaint must ordinarily be filed within two years of the cause of action.
  4. A consumer may file a complaint where he ordinarily resides or works.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c) — only three
  • Statements 1, 3 and 4 are correct.
  • Statement 2 is the central error of this chapter: jurisdiction follows the consideration paid, never the compensation claimed. A ₹40 lakh purchase with a ₹5 crore claim stays in the District Commission.
  • Statement 4 marks a real change from the 1986 Act, under which the consumer had to travel to where the opposite party did business.
Practice · How many

Consider the following bodies:

  1. Central Consumer Protection Authority
  2. National Green Tribunal
  3. NITI Aayog
  4. Competition Commission of India

How many of the bodies given above are statutory bodies?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c) — only three
  • The CCPA (Consumer Protection Act, 2019), the NGT (NGT Act, 2010) and the CCI (Competition Act, 2002) are all statutory.
  • NITI Aayog is executive — a Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015. It is the standard intruder in any list of this kind.
  • Note that none of the four is constitutional, which is the follow-up question a setter would ask next.
Practice · Negative

Which one of the following was NOT introduced by the Consumer Protection Act, 2019?

  • (a) A statutory scheme of product liability
  • (b) A central regulator able to act suo motu
  • (c) The three-tier structure of consumer redressal
  • (d) Mediation as a mode of settling consumer disputes
Answer: (c) is NOT new
  • The three-tier structure came from the 1986 Act — what the 2019 Act changed was the names (Forum became Commission) and the money limits.
  • The genuinely new items are product liability, the CCPA, mediation, express e-commerce coverage, unfair contracts and endorser liability.
  • A question asking what an amending Act “introduced” is really asking what existed before it.
Practice · Statements

Consider the following statements about the Central Consumer Protection Authority:

  1. It can initiate action on its own motion without a complaint.
  2. It has an investigation wing headed by a Director-General.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (c) — Both 1 and 2
  • Both accurate. The suo motu power is the whole reason a regulator was added to a system that already had adjudicators.
  • Another deliberate “both correct” set — your measured instinct is to reject option (c) or (d) assuming something must be wrong, and that instinct is what these are here to train out.
Practice · Pairs

Consider the following pairs of body and its founding basis:

  1. Inter-State Council — Article 263
  2. Zonal Councils — States Reorganisation Act, 1956
  3. National Security Council — Article 352
  4. Central Vigilance Commission — CVC Act, 2003

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c) — only three
  • Pairs 1, 2 and 4 are correct.
  • Pair 3 is false twice over: the National Security Council is an executive creation of 1998 with no constitutional basis at all, and Article 352 is the National Emergency provision — an article dragged in from an unrelated chapter.
  • The CVC is worth its own line: executive from 1964 on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation, statutory only from 2003.
Practice · Assertion–Reason

Consider the following two statements:

  1. Statement-I: The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 created the Central Consumer Protection Authority in addition to the consumer commissions.
  2. Statement-II: A consumer commission can act only when a complaint is brought before it.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

  • (a) Both correct and Statement-II explains Statement-I
  • (b) Both correct but Statement-II does not explain Statement-I
  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is not
  • (d) Statement-I is not correct but Statement-II is
Answer: (a)
  • Both correct, and the second is precisely why the first was necessary: an adjudicator is reactive by nature, so systemic harms — a misleading advertisement, an unsafe product line — needed a body that could move first.
  • That sentence is the cleanest one-line justification for the CCPA in a Mains answer.
Practice · Negative

Which one of the following statements is NOT correct?

  • (a) An appeal from an order of the District Commission lies to the State Commission within thirty days
  • (b) A settlement arrived at through mediation under the Act is appealable
  • (c) A person directed to pay an amount must deposit fifty per cent of it before appealing
  • (d) The consumer commissions are quasi-judicial bodies
Answer: (b) is NOT correct
  • A mediated settlement is not appealable — mediation only proceeds where both parties agree, so there is nothing left to contest.
  • This is the note's cleanest “rule with a carve-out”: every order is appealable except this one. An absolute in a stem — every, all, any — is the signal to check the Ledger.

Twenty-one of the fifty questions in the Week 4 paper were “how many of the above” — 42 per cent — scoring 52 per cent. It cannot be solved by elimination; each statement stands alone.

The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission is a constitutional body.

  • (b) False

Under the 2019 Act a celebrity endorser of a misleading advertisement may be barred from endorsements.

A product liability action may be brought only against the manufacturer.

  • (b) False

Trap 16 · True or False

The National Security Council is a statutory body.

SEBI is a statutory body and also exercises quasi-judicial functions.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False

  • Statutory (Act of 1992), regulatory and quasi-judicial at once. The three labels are not mutually exclusive — assuming they are is the trap.

The Central Vigilance Commission has been a statutory body since 1964.

  • (a) True

Appeals from the Competition Commission of India lie to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal.

The consumer commissions have the powers of a civil court in respect of specified matters.

  • Which is what makes them quasi-judicial, and what makes their orders enforceable as decrees.

M52Practice · Week 615 marks · 250 words

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 shifted consumer justice from adjudication alone to regulation. Examine the significance of that shift, and the limits it has run into.

Directive: "Examine." The shift is real and the marks lie in naming why adjudication alone was insufficient — then conceding, specifically, where the new design underdelivers.
Skeleton
  1. What the 1986 design could and could not do
  2. The three additions of 2019
  3. Why regulation was the necessary addition
  4. The limits
  5. Verdict

Model answer

The 1986 Act built a three-tier adjudicating pyramid — cheap, lawyer-optional and quick by design. Its structural limit was that it was reactive: a Commission moves only when a consumer complains, and the consumers least able to complain are those the market harms most.

What 2019 added

Three things. A regulator — the CCPA (2020) — able to act suo motu, recall goods, cancel licences, bring class actions and penalise misleading advertisements and their endorsers. A statutory scheme of product liability reaching the manufacturer, the seller and the service provider alike. And express coverage of e-commerce, together with the new category of the unfair contract.

Why regulation was the missing piece

Individual adjudication cannot address a systemic harm. One misleading advertisement injures millions in amounts too small for any of them to litigate — a collective-action problem no forum can solve by deciding cases one at a time. Only a body that can move before a complaint, and act for a class, reaches it.

The limits

Access was quietly narrowed and then partly restored: the 2019 thresholds of ₹1 crore and ₹10 crore pushed cases downward and worsened District pendency, forcing revision to ₹50 lakh and ₹2 crore in December 2021. Vacancies and infrastructure remain the binding constraint on speed, and a scheme whose promise is speed is discredited by delay more than by any doctrinal flaw.

The shift was correct in principle. Its delivery still turns on ordinary administrative capacity, which no amount of statutory design supplies.

Value additions used: the reactive-adjudication diagnosis · CCPA 2020 with suo motu and class action · product liability's three defendants · endorser liability · the 2021 threshold revision with its stated reason.

Last-mile revision — Consumer Protection & Consumer Commissions

  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019 repealed the 1986 Act. 1986 had Forums; 2019 has Commissions.
  • Tiers & money (since 31 Dec 2021): District up to ₹50 lakh · State ₹50 lakh–₹2 crore · National above ₹2 crore. As enacted the limits were ₹1 crore / ₹10 crore — superseded.
  • Jurisdiction follows the CONSIDERATION PAID, never the compensation claimed. (Under the 1986 Act the claim did count.)
  • Appoints: State Government for District & State Commissions · Central Government for the NCDRC and the CCPA.
  • Numbers trio: complaint within 2 years · appeal within 30 days at each stage · pre-deposit 50%. File where the consumer resides or works.
  • CCPA — 24 July 2020. A regulator, not an adjudicator. Suo motu · recall · reimbursement · licence cancellation · class action · misleading-advertisement and endorser penalties (ban 1 yr, repeat 3 yrs). Investigation wing under a Director-General.
  • Product liability — against manufacturer · service provider · seller. Defences: seller who neither altered nor failed in care; misuse or modification by the consumer.
  • New in 2019: product liability · CCPA · mediation · e-commerce · unfair contracts · endorser liability. Not new: the three-tier structure and the six consumer rights.
  • A mediated settlement is NOT appealable — the one carve-out in the appeal ladder.
  • STATUS — the topic's real question. Consumer Commissions, CCPA, NHRC, NCW, NCM, CVC (2003), CIC, Lokpal, NGT, SEBI, CCI, TRAI, Zonal Councils = statutory. NITI Aayog, Law Commission, National Security Council, SSC, NDC = executive. Inter-State Council (Art 263) = constitutional.
  • PYQ pattern: 2023 — of NCBC/NHRC/Law Commission/NCDRC, only one constitutional. 2025 — of Inter-State Council/NSC/Zonal Councils, only one. Fix status before anything else.
  • Appeal routes: consumer → Supreme Court · NGT → Supreme Court · CCI → NCLAT · CAT → High Court after L. Chandra Kumar.
  • Cross-link: tribunals, CAT, Lok Adalats, Gram Nyayalayas, Art 39A → Topic 6, Note 07.
