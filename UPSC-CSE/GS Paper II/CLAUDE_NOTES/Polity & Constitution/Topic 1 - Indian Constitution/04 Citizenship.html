◆ How this note fits the wider set

This note is the second half of "significant provisions" alongside Union & its Territory (Note 3) — kept as its own file because Citizenship has an entirely distinct set of governing articles (5–11, not 1–4), its own standalone Act (1955), and its own separately-testable amendment history culminating in the 2019 Act.

A note on scope: the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 is a live policy debate. This file states its provisions, its legal challenges, and verified exam questions about it factually — it does not take a position on whether the Act is desirable, only on what it says and what has actually been asked about it.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Citizenship answers a deceptively simple question: who actually counts as "belonging" to India, with the full rights that come with it — voting, holding public office, an Indian passport? The Constitution itself only handles this question for one narrow moment in time — everyone who was already resident in India at 26 January 1950, the day it commenced — because it needed a starting list before anything else could function. That one-time list is Articles 5-11, covered first below.

But a country obviously needs rules for everyone born, or arriving, or leaving after that starting day too — and the Constitution deliberately does not spell those out itself. Instead it hands the job to Parliament via ordinary law, which is why the real rulebook for citizenship today is the Citizenship Act, 1955 (updated many times since) rather than the Constitution's own text. That single design choice — "the Constitution sets the starting list, Parliament runs everything after" — is why this note has two very different halves: a short constitutional core (Articles 5-11) and a much longer, frequently-amended statute (the 1955 Act, including its most controversial recent update, the CAA 2019).

Also worth knowing before the detail: India recognises exactly one citizenship, not a separate "State citizenship" the way the US has separate state and federal citizenship — wherever you live in India, your citizenship status is identical and comes only from the Union, never from a State.

02

Citizenship — Articles 5 to 11 & the Citizenship Act, 1955

Articles 5–11 are transitional provisions — they answer only "who was a citizen on 26 January 1950," not "who is a citizen today." That ongoing question was handed to Parliament via Article 11, which is why almost everything examinable beyond the commencement rules comes from the Citizenship Act, 1955 and its amendments, not from the Constitution's text itself.

Articles 5–11 at a glance

ArticleWhat it covers
Article 5Citizenship at commencement for persons domiciled in India — by birth in India, or a parent born in India, or five years' ordinary residence before commencement.
Article 6Citizenship for persons who migrated to India from Pakistan before/after 19 July 1948, subject to residence and registration conditions.
Article 7Citizenship for persons who migrated to Pakistan but later returned to India under a resettlement/permanent-return permit.
Article 8Citizenship for persons of Indian origin residing outside India, if they or a parent/grandparent was born in undivided India, on registration with an Indian diplomatic mission.
Article 9A person who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of a foreign state is not, and cannot be, a citizen of India — the constitutional root of the single-citizenship rule.
Article 10Continuance of citizenship rights, subject to any law Parliament makes.
Article 11Parliament may make any provision on the acquisition and termination of citizenship, and all other matters relating to it — the basis for the Citizenship Act, 1955.

How the Citizenship Act, 1955 has been amended

AmendmentWhat changed
1986Restricted birth-based (jus soli) citizenship — being born in India was no longer sufficient by itself; at least one parent had to be an Indian citizen at the time of birth.
2003Tightened the birth-based rule further (barred if either parent was an illegal migrant at the time of birth); introduced the concept of "illegal migrant" as a bar to citizenship by registration or naturalisation; created the legal basis for the Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) scheme.
2005OCI scheme formally launched under the 2003 amendment's framework.
2015Person of Indian Origin (PIO) card scheme merged into the OCI scheme, ending the two-scheme confusion.
2019 (CAA)Exempts Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, and Christian migrants from Pakistan, Afghanistan, and Bangladesh who entered India on or before 31 December 2014 from being treated as "illegal migrants," making them eligible for Indian citizenship by registration or naturalisation on a fast-tracked basis. Rules under the Act were notified in 2024. The Act does not extend this route to Muslim migrants from those countries, which is the central ground of the legal and political challenge to it before the Supreme Court.

🔴 Prelims — verbatim-memorisable facts

●India recognises single citizenship — no separate state citizenship or state domicile.
●Both a citizen by birth and a naturalised citizen are eligible to be President — unlike the USA, which requires natural-born citizenship.
●Citizenship can be taken away — via fraud, disloyalty, unlawful wartime trade with an enemy, or 7 years' continuous residence abroad.
●OCI is not dual citizenship — no voting rights, no public office, no agricultural land purchase.
●1986 amendment first restricted pure birth-based citizenship.
●CAA 2019 cutoff date: entry into India on or before 31 December 2014; covers six communities from three countries (Pakistan, Afghanistan, Bangladesh).
⚠️

Trap: A 2010 Uttarakhand High Court ruling clarified there is no separate "domicile" for each state either — India has one domicile, just as it has one citizenship. Prelims has directly tested whether both halves of that statement are true; do not assume "citizenship" and "domicile" need separate verdicts.

✚

Recent-judgment layer — In re: Section 6A, Citizenship Act (October 2024). A five-judge Constitution Bench upheld (4:1) Section 6A — the special Assam Accord provision giving citizenship to migrants of Indian origin who entered Assam before 25 March 1971 (with those entering between 1 January 1966 and that date getting citizenship after a 10-year wait without voting rights). The majority held the cut-off rational (25 March 1971 = the start of Operation Searchlight in then-East Pakistan) and rejected the argument that demographic change in Assam violated Article 29's cultural-rights protection. Pair it with the CAA fact block above and you hold both live citizenship controversies: CAA 2019 (rules notified March 2024, constitutional challenge still pending) and Section 6A (decided, upheld). A statement claiming "the Supreme Court has struck down Section 6A" or "has decided the CAA challenge" is wrong on both counts — one upheld, one pending.

📖 Pros / cons / way forward — the CAA debate, both sides armed

For: a humanitarian fast-track for persecuted religious minorities from three specific Islamic-majority neighbours; does not touch any existing Indian citizen's rights; citizenship-by-persecution precedents exist worldwide. Against: first citizenship law to use religion as an express criterion, testing Article 14's reasonable-classification standard (why exclude Ahmadiyyas of Pakistan, Rohingyas of Myanmar, Tamils of Sri Lanka if persecution is the rationale?); feared interaction with a nationwide NRC; secularism-as-basic-structure concerns. Way forward (liftable): a persecution-based rather than religion-listed criterion would achieve the humanitarian aim while curing the classification objection — and the pending Supreme Court verdict will settle whether the current design survives Article 14 scrutiny.

(C) Where the real marks go

In Prelims, marks concentrate on precisely distinguishing what is and is not true about citizenship — single citizenship and domicile are true, unqualified statements about irrevocability or birth-only presidential eligibility are traps. Mains marks, where CAA-adjacent questions appear in coaching practice sets, tend to reward balancing the Act's stated humanitarian rationale against its Article 14/secularism challenge — but candidates should verify any specific "PYQ" claimed for this before citing it as genuine, since several circulating online do not trace to an actual UPSC paper.

M9Practice · Week 1 test15 marks · 250 words

What is the controversy behind the Citizenship Amendment Act? Can the policy gain a wider ground of reconciliation with the NPR/NRC project?

Directive: two-part — describe the controversy, then a forward-looking judgement. Part 2 ("can it gain wider reconciliation") invites a reform-oriented answer. This is a politically sensitive topic: the answer must be constitutionally framed and even-handed, arguing through Article 14 rather than taking a political side.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: what CAA does, precisely
  2. The case for it
  3. The constitutional objections
  4. The NPR/NRC interaction
  5. Way forward toward reconciliation

Model answer

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 exempts Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian migrants from Pakistan, Afghanistan and Bangladesh who entered on or before 31 December 2014 from being treated as "illegal migrants", fast-tracking naturalisation through the new Section 6B of the Citizenship Act, 1955. Rules were notified in March 2024; challenges remain pending before the Supreme Court.

The case for it

A humanitarian route for religious minorities in three neighbouring states with a constitutionally established majority faith; it alters no existing citizen's status.

The constitutional objections

First, it is the first Indian citizenship law to use religion as an express criterion, raising an Article 14 question: reasonable classification requires an intelligible differentia with a rational nexus to the object. If the object is protecting the persecuted, why exclude Ahmadiyyas and Shias of Pakistan, Rohingyas of Myanmar, or Sri Lankan Tamils? Second, secularism is part of the basic structure (S.R. Bommai, 1994). Third, the North-East objects on demographic and Assam Accord grounds — the Accord's cut-off being 25 March 1971, upheld in In re Section 6A (2024).

The NPR/NRC interaction

Fear arises from the combination: a nationwide NRC would place the burden of proving citizenship on individuals, while CAA offers a religiously selective safety net for those excluded. That asymmetry, more than either measure alone, drove the protests.

Toward reconciliation

Recast the criterion from a religion list to demonstrated persecution, applied neutrally; decouple CAA from any nationwide NRC by explicit statement; guarantee Sixth Schedule and Inner Line Permit safeguards; and await the Court's ruling on Article 14. The objective — protecting the persecuted — is legitimate; the classification is what must be repaired.

Value additions used: CAA cut-off + Section 6B + March 2024 Rules · Art 14 classification test · Bommai (1994) on secularism · Assam Accord 25 March 1971 · In re Section 6A (2024) · Sixth Schedule / ILP · persecution-based redraft.

◆ One-page recall sheet

Last-mile revision — Citizenship

  • Articles 5–11 = transitional rules for citizenship at commencement (26 Jan 1950) only.
  • Article 9 — voluntary foreign citizenship acquisition ends Indian citizenship (single citizenship root).
  • Article 11 — Parliament's open-ended power → basis of the Citizenship Act, 1955.
  • Acquisition: birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory.
  • Termination: renunciation, termination, deprivation.
  • Deprivation grounds: fraud, disloyalty, unlawful wartime trade with an enemy, 7 years continuous residence abroad.
  • 1986 amendment — first restricted pure birth-based (jus soli) citizenship.
  • 2003 amendment — introduced "illegal migrant" bar; legal basis for OCI.
  • 2015 — PIO card scheme merged into OCI.
  • CAA, 2019 — Hindu/Sikh/Buddhist/Jain/Parsi/Christian migrants from Pakistan/Afghanistan/Bangladesh, entry by 31 Dec 2014, exempted from "illegal migrant" bar; rules notified 2024.
  • OCI ≠ dual citizenship — no vote, no public office, no farmland purchase.
  • Both citizen-by-birth and naturalised citizens can be President — unlike the USA.
  • India has one domicile nationwide (Uttarakhand HC, 2010) — same logic as single citizenship.
