Union and its Territory

Senior-mentor notes on Articles 1–4 — how Parliament can create, merge, rename, or resize a State without a constitutional amendment, and how India's internal map has actually changed since 1950.

Article 1 Article 3 States Reorganisation Telangana 2014

◆ How this note fits the wider set

This note covers the first genuinely substantive provisions inside "significant provisions" — the third element of Topic 1's six-part syllabus line, after historical underpinnings/evolution (Note 1) and features (Note 2). Union & its Territory is kept separate from Citizenship (Note 4), even though both sit under "significant provisions," because each is independently testable and each has its own distinct PYQ history.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Here is the question this whole note answers: once India existed as one country in 1950, could its internal map ever change — new states created, old ones merged, borders redrawn — and if so, who gets to decide that? Most countries treat their internal borders as close to permanent; India deliberately did not. The framers expected the map to keep changing (partly because British India's borders were drawn for administrative convenience, not to match where people who spoke the same language actually lived), so they built a simple, fast mechanism for redrawing it into the Constitution itself, right at Article 3.

That single design choice — "the map is not fixed, and Parliament can redraw it by a simple law, not a difficult amendment" — is the one idea worth holding onto before any article numbers. It explains why India went from roughly 27 states-and-territories at independence to a very different map today, and why a State government, however powerful locally, cannot stop Parliament from altering its own boundaries (it can only be consulted, not overruled — a distinction Prelims tests constantly, and one the rest of this note details precisely).

Syllabus placement & the logical chain

Official GS-II syllabus wording (verbatim): "Indian Constitution— historical underpinnings, evolution, features, amendments, significant provisions and basic structure."

Article 1 declares India a "Union of States" — a phrase already flagged in Note 2 as the textual root of India's quasi-federal character. This note unpacks what that actually means in practice: Parliament can redraw the internal map by simple majority, a power no genuine federation grants its central legislature over its constituent units without their consent.

02

Union and its Territory — Articles 1 to 4

This is a short, sharply-defined sub-part — four articles, one core mechanism (Article 3), and a long list of dates to anchor it against. Prelims tests the mechanism itself (who recommends, who is consulted, what majority is needed) and the historical sequence of state formations. Mains rarely asks about the mechanism directly, but frequently uses a specific reorganisation (Telangana, J&K) as the springboard for a federalism or governance-efficiency question.

Article 1 states: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." The deliberate choice of "Union" over "Federation" signals that constituent units cannot secede and did not enter into any compact — the Union exists first, and states are carved out of it, not vice versa. Articles 2–4 give Parliament the actual toolkit to reshape that map.

Articles 1–4 at a glance

ArticleWhat it does
Article 1Names India "a Union of States," lists the territory of India (states, union territories, and any other acquired territory).
Article 2Parliament may admit into the Union, or establish, new states not originally part of India's territory, on terms it thinks fit (used for Sikkim's accession, 1975).
Article 3Parliament may by law form a new state, increase/diminish any state's area, alter boundaries, or alter the name of any state — subject to the President's-recommendation-plus-consultation procedure in Fig 1.
Article 4Laws made under Articles 2 or 3 that amend the First Schedule (states/UTs) or Fourth Schedule (Rajya Sabha seat allocation) are NOT treated as amendments under Article 368 — they need only the ordinary Article 3 procedure.

How the map actually changed — the reorganisation timeline

YearWhat happened
1950 (original)Four-fold classification: Part A states (former Governors' provinces), Part B states (former princely-state unions with a Rajpramukh), Part C states (former Chief Commissioners' provinces + some princely states), Part D (the Andaman & Nicobar Islands).
1953Andhra State carved out of Madras — the first state formed on a purely linguistic basis, following the Potti Sriramulu agitation.
1956States Reorganisation Act (on the Fazl Ali Commission's recommendation) — abolished the Part A/B/C/D scheme entirely; replaced it with the States and Union Territories structure that still applies; reorganised the map on linguistic lines. This is also the year of the 7th Constitutional Amendment, which gave this new scheme its constitutional basis.
1960Bombay State split into Maharashtra and Gujarat.
1963 / 1966 / 1972Nagaland (1963); Punjab reorganised into Punjab, Haryana, and the UT of Himachal Pradesh (1966); Manipur, Tripura, and Meghalaya made full states (1972).
1975Sikkim made a full state via the 36th Amendment (after a 1974 associate-state arrangement under the 35th Amendment) — Article 2 in action, not Article 3.
1987Goa, Mizoram, and Arunachal Pradesh made full states.
2000Chhattisgarh (from Madhya Pradesh), Uttarakhand (from Uttar Pradesh), and Jharkhand (from Bihar) — three new states in one year.
2014Telangana carved out of Andhra Pradesh via the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act — the 29th state, and the most recent creation of an entirely new state.
2019Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act — J&K state reconstituted into two Union Territories (Jammu & Kashmir with a legislature, and Ladakh without one), alongside the abrogation of Article 370's operative special-status provisions. High-yield, actively-tested current affairs territory in its own right.
✚

Recent-judgment layer — In re: Article 370 (December 2023), the constitutional verdict on 2019. A five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously upheld the abrogation. The holdings worth carrying as exam anchors: (1) Article 370 was a temporary provision, born of wartime conditions in the State — its very placement in Part XXI ("Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions") signalled impermanence; (2) J&K retained no element of internal sovereignty distinct from other States after accession — its constituent-assembly history did not create a separate sovereignty; (3) the President could validly apply the whole Constitution to J&K, and the Constituent Assembly's 1957 dissolution did not freeze Article 370 in place forever; (4) the Court upheld the carve-out of Ladakh as a UT under Article 3, while pointedly not ruling on whether a full State could be permanently downgraded to a UT — instead recording the Union's assurance and directing that J&K's statehood be restored "at the earliest" and elections held. That deliberately-left-open question — can Article 3 convert a State into a UT? — is now the sharpest unresolved edge of this whole topic, and exactly the kind of thing a current-affairs-aware setter frames a statement question around.

🔴 Prelims — verbatim-memorisable facts

●Article 3 bills need only simple majority — not Article 368's special majority.
●State Legislature's views on an Article 3 bill are consultative, not binding.
●1956 States Reorganisation Act abolished Part A/B/C/D states; based on the Fazl Ali Commission.
●Andhra State (1953) — first state formed purely on linguistic lines.
●Sikkim became a full state in 1975 via Article 2, not Article 3.
●Telangana (2014) — 29th state, most recent full state creation.
●Article 4 changes to Schedule 1/4 are not Article 368 amendments.
⚠️

Trap: Do not assume Article 3 requires the affected state's consent — it requires only that the state be given a chance to state its views, within a time limit the President sets, and Parliament is free to override those views entirely. This single distinction (consultation vs. consent) is the most frequently tested point in this note.

03

Mains craft — model intro & value-additions

Model intro line (liftable): "That Parliament can redraw a state's boundaries by ordinary legislation, subject only to consulting — never obtaining the consent of — the state concerned, is the clearest textual proof that India chose a Union of States over a federation of states, and every reorganisation since 1953 has been conducted on exactly those terms."

✚

Value-additions to drop into any answer: (1) Name-drop precisely — the Fazl Ali Commission (States Reorganisation Commission, 1953) as the body whose report underlies the 1956 Act, not a vague "linguistic reorganisation happened"; (2) Analogy — Article 3 as a "in-house renovation clause," letting the Union remodel its own rooms without asking the tenant's permission, only their opinion; (3) Current link — the Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 as the most recent and most consequential use of this power, worth citing whenever a question probes the limits of Centre-State federalism.

PYQ analysis (2013 onward)

Trend verdict: The Article 1–4 mechanism itself is tested periodically in Prelims as a statement-based fact-check (majority required, binding-ness of consultation). Mains rarely interrogates the mechanism directly — it uses a specific reorganisation event as a launchpad for a broader question on governance efficiency or federal structure, which is exactly what the verified 2013 question below does.

M82013 · GS-II10 marks · 150 words

Many State Governments further bifurcate geographical administrative areas like Districts and Talukas for better governance. In light of the above, can it also be justified that more number of smaller States would bring in effective governance at State level? Discuss.

Directive: "Can it be justified… Discuss." A both-sides question with an analogy embedded in it. Crucially: the analogy between districts and States is imperfect, and saying so is a high-value insight — administrative sub-division is not the same as creating a political unit.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 3 makes it constitutionally easy
  2. Case for smaller States
  3. Case against
  4. Why the analogy is imperfect
  5. Verdict: criteria, not size

Model answer

Article 3 lets Parliament create new States by simple majority, the State legislature's views being merely consultative — so this is a question of policy, not constitutional difficulty.

The case for

Shorter administrative distances and better last-mile delivery; homogeneous units easing planning; closer accountability; redress of regional neglect. Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand (2000) saw improved growth after separation.

The case against

Fixed costs of new capitals, secretariats and High Courts; non-viable units dependent on Union transfers; disputes over water, capital and cadre, as Andhra Pradesh–Telangana (2014) showed; and encouragement of sub-regional agitations. The States Reorganisation Commission (Fazl Ali, 1955) warned against language or size as a sole criterion, insisting on administrative viability and national unity.

Why the analogy is imperfect

Bifurcating a district creates no new political tier; creating a State creates a legislature, executive, judiciary and a fiscal claim on the Union — so efficiency arguments do not transfer automatically. Smaller States are therefore justifiable case by case, best decided by a permanent, criteria-based Second States Reorganisation Commission.

Value additions used: Article 3 mechanics (simple majority, consultative State view) · the 2000 trio · Telangana 2014 disputes · Fazl Ali SRC 1955 criteria · administrative-vs-political distinction · second SRC as the way forward.

Last-mile revision — Union and its Territory

  • Article 1 — India, "a Union of States," not a federation of states.
  • Article 2 — Parliament may admit/establish new states (used for Sikkim, 1975).
  • Article 3 — Parliament may form/resize/rename states by simple majority.
  • Article 4 — Schedule 1/4 changes under Art. 2/3 are not Art. 368 amendments.
  • Bill needs President's recommendation; affected state gives non-binding views only.
  • 1953 — Andhra State, first purely linguistic state.
  • 1956 — States Reorganisation Act; Part A/B/C/D scheme abolished; Fazl Ali Commission.
  • 1960 — Bombay → Maharashtra + Gujarat.
  • 1966 — Punjab → Punjab + Haryana + Himachal Pradesh (UT).
  • 1975 — Sikkim, full statehood (36th Amendment, via Article 2).
  • 2000 — Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand — three new states.
  • 2014 — Telangana, the 29th state, from Andhra Pradesh.
  • 2019 — J&K Reorganisation Act — J&K and Ladakh made Union Territories.
  • Tripura — Part 'C' State → centrally administered territory (1956 SRA) → full State later; Nagaland — created by a Constitutional Amendment Act (13th CAA, 1962/63), not the 1956 SRA.
