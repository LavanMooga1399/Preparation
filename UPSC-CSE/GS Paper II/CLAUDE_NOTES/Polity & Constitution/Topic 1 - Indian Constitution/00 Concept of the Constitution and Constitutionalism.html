Concept of the Constitution & Constitutionalism

Senior-mentor notes on the theoretical layer beneath Topic 1 — what a "constitution" actually is, how it differs from "constitutionalism," how constitutions are classified, and the doctrine of constitutional morality that increasingly anchors Indian judgments.

◆ Why this note exists

Newer editions of Laxmikanth's Indian Polity add an early chapter, commonly titled "Concept of the Constitution," covering constitutionalism, the descriptive terms used for a constitution, and the classical classification schemes — material that used to be assumed knowledge rather than a dedicated chapter. It is added here as Note 00, not because it is one of Topic 1's six syllabus elements (historical underpinnings, evolution, features, amendments, significant provisions, basic structure), but because it is the conceptual scaffolding all six sit on — particularly Basic Structure (Note 06), which is really constitutionalism enforced by the judiciary.

Recent Mains questions on constitutional morality (2019 and 2021) confirm this theoretical layer is now directly examined, not just background reading — see the verified PYQs in Section 07.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Before any of the technical vocabulary: a constitution is simply the rulebook for how a country runs itself. It says who gets to make laws, who enforces them, who settles disputes, and — most importantly — what the government is not allowed to do to its own citizens, no matter how much power it holds. Every country needs one, written or unwritten, for the same reason a card game needs agreed rules before anyone can play: without them, whoever is strongest simply does whatever they want.

"Constitutionalism" looks like a bigger version of the same word, but it means something almost the opposite in spirit. It is the idea that even the government itself must obey the rulebook — that merely having a constitution is not enough if the people in power can bend or ignore it whenever it is inconvenient. A country can have a beautifully detailed constitution and still fail at constitutionalism (a dictatorship can write a fine rulebook and then simply not follow it); a country succeeds at constitutionalism only when power is actually, reliably limited by that rulebook in practice, not merely on paper.

Once that one distinction is clear — having a rulebook versus actually being bound by it — everything else in this note is just detail: what kind of rulebook India chose (a very long, very specific one, discussed below), and how Indian courts have recently started treating certain unwritten values, like fairness and non-arbitrariness, as rules the government must follow even where the text is silent. That last idea is called constitutional morality — the newest and most Mains-tested concept in this note, and the one Sections 04-07 build toward.

02

What is a "Constitution"?

Prelims rarely asks "define a constitution" directly, but it frequently tests the descriptive vocabulary scholars use for one — and Mains uses this vocabulary as the opening register for any high-scoring Polity answer. A constitution, in K.C. Wheare's classical formulation, is the document that establishes and regulates the principal organs of government and declares the principles governing their operation — but "the constitution" of a country is broader than just that document; it includes conventions, judicial interpretations, and practices that have grown around it.

03

Constitution vs. Constitutionalism

This is the single most important distinction in the whole primer, and the one Mains rewards most directly. A constitution is a document or set of rules. Constitutionalism is an idea — the belief that governmental power ought to be limited by law, made accountable, and exercised for the public good rather than arbitrarily. A country can have an elaborate constitution on paper and still lack constitutionalism in practice, if that document places no genuine restraint on those in power.

✚

A.V. Dicey's Rule of Law (closely linked to constitutionalism): (1) supremacy of law over arbitrary power; (2) equality before the law, including for officials; (3) the constitution as a consequence of the ordinary law of the land (rights flow from judicial decisions, not a separate grant). Cite this trio whenever an answer needs to argue why a government action lacks constitutionalism, not just assert that it does.

Constitutional Government — constitutionalism institutionalised

If constitutionalism is the idea that power must be limited, constitutional government is that idea made operational — a system in which government is actually conducted according to, and restrained by, a constitution, as opposed to "government" in the bare descriptive sense of whoever happens to rule. UPSC has tested this exact conceptual line three times at Prelims (2014, 2020, 2021 — Section 07), which makes it as high-yield as constitutionalism itself, even though most standard readings fold it into a single paragraph. Two names worth attaching to it: Carl J. Friedrich, who defined constitutionalism/constitutional government as the division and restraint of political power through law; and Herman Finer, who tied it to the practical machinery — an independent judiciary, a free press, and periodic elections — that keeps restraint real rather than nominal.

✚

Four working features of a constitutional government (useful as an enumeration in any Mains answer): (1) limited government — authority is confined to what the constitution grants, not open-ended; (2) rule of law — officials and citizens alike are governed by pre-announced legal norms, not arbitrary discretion; (3) separation of powers with checks — no single organ concentrates unchecked authority; (4) accountability and judicial review — government action can be tested against the constitution and struck down if it fails. A state can be a "government" without being a "constitutional government" if it fails even one of these; a constitutional government is not defined by having elections alone, but by power being genuinely restrained in the interest of individual liberty.

🔴 Prelims — verbatim-memorisable facts

●Constitution = document/rules; Constitutionalism = the idea of limited, accountable government.
●Giovanni Sartori's term for a constitution without real constitutionalism: "façade" (sham) constitution.
●A.V. Dicey's Rule of Law has three elements: supremacy of law, equality before law, judge-made constitutional rights.
●Indian courts have described constitutionalism as premised on the Rule of Law, substituting objective constitutional standards for subjective official discretion (a formulation echoed in Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India, 2006).
●Constitutional government = government whose authority is effectively restricted by the constitution in the interest of individual liberty — not merely a government that happens to have a constitution (tested at Prelims in 2014, 2020, 2021).
●Carl J. Friedrich linked constitutionalism to the division and restraint of power through law; Herman Finer tied it to working machinery — independent judiciary, free press, periodic elections.
04

Classifying Constitutions

James Bryce's classical rigid/flexible classification (1901) is the one every textbook still opens with, even though comparative scholars now consider it dated — many "flexible" constitutions (Britain's) show remarkable long-run stability, and India's own "written" Constitution (Note 02) blends rigid and flexible amendment routes in a single document (Note 05), which classical categories struggle to capture cleanly.

ClassificationCategoriesWhere India sits
Written vs. UnwrittenWritten = codified in a single document (USA, India); Unwritten = uncodified, built from statutes/conventions/case law (UK, New Zealand, Israel).Written — the lengthiest written constitution in the world (Note 02).
Rigid vs. Flexible (Bryce)Rigid = special procedure required to amend, distinct from ordinary lawmaking; Flexible = amendable by the ordinary legislative process.Blend of both (Note 05) — a feature classical Bryce categories were not designed to describe.
Federal vs. UnitaryFederal = powers divided between two levels of government by the constitution itself; Unitary = all power constitutionally vested in the Centre.Federal in form, unitary in spirit — "quasi-federal" (Note 02).
Parliamentary vs. PresidentialParliamentary = executive drawn from and responsible to the legislature; Presidential = executive independently elected, fixed term, separate from the legislature.Parliamentary, at both Union and State levels (Note 02).
⚠️

Trap: Do not assume "written" automatically means "rigid" — they are independent classifications along different axes. India is written AND a blend of rigid/flexible; the UK is unwritten AND flexible; a country could in principle be written yet fully flexible if its constitution can be amended by ordinary legislative majority.

05

Constitutional Morality

This is the highest-yield concept in this entire primer for Mains — tested directly in 2019 and 2021, and the subject of a live 2026 legal controversy (see the value-addition in Section 06). The phrase originates with the British historian George Grote's History of Greece (1846), describing the diffused public temperament needed to sustain a constitutional (as opposed to autocratic) form of government. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar borrowed it directly in his 4 November 1948 Constituent Assembly speech, warning that a constitution — however well drafted — is only as good as the constitutional morality of the people and administrators working it; without that morality, he warned, India's constitutional forms could turn out to be "only a top-dressing on an Indian soil which is essentially undemocratic."

🔴 Prelims — verbatim-memorisable facts

●"Constitutional morality" originates with George Grote, History of Greece, 1846.
●Ambedkar adopted the phrase in his 4 November 1948 Constituent Assembly speech.
●Navtej Singh Johar (2018): "constitutional morality prevails over social morality" — Section 377 struck down.
●Naz Foundation (2009, Delhi HC) was the doctrine's first major modern revival, later echoed by the Supreme Court.
●Sabarimala (2018) — Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala — used constitutional morality against a religious-practice exclusion.
●Joseph Shine (2018): adultery (IPC Section 497) decriminalised — the fourth member of the same constitutional-morality revival set.
📖 The competing view — the doctrine's critics, for a balanced Mains answer

An answer that only celebrates constitutional morality reads half-finished. The standing criticism, pressed by senior members of the bar and governments alike: the doctrine is vague and judicially self-defined — since no text fixes its content, each bench can pour its own values into the phrase, making it (in the critics' words) a potentially "dangerous weapon" that substitutes judges' morality for the legislature's. The defenders' reply: every open-textured constitutional concept (due process, basic structure, reasonableness) works this way, and the doctrine's actual record — Navtej, Joseph Shine, Sabarimala — shows it deployed to protect constitutional rights against majoritarian pressure, which is precisely the counter-majoritarian job courts exist to do. State both sides, then land on Ambedkar: constitutional morality "is not a natural sentiment; it has to be cultivated" — the doctrine is less a judicial weapon than a discipline the whole polity, courts included, is still learning.

06

Mains craft — model intro & value-additions

Model intro line (liftable): "Ambedkar's warning that a constitution is only as good as the constitutional morality of those who work it has proved prophetic — from Naz Foundation in 2009 to Navtej Singh Johar and Sabarimala in 2018, the Indian judiciary has repeatedly reached for exactly the borrowed vocabulary he gave it in 1948 to hold that constitutional values must prevail over majoritarian social morality."

✚

Value-additions to drop into any answer: (1) Name-drop precisely — George Grote as the actual originator of the phrase, which most answers wrongly credit to Ambedkar alone; (2) Analogy — constitutionalism as the "software" running on the "hardware" of a written constitution, with constitutional morality as the operating discipline that keeps the software from crashing into arbitrariness; (3) Current link — the Union government's 2026 argument in the Sabarimala Review that constitutional morality has become a "vague and subjective" tool for judicial overreach is a live, citable controversy for any answer weighing the doctrine's strengths against its criticisms.

PYQ analysis

"Constitutional government" is the recurring Prelims counterpart — verified genuine in 2014, 2020, and 2021, always tested as a statement-based/definitional MCQ built around the "limited government / restraint of power" formulation in Section 03. M12019 · GS-II10 marks · 150 words

What is meant by the term 'constitutional morality'? How does one uphold constitutional morality?

Directive: "What is meant… / How does one…" — a two-part explain question, not a debate. Part 1 demands a precise definition with its origin; Part 2 demands concrete mechanisms. Splitting the answer physically into these two halves is itself a scoring move.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Grote's origin + Ambedkar's adoption
  2. What it means — four elements
  3. How it is upheld — institutions, judiciary, citizens
  4. Conclusion: Ambedkar's "has to be cultivated"

Model answer

Constitutional morality means adherence to the spirit and core values of the Constitution, not mere obedience to its text. Coined by George Grote (1846) and adopted by Ambedkar on 4 November 1948, it lay dormant for decades, was revived in Naz Foundation (2009), and matured in Navtej Singh Johar, Sabarimala and Joseph Shine (all 2018).

Its content

Constitutional supremacy over transient majorities; due process and institutional form; individual dignity against majoritarian pressure; and the primacy of constitutional over social morality — the Navtej formulation used to read down Section 377.

How it is upheld

By courts, striking down majoritarian practice and testing political conduct against it (Manoj Narula, 2014); by institutions observing constitutional forms — free elections, legislative deliberation, an independent CAG and Election Commission; by the executive exercising discretion within constitutional limits rather than political convenience; and by citizens, through Article 51A duties and constitutional literacy.

Ambedkar's caution is the centre of the answer: constitutional morality "is not a natural sentiment; it has to be cultivated."

Value additions used: Grote (1846) · Ambedkar 4 Nov 1948 · the dormancy→revival arc · Naz Foundation (2009) · Navtej Singh Johar / Sabarimala / Joseph Shine (2018) · Manoj Narula (2014) · Article 51A · the "cultivated" quotation.
Budget check: 165 / 150 words. Cut: the separate closing paragraph ("a Constitution can only be as good as those who work it") and the one-line description attached to each 2018 judgment. Bought: the dormancy→revival arc in the opening line, and Manoj Narula (2014) — which extends the doctrine from rights cases to political conduct.
What would cost you marks: attributing the phrase to Ambedkar rather than Grote (the single most common error here); answering only "what it means" and neglecting the "how to uphold" half; listing cases without stating what each held.
M22021 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

'Constitutional Morality' is rooted in the Constitution itself and is founded on its essential facets. Explain the doctrine of 'Constitutional Morality' with the help of relevant judicial decisions.

Directive: "Explain… with the help of relevant judicial decisions." Explain = clarify with detail and illustration; it is not a debate. But the tail imposes a hard requirement: the answer must be case-led. An answer without named judgments cannot score, however well written.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: rooted in Preamble + Parts III/IV
  2. Essential facets it draws on
  3. Judicial evolution — four cases with holdings
  4. The critique (adds balance)
  5. Conclusion: cultivated, not assumed

Model answer

Constitutional morality is the principle that governance must conform to the Constitution's underlying values, not merely its letter. It is rooted in the document itself — in the Preamble's promise of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, in the rights of Part III (notably Articles 14, 15 and 21), and in the goals of Part IV.

Essential facets it rests on

Constitutional supremacy; rule of law; due process; individual dignity; institutional trust; and the subordination of popular sentiment to constitutional guarantees.

The doctrinal arc

Coined by Grote (1846) and invoked by Ambedkar (4 November 1948), the doctrine lay unused until Naz Foundation (2009) revived it. Manoj Narula (2014) applied it to the criminalisation of politics; Government of NCT of Delhi (2018) converted it into "constitutional trust" governing Centre–State conduct; Navtej Singh Johar (2018) held constitutional morality prevails over social morality while reading down Section 377; Sabarimala (2018) deployed it against religious exclusion; Joseph Shine (2018) grounded the striking down of Section 497.

The competing view

Critics call the doctrine vague and judge-defined: no text fixes its content, so each bench may pour in its own values — an objection pressed by governments in the Sabarimala review. Defenders reply that all open-textured constitutional concepts work this way, and that its record is one of protecting rights against majoritarian pressure, which is precisely the counter-majoritarian function courts exist to perform.

Its legitimacy will therefore depend on principled, restrained use. As Ambedkar warned, constitutional morality "has to be cultivated" — by institutions as much as by citizens.

Value additions used: Preamble + Parts III/IV anchoring with Arts 14, 15, 21 · Grote (1846) · Ambedkar 4 Nov 1948 · Naz Foundation (2009) · Manoj Narula (2014) · Govt of NCT of Delhi (2018) "constitutional trust" · Navtej / Sabarimala / Joseph Shine (2018) · the vagueness critique · Ambedkar quotation.
Budget check: 251 / 250 words. Cut: the procedural detail of which court decided Naz, and loose connectives in the critique paragraph. Bought: the doctrine's origin (Grote, Ambedkar) so the arc is complete; Govt of NCT of Delhi (2018), which extends constitutional morality into federal conduct; and clause-level Article anchoring (14, 15, 21).
What would cost you marks: naming cases without their holdings; omitting the critique — a 15-mark "explain" answer with zero balance reads thin; forgetting to show the doctrine is rooted in the Constitution, which is the exact claim the question puts to you.
