Amendment of the Constitution

Senior-mentor notes on Article 368 — the three routes by which the Constitution can change, the procedure each one follows, and the major amendment acts that reshaped Indian polity.

Article 368 Special Majority 42nd Amendment 44th Amendment
3distinct routes to amend the constitution
◆ How this note fits the wider set

This note covers "amendments," the fourth of Topic 1's six syllabus elements, following historical underpinnings/evolution (Note 1), features (Note 2), and significant provisions so far (Notes 3–4). It is deliberately kept separate from Basic Structure Doctrine (Note 6) — this file is about the mechanism of change; Note 6 is about the judicially-created limit on that mechanism. Reading them back to back is recommended, but each stands on its own for revision.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Every constitution faces the same design problem: make it too easy to change, and a temporary majority in power can rewrite the rules to suit itself; make it too hard to change, and the country gets stuck with a document that can no longer fix its own mistakes or adapt to a changing world. Article 368 is India's answer to that problem — and the framers' solution was clever: rather than pick one difficulty level for the whole Constitution, they built three different difficulty levels and sorted every provision into one of them, depending on how important or how federally-sensitive that provision is.

Think of it like a building with three kinds of locks. Ordinary, low-stakes provisions get an easy lock — Parliament can change them like any other law (this route is so easy it barely counts as a "constitutional amendment" in the everyday sense, and this note explains why). Genuinely important provisions get a stronger lock — Parliament needs a two-thirds "special majority," not just a simple majority. And provisions that affect the federal balance between the Centre and the States — Rajya Sabha's powers, how High Court judges are appointed, and so on — get the strongest lock of all: the special majority in Parliament, plus half of all State legislatures have to separately agree.

Once you see it as "three locks of different strengths, sorted by how sensitive the provision is," the rest of this note is just: which provision goes behind which lock, and which historical amendments (the 42nd, the 44th, and others) tried to change the locks themselves.

02

Amendment Procedure — Article 368 and its three routes

Prelims tests this sub-part almost entirely as a "which majority, for which provision" exercise — exactly the kind of statement-based question the verified 2025 PYQ below represents. Mains tests it by asking whether the Constitution's amendment process has struck the right balance between rigidity (protecting core values) and flexibility (letting the document evolve) — a debate that inevitably pulls in the major amendment acts below as evidence.

Article 368 is not the only route to constitutional change — some provisions can be altered by Parliament's ordinary law-making power, outside Article 368 entirely (as already seen with Articles 2–4 in Note 3). Article 368 itself then splits into two further routes depending on what is being amended.

Major amendment acts that reshaped the Constitution

AmendmentYearWhat it did
1st Amendment1951Added the Ninth Schedule (laws placed here were originally immune from judicial review on Fundamental Rights grounds); added grounds for "reasonable restrictions" on free speech; protected land-reform laws.
7th Amendment1956Gave constitutional basis to the States Reorganisation Act — covered in Note 3.
24th Amendment1971Made Presidential assent to amendment bills obligatory; inserted Article 13(4) and Article 368(3) explicitly stating a constitutional amendment is not "law" under Article 13 — a direct legislative response to the Golaknath ruling (see Note 6).
25th Amendment1971Curtailed the fundamental right to property; inserted Article 31C shielding laws implementing certain Directive Principles (Art 39(b)(c)) from challenge under Articles 14/19 — and attempted to bar judicial review of such laws entirely (that ouster clause was struck down in Kesavananda Bharati — see Note 6).
39th Amendment1975Placed disputes over the election of the President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, and Speaker beyond the reach of judicial review (struck down in Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain, 1975 — see Note 6).
42nd Amendment1976Often called the "Mini-Constitution" — added "Socialist," "Secular," "Integrity" to the Preamble (Note 2); added Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A); attempted to give Parliament unlimited amending power and bar all judicial review of amendments (struck down in Minerva Mills, 1980 — see Note 6).
44th Amendment1978Reversed several 42nd Amendment excesses; removed the right to property from Part III (Fundamental Rights) and made it a legal right under Article 300-A instead; restored some checks on the Emergency power.
73rd & 74th Amendments1992Gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj institutions and Urban Local Bodies respectively — the "third tier" of government.
99th Amendment2014Created the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) to replace the judicial collegium — struck down in 2015 (see Note 6).
101st Amendment2016Introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime.
103rd Amendment2019Introduced a 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) — its constitutional validity, including whether it could exceed the traditional 50% reservation ceiling, was upheld by the Supreme Court in 2022 (Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India).

🔴 Prelims — verbatim-memorisable facts

●No joint sitting exists for a Constitutional Amendment Bill — unlike ordinary and money bills.
●President's assent to an amendment bill is obligatory since the 24th Amendment, 1971.
●Election of the President/VP, extent of executive power, Union-State legislative lists, and Article 368 itself all need State ratification.
●Conditions of a Governor's office need only Parliament's ordinary special majority — no State ratification.
●42nd Amendment (1976) = the "Mini-Constitution"; added Fundamental Duties and Preamble words.
●44th Amendment (1978) moved the right to property from Part III to Article 300-A.
●Amendment bills can be introduced by a private member too, needing no prior Presidential recommendation (contrast with Article 3 in Note 3).
⚠️

Trap: Do not confuse the Article 3 procedure (Note 3 — needs the President's prior recommendation before introduction, simple majority, no State ratification) with the Article 368 Route 3 procedure (no prior Presidential recommendation needed to introduce, special majority, PLUS State ratification). The two "federal" procedures pull in opposite directions on almost every checkpoint, which is exactly why UPSC likes to test them against each other.

03

Mains craft — model intro & value-additions

Model intro line (liftable): "Article 368's three-tier amendment scheme was designed as a compromise between American-style rigidity and British-style flexibility, but the swing from the 42nd Amendment's attempt to make Parliament's amending power unlimited to the 44th Amendment's course-correction shows that the real safeguard against misuse has been judicial, not textual."

✚

Value-additions to drop into any answer: (1) Name-drop precisely — Article 13(4) and Article 368(3), the two provisions the 24th Amendment inserted to directly overrule Golaknath, rather than a vague "Parliament responded to the courts"; (2) Analogy — the three amendment routes as a building's three levels of renovation permit (cosmetic change, structural change, change to shared load-bearing walls needing every co-owner's sign-off); (3) Current link — the 103rd Amendment's EWS reservation and its 2022 Supreme Court validation is a live example of Article 368 Route 2 in action, worth citing in any answer on the amendment process's continued relevance.

📖 Constituent Assembly ammunition — Ambedkar's defence of Article 368's design

When critics in the Assembly attacked the amendment procedure — some wanting American-style near-immutability, others wanting simple-majority flexibility — Ambedkar's closing defence was that the Assembly had deliberately avoided both extremes and, unlike Canada or Australia, had not put the amending power beyond ordinary reach: future generations must be able to correct the founders' errors without revolution. He famously declined to bind posterity: the Constitution's amendability was itself an act of democratic humility. The counter-history that makes this a complete Mains answer: within 25 years, the 42nd Amendment used exactly that accessible procedure to attempt unlimited amending power — proving both that Ambedkar's flexibility was real and that the missing safeguard had to be invented judicially (Note 06's basic structure).

✚

Concept behind the limit — the "implied limitations" argument (the bridge to Note 06). Before "basic structure" was a doctrine, it was an argument by counsel Nani Palkhivala in Kesavananda Bharati: even where a constitution's text places no express limit on the amending power, a limit is implied by the nature of the power itself. The word "amend" means to improve or change within an existing frame — it cannot mean the power to destroy the frame. His now-famous reductio: if Article 368 truly had no limits, Parliament could lawfully abolish elections, delete Article 21, repeal Articles 1 through 395 one by one, or amend the Constitution out of existence entirely — the creature (Parliament, created by the Constitution) abrogating its own creator. Seven of thirteen judges accepted the conclusion. Carrying this argument, and not just the holding, is what separates a conceptual answer from a memorised one.

04

PYQ analysis (2013 onward)

Trend verdict: The "which majority for which provision" distinction is tested almost every year in some form, most recently and precisely in 2025. Full Mains questions specifically on the amendment procedure (as opposed to its most famous by-products, the basic structure cases) are rarer — most Mains value here comes from citing a specific amendment act as evidence in a broader federalism or rights question.

(A) Genuine direct PYQs

2025Prelims

"For a constitutional amendment with respect to which of the following, ratification by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States is required before presenting the bill to the President of India for assent? 1. List I — Union List, in the Seventh Schedule. 2. Extent of the executive power of a State. 3. Conditions of the Governor's office." Correct answer: 1 and 2 only.

Verdict: direct hit

Precisely the Route 3 vs. Route 2 distinction in Fig 1 and the trap flagged above (Governor's office needs no State ratification).

2024Prelims

"As per Article 368 of the Constitution of India, the Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution by way of: 1. Addition 2. Variation 3. Repeal." Ans: (d) 1, 2 and 3 — all three modes are textually available under Art. 368.

Verdict: direct hit

A bare-text question straight off Article 368's own wording; the kind of statement-based Prelims item this topic reliably produces.

2023Prelims

"In India, which one of the following Constitutional Amendments was widely believed to be enacted to overcome the judicial interpretations of Fundamental Rights?

  • (a) 1st Amendment
  • (b) 42nd Amendment
  • (c) 44th Amendment
  • (d) 86th Amendment." Ans: (a) 1st Amendment
Verdict: direct hit

The 1st Amendment (1951) added the Ninth Schedule and Art. 31A/31B precisely to insulate land-reform laws from Fundamental Rights review; ties directly to the Ninth Schedule/Golaknath thread this note shares with Note 06.

2022Prelims

Statement-based: (1) a Constitution Amendment Bill requires prior Presidential recommendation; (2) Presidential assent on such a Bill is obligatory; (3) it must be passed by both Houses by special majority with no provision for joint sitting. Ans: (b) 2 and 3 only.

Verdict: direct hit

Confirms two traps at once: no prior Presidential recommendation is required to introduce an amendment bill (unlike Money Bills), while assent, once passed, is obligatory (24th Amendment) and no joint sitting exists.

2019Prelims

Statement-based: (1) the 44th Amendment introduced an Article placing the Prime Minister's election beyond judicial review; (2) the Supreme Court struck down the 99th Amendment (NJAC) as violative of judicial independence. Ans: (d) neither statement is correct.

Verdict: direct hit

A genuine trap: it was the 39th Amendment (1975), not the 44th, that shielded the PM/President's election from courts (later struck down in the Indira Gandhi election case), and statement 2 misstates the NJAC ground slightly, making the safe answer "neither."

2017Prelims

"Which principle among the following was added to the Directive Principles of State Policy by the 42nd Amendment to the Constitution?

  • (a) Equal pay for equal work
  • (b) Participation of workers in the management of industries
  • (c) Right to work, education and public assistance
  • (d) Securing living wage and human conditions of work." Ans: (b)
Verdict: direct hit

Confirms the 42nd Amendment's specific DPSP addition, distinct from the other options which are older, original-DPSP provisions.

2013Prelims

Statement-based: (1) an amendment can be initiated by introduction of a bill in the Lok Sabha only; (2) an amendment affecting the federal character requires ratification by the legislatures of all States. Ans: (d) neither — an amendment bill can originate in either House, and federal-character ratification needs only one-half of the States, not all.

Verdict: direct hit

A double trap testing exactly the "either House" origination point and the "one-half, not all" ratification threshold in Fig 1.

2009Prelims

"Which one of the following Constitutional Amendments states that the total number of Ministers, including the Prime Minister, in the Council of Ministers shall not exceed fifteen percent of the total number of [Lok Sabha members]?

  • (a) 90th
  • (b) 91st
  • (c) 92nd
  • (d) 93rd." Ans: (b) 91st Amendment (2003)
Verdict: direct hit

A numbered-amendment recall question; the 91st Amendment is the anti-defection-strengthening amendment that also capped Council of Ministers size.

(C) Where the real marks go

In Prelims, marks concentrate on correctly sorting a named provision into simple majority / special majority / special majority-plus-ratification. In Mains, marks rarely reward describing Article 368's text in isolation — they reward using a specific amendment (42nd, 44th, 99th, 103rd) as evidence for an argument about the balance of power between Parliament, the States, and the judiciary.

M10Practice15 marks · 250 words

"The Indian amending process is neither as rigid as the American nor as flexible as the British — and that middle path has been both its strength and its vulnerability." Discuss with reference to Ambedkar's defence of Article 368 and the 42nd–44th Amendment cycle.

Directive: "Discuss" with two named anchors. The tail names what must appear — CA debate material and the 42nd/44th cycle. Both must be present. The quoted claim has a two-sided shape ("strength and vulnerability") which dictates the body's two blocks.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: the three-tier design
  2. Ambedkar's defence — the strength
  3. The 42nd Amendment — the vulnerability
  4. The correction — 44th + judicial brake
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Article 368 creates a three-tier amending process — simple majority for some provisions, special majority for most, and special majority plus ratification by half the States for federal provisions. It deliberately avoids both American rigidity (three-fourths of States) and British flexibility (an ordinary law).

The strength: Ambedkar's defence

Answering critics in the Assembly, Ambedkar argued the founders must not bind posterity — future generations should be able to correct their errors without revolution. The record vindicates this: over a hundred amendments have absorbed profound change, including the 73rd and 74th creating local self-government, the 101st enabling GST, and the 106th reserving one-third of seats for women.

The vulnerability: the 42nd Amendment

The same accessibility was turned against the Constitution. The 42nd Amendment (1976), enacted during the Emergency, declared Parliament's amending power unlimited, barred judicial review of amendments, subordinated Fundamental Rights to all Directive Principles, and extended the Lok Sabha's term. Flexibility designed for correction became an instrument of concentration.

The correction, from two directions

Textually, the 44th Amendment (1978) restored the term, replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion", and protected Articles 20 and 21 from suspension. Doctrinally, the judiciary supplied the brake the text omitted: Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda Bharati (1973), establishing the basic structure → Minerva Mills (1980), which struck down the 42nd's clauses, holding limited amending power and judicial review to be themselves basic features.

The middle path is therefore vindicated — but only because the judiciary supplied a limit the text omitted. Its safety rests on the basic structure doctrine, not on Article 368's own words.

Value additions used: three-tier design · Ambedkar's posterity argument · 73rd / 74th / 101st / 106th as evidence of useful flexibility · 42nd Amendment specifics · 44th Amendment repairs · the arc Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980) · Arts 20 and 21.

  • Route 2 — special majority (majority of total membership + 2/3 present & voting) — most provisions incl. Fundamental Rights.
  • Route 3 — Route 2 + ratification by ≥ half the States — federal provisions (President's election, Art 73/162, judiciary, Art 368 itself).
  • No joint sitting for amendment bills — unlike ordinary/money bills.
  • Presidential assent obligatory since 24th Amendment, 1971.
  • 1st Amendment (1951) — Ninth Schedule created.
  • 24th Amendment (1971) — countered Golaknath; assent made obligatory.
  • 25th Amendment (1971) — Art 31C; curtailed property right.
  • 39th Amendment (1975) — PM/President election shielded from courts (struck down).
  • 42nd Amendment (1976) — "Mini-Constitution"; Fundamental Duties; Preamble words; unlimited amending power claimed (struck down).
