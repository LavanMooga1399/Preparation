Basic Structure Doctrine

Senior-mentor notes on the judicially-created limit on Parliament's amending power — from Golaknath through Kesavananda Bharati to Minerva Mills and the 2015 NJAC judgment.

Kesavananda Bharati Golaknath Minerva Mills NJAC 2015

◆ How this note fits the wider set

This note completes the fifth and sixth elements of Topic 1's syllabus line together — "amendments" (mechanism, covered in Note 5) and "basic structure" (limit on that mechanism, covered here). The two are inseparable in substance but kept as separate files because the case-law sequence below is dense enough to deserve its own uninterrupted read.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Note 5 established that Parliament can amend almost any part of the Constitution, given the right majority. That raises an obvious, slightly alarming question: if Parliament can amend anything, what actually stops a determined majority from amending away democracy itself — abolishing elections, abolishing the judiciary's power to check the government, turning the Constitution into a rulebook that only protects those currently in power? Nothing in the Constitution's original text answers that question directly. This note is the story of how the Supreme Court answered it anyway, entirely through its own judgments, without a single new word being added to Article 368.

The Court's answer, arrived at only after a long back-and-forth across three big cases, was this: Parliament's amending power is very wide, but it is not unlimited — there is an inner core of features (democracy, secularism, judicial review, the rule of law, and a handful of others) that no amendment, however procedurally correct, is allowed to destroy. That inner core is the "Basic Structure" — a phrase that appears nowhere in the Constitution's text, invented entirely by judges, and yet today treated as the single most important constraint on Parliament's power. Think of it as an unwritten, unamendable constitution-within-the-Constitution.

The case sequence below (Golaknath → Kesavananda Bharati → Minerva Mills → NJAC) is really just the story of the Court slowly building, testing, and then confidently using this idea — read it as one continuous argument, not four separate facts.

02

The Basic Structure Doctrine — origin, elements, and defence

This is dense case law, but it compresses into one clean narrative: courts first said Parliament could not touch Fundamental Rights at all (Golaknath), Parliament pushed back and said it could touch anything (24th Amendment), and the courts then found a middle path — Parliament can amend anything, including Fundamental Rights, but not so as to destroy the Constitution's "basic structure" (Kesavananda Bharati). Everything after 1973 is Parliament testing that boundary and the courts defending it.

The doctrine's key defences, case by case

CaseYearWhat it settled
Minerva Mills v. Union of India1980Struck down two clauses the 42nd Amendment had inserted into Article 368 — one giving Parliament unlimited amending power, another barring all judicial review of amendments. Held: a limited amending power and judicial review are themselves part of the basic structure — Parliament cannot use its amending power to destroy the very limits on that power.
Waman Rao v. Union of India1981Fixed the doctrine's application as prospective from 24 April 1973 (the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment) — laws placed in the Ninth Schedule before that date remain immune from basic structure review; those added after are not.
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India1994Held secularism and federalism to be part of the basic structure (already noted in Note 2's PYQ analysis).
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu2007Confirmed that any law inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can still be tested against the basic structure — closing a route some governments had used to insulate legislation from judicial review.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. Union of India (the "NJAC case")2015Struck down the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act, 2014, holding that primacy of the judiciary in judicial appointments is part of the basic structure, and that the NJAC's composition (including the Law Minister and two "eminent persons") compromised judicial independence. The collegium system was restored.

Illustrative elements of the basic structure (non-exhaustive)

No single judgment gives a complete or closed list — this is a live, still-evolving list built case by case. The most frequently cited elements are: supremacy of the Constitution; sovereign, democratic, and republican character; secular character; separation of powers between the legislature, executive, and judiciary; federal character; unity and integrity of the nation; parliamentary system of government; rule of law; judicial review; independence of the judiciary; free and fair elections; and — per Minerva Mills — the limited nature of Parliament's own amending power.

🔴 Prelims — verbatim-memorisable facts

●Golaknath (1967): Parliament CANNOT amend Fundamental Rights.
●Kesavananda Bharati (1973): 13-judge bench, 7:6 — birth of the basic structure doctrine.
●Basic structure has no exhaustive list — courts decide case by case.
●Minerva Mills (1980): judicial review + limited amending power = basic structure.
●Waman Rao (1981): doctrine applies prospectively from 24 April 1973.
●Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): free & fair elections = basic structure.
●NJAC case (2015): judicial independence in appointments = basic structure; 99th Amendment struck down.
⚠️

Trap: Do not say the Kesavananda Bharati bench "struck down" the 24th Amendment — it upheld the 24th Amendment's core claim (Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights) while simultaneously creating a new limit (the basic structure) that no amendment, including the 24th, can cross. The case is a partial win for Parliament, not a defeat.

📖 The concept behind the doctrine — Palkhivala's implied-limitations argument, the part textbooks skip

Most answers state the doctrine's result and skip the reasoning that produced it — which is where a conceptual question will catch you. Counsel Nani Palkhivala's core submission to the 13-judge bench: the power to "amend" is by its own nature the power to change something while keeping it the same thing — so an unlimited Article 368 is a contradiction in terms. His hypotheticals, worth deploying verbatim in Mains: if no implied limit exists, Parliament could amend away elections, delete Article 21, or repeal Articles 1–395 seriatim until nothing remained — the Constitution's own creature using the Constitution's own procedure to abrogate the Constitution. The doctrine, in other words, is not judicial invention from nowhere; it is the Court accepting that "amendment" cannot lawfully mean "abrogation." The judgment marked its 50th anniversary in April 2023 — a ready current-affairs hook the Supreme Court itself observed by publishing the case's full record online.

✚

Recent-expansion layer — the list keeps growing, and negatives exist too. Additions worth knowing beyond the classic list: primacy of the Chief Justice/collegium in judicial appointments (NJAC case, 2015 — the doctrine's most consequential modern use); parliamentary democracy and the Tenth Schedule's place in it (Kihoto Hollohan, 1992, upheld anti-defection while making Speaker decisions judicially reviewable); and — the frequently-missed negative holding — in the EWS judgment (Janhit Abhiyan, 2022, 3:2) the Court held that reservation on purely economic criteria, and even the exclusion of SC/ST/OBC poor from the EWS quota, does not violate the basic structure — with the two dissenting judges disagreeing on precisely that exclusion point. A statement claiming "the Supreme Court has never rejected a basic-structure challenge to a constitutional amendment" is false — EWS is the standing counter-example.

✚

Value-additions to drop into any answer: (1) Name-drop precisely — the 7:6 margin in Kesavananda Bharati, to show the doctrine's contested, non-obvious origin rather than presenting it as settled from day one; (2) Analogy — the basic structure as a building's load-bearing walls: Parliament can renovate any room (even ones as important as Fundamental Rights) but cannot remove the walls holding the structure up; (3) Current link — the 2015 NJAC judgment as the doctrine's most recent and most consequential application, directly relevant whenever a question probes judicial independence or Centre-judiciary tension.

M112017 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

Critically examine the Supreme Court's judgement on the 'National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014' with reference to the appointment of judges of higher judiciary in India.

Directive: "Critically examine." Deconstruct the judgment and deliver a balanced verdict. "Critically" is compulsory here — an answer that only praises or only attacks the verdict is capped. You must argue both the majority's reasoning and the dissent's.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: name it the Fourth Judges Case + the core tension
  2. The doctrinal lineage in one line
  3. Why the Court struck it down
  4. The case against the judgment
  5. Balanced verdict + way forward

Model answer — revised after examiner-style review; see the note below

The NJAC judgment — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015), the Fourth Judges Case — tested the constitutional balance between judicial independence and democratic accountability in appointments to the higher judiciary under Articles 124 and 217.

The doctrinal lineage

Article 124 requires only "consultation" with the CJI. S.P. Gupta (First Judges Case, 1981) read this as executive primacy; the Second (1993) and Third (1998) Judges Cases converted "consultation" into "concurrence", creating the collegium. The 99th Amendment and NJAC Act, 2014 — passed near-unanimously in Parliament and ratified by over half the State legislatures — replaced it with a six-member commission including the Law Minister and two eminent persons.

Why the Court struck it down (4:1)

The majority held that judicial primacy in appointments is part of the basic structure, because an independent judiciary is indispensable to judicial review and constitutional supremacy — reinforced by Article 50's directive to separate the judiciary from the executive. Fatally, the veto rule let any two members block a recommendation, enabling non-judicial members to override the judges.

The case against the judgment

Justice Chelameswar's dissent held the collegium opaque and unaccountable, and nowhere in the text. Critics add that primacy is not exclusivity; that a plural commission was recommended by the 121st Law Commission Report (1987) and the Venkatachaliah Commission (NCRWC, 2002); and that invalidating a duly ratified amendment strains separation of powers and democratic legitimacy.

Verdict

The judgment was right in principle but incomplete in effect — it protected independence but restored an unreformed collegium, leaving the Memorandum of Procedure unfinalised a decade later. The way forward lies in reform within judicial primacy: a settled MoP, published criteria, a permanent secretariat and recorded reasons — securing accountability without surrendering independence.

Last-mile revision — Basic Structure Doctrine

  • Shankari Prasad (1951) / Sajjan Singh (1965) — Parliament can amend any part, incl. FRs.
  • Golaknath (1967) — reverses this; FRs cannot be amended.
  • 24th Amendment (1971) — Parliament's counter; Art 13(4) + 368(3) added.
  • Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — 13-judge bench, 7:6 — basic structure doctrine born; FRs ARE amendable, subject to basic structure.
  • Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) — free & fair elections = basic structure; 39th Amendment struck down.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976) — tried to give Parliament unlimited power + bar judicial review.
  • Minerva Mills (1980) — struck down those 42nd Amendment clauses; judicial review + limited amending power = basic structure.
  • Waman Rao (1981) — doctrine applies prospectively from 24 April 1973.
  • S.R. Bommai (1994) — secularism + federalism = basic structure.
  • I.R. Coelho (2007) — post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws are reviewable; "blanket immunity" for Ninth Schedule laws is a Prelims trap (2018 PYQ) — false.
  • Ninth Schedule created by the 1st Amendment, 1951, under Nehru (2019 PYQ).
  • NJAC case (2015) — judicial independence in appointments = basic structure; 99th Amendment struck down.
  • No exhaustive list of basic structure elements exists — built case by case.
