Directive Principles of State Policy

Senior-mentor notes on Part IV, Articles 36–51 — the Constitution's socio-economic conscience: non-justiciable by design, yet the single most argued-over relationship in Indian constitutional law once it collides with Fundamental Rights.

Part IV, Art. 36–51 Irish Origin Gandhian / Socialist / Liberal Art. 31C & the FR-DPSP Saga
37Article that makes DPSP non-justiciable
◆ Why this note exists as its own file

DPSP and Fundamental Duties are always taught back-to-back (Laxmikanth Ch. 9–10, and your own Week 2 Self-Study Plan groups them on the same day) — but they are independently and heavily tested, so they earn their own files rather than being crammed into Note 07. DPSP is the more consequential of the two: it is the direct cause of four of India's most important constitutional amendments and three of its most important Supreme Court cases, all already mapped in Note 06's Basic Structure timeline. This note gives you the DPSP-specific content those cases were actually fought over.

Trend verdict up front: DPSP is a reliable Prelims performer — a dozen genuine, verified questions since 2010, mostly simple attribution ("which Part/Article says this") — and a recurring Mains hook for any answer on socio-economic rights, welfare-state debates, or the FR-DPSP relationship.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Fundamental Rights (Note 07) are promises the government must keep — enforceable in court, backed by a direct remedy. Directive Principles are a different kind of promise entirely: they are a list of goals the government should work towards — reducing inequality, providing a living wage, protecting the environment, building village self-government — but no court can force the government to actually deliver any of them. If a government simply ignores a Directive Principle for years, no citizen can sue over that failure the way they could over a Fundamental Rights violation.

That sounds like it makes DPSP toothless, and on paper it almost is — but that is exactly why this part of the Constitution matters so much in practice: DPSP are effectively India's vision statement for what kind of society it is trying to become (a welfare state, not just a rights-protecting one), even though that vision cannot be enforced directly. Politically, a government that consistently ignores DPSP pays a price at the ballot box, even if it pays no price in court — the accountability is real, just indirect.

Because Fundamental Rights are enforceable and DPSP are not, the two have collided in court again and again whenever a DPSP-inspired law (like land reform) has been challenged as violating a Fundamental Right (like the old right to property). That fight — which side wins when the two collide — is the single most important storyline in this note, and it is also most of the reason Note 06's Basic Structure Doctrine exists at all.

01

Where this fits & the logical chain

DPSP is the second of the three "significant provisions" that make up the bulk of Topic 1's PYQ weight, alongside Fundamental Rights (Note 07) and Fundamental Duties (Note 09). It is placed here, after Fundamental Rights, because you cannot understand DPSP's real exam significance — the Article 31C saga, Minerva Mills, the DPSP-vs-FR balance — without Note 07's Article 14/19/21 content and Note 06's Basic Structure timeline already fresh.

🔗

Logical chain: Fundamental Rights (Note 07 — justiciable, individual, "thou shalt not") → Directive Principles (this file — non-justiciable, collective/socio-economic, "the State shall endeavour to") → Fundamental Duties (Note 09 — non-justiciable, individual obligations, the newest and smallest of the three). Together these three parts are frequently called the Constitution's "conscience" (Granville Austin's phrase) — a Mains examiner favourite triad.

02

What the Directive Principles actually are

Part IV, Articles 36 to 51, borrowed directly from Article 45 of the Irish Constitution of 1937 (itself inspired by the Spanish Constitution) — India's single most consequential constitutional borrowing after the Government of India Act, 1935. The DPSP are instructions to the State — the Union, every State government, and every local body — on the kind of socio-economic order they must work towards, even though no court can force them to.

ArticleWhat it establishes
Article 36Defines "the State" for Part IV exactly as Article 12 does for Part III (Fundamental Rights) — so the same bodies are bound by both.
Article 37The single most important sentence in this note: the DPSP "shall not be enforceable by any court," but are nevertheless "fundamental in the governance of the country" and it is "the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws." Non-justiciable, but not optional.
⚠️

Trap: "Non-justiciable" does not mean "unimportant" or "symbolic." Article 37 explicitly calls them fundamental to governance — the correct framing for a Mains answer is that they are legally unenforceable but constitutionally and politically binding, not that they are aspirational fluff.

03

Classification — Gandhian, Socialist, Liberal-Intellectual

The DPSP were not written in this order or under these labels in the Constitution itself — this three-way classification is a scholarly convenience (not an official constitutional category) but it is exactly how Prelims match-the-pairs questions are built, including the genuine 2012 PYQ verified in Section 09 below.

🔴 Prelims — verbatim-memorisable facts

●Article 40 (Panchayats) and Article 43 (cottage industries) are the two classic Gandhian DPSPs tested together.
●Article 39(b)/(c) — equitable distribution of material resources / no concentration of wealth — is the single most-tested Socialist DPSP (2021 PYQ).
●Article 50 — separation of judiciary from the executive — is a Liberal-Intellectual DPSP, tested twice as a genuine PYQ (2020, 2017).
●Article 44 Uniform Civil Code remains unimplemented at the Union level; Uttarakhand became the first State to enact a UCC (2024).
04

DPSP vs. Fundamental Rights — the conflict, resolved

This is the highest-value Mains angle in this note, and it is really Note 06's Basic Structure timeline read through a DPSP lens. The short version: for three decades Parliament tried to give DPSP priority over Fundamental Rights whenever they clashed; the judiciary kept pushing back until Minerva Mills (1980) settled on harmonious construction — neither part is absolutely supreme over the other.

MilestoneYearWhat changed
Champakam Dorairajan1951Supreme Court held Fundamental Rights prevail over DPSP wherever they conflict — DPSP must give way.
1st Amendment1951Parliament's direct response — added Article 15(4) (special provisions for backward classes) and the Ninth Schedule, to protect DPSP-driven land-reform laws from FR challenge.
25th Amendment — Art. 31C inserted1971Laws implementing Article 39(b)/(c) specifically could not be challenged as violating Articles 14 or 19.
Kesavananda Bharati1973Upheld Art. 31C's protection for Article 39(b)/(c) laws, but struck down the clause that tried to oust judicial review entirely (Note 06).
42nd Amendment — Art. 31C widened1976Extended Art. 31C's immunity to laws implementing any Directive Principle, not just 39(b)/(c) — effectively giving all DPSP priority over Articles 14, 19 and 31.
Minerva Mills1980Struck down the 1976 widening as unconstitutional — restored Art. 31C to its original 39(b)/(c)-only scope. Held that the balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSP is itself part of the basic structure (Note 06); neither can be given absolute primacy over the other.
✚

Mains framing to lift directly: Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are not adversaries but complementary halves of a single constitutional vision — Granville Austin's "conscience of the Constitution" — and Minerva Mills is the case that made this framing binding law, not just political rhetoric.

✚

The four-word character contrast (a Week 2 statement set): DPSP are positive obligations (they tell the State what to DO; Fundamental Rights are mostly negative — what the State must NOT do); they aim at socio-economic democracy, not merely political democracy (the trap swap: "socio-political" is the wrong word); they are societarian and collective, not personal and individualistic (that description belongs to Fundamental Rights); and they are non-justiciable (Article 37). Any four-statement question on DPSP's "nature" is built by flipping one of these four words.

✚

Recent-judgment layer — Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (November 2024): the Article 39(b) saga's newest chapter. A nine-judge bench (8:1 on the main point) finally answered a question referred decades earlier: does "material resources of the community" in Article 39(b) cover all private property? The majority said no — not every privately-owned asset becomes a "material resource of the community" merely because the community has needs; whether a given resource qualifies depends on its nature, scarcity, and impact on public welfare, judged case by case. In doing so the Court expressly moved away from the expansive Justice Krishna Iyer-era reading (endorsed in Sanjeev Coke, 1983) that treated essentially all private wealth as redistributable community resource — the majority calling that view the product of "a particular economic ideology" the Constitution does not mandate. The bench also confirmed Article 31C survives to its Kesavananda-era extent (protecting 39(b)/(c) laws). Exam anchors: 9 judges, Nov 2024, 39(b) narrowed, Krishna Iyer doctrine departed from, 31C alive. This is now the single most likely "recent judgment + DPSP" question in existence.

05

New Directive Principles added by amendment

DPSP is not a frozen 1950 list — Parliament has added to it several times, almost always via the same amendments already in Note 05's Amendment timeline.

AmendmentNew/changed Directive Principle
42nd Amendment, 1976Added Art. 39A (equal justice and free legal aid), 43A (workers' participation in management), 48A (protection of environment and wildlife); rewrote Art. 39(f) (child protection against exploitation).
44th Amendment, 1978Added Art. 38(2) — minimising income inequalities, not just status/opportunity inequalities.
86th Amendment, 2002Rewrote Art. 45 — from "free and compulsory education for children up to 14" (its original 1950 form) to early-childhood-care-and-education for children below six; the 6–14 right itself was upgraded out of DPSP and into a new Fundamental Right, Article 21A (Note 07).
97th Amendment, 2011Added Art. 43B — promotion of cooperative societies.
⚠️

Trap: The 86th Amendment is a favourite because it touches three different Parts at once — it downgraded Article 45's original content out of DPSP, upgraded a version of it into Fundamental Rights as Article 21A, and added Article 51A(k) (a parent/guardian's duty to provide these educational opportunities) to Fundamental Duties (Note 09). One amendment, three Parts — a clean way to show interconnection in a Mains answer.

06

Implementation — which DPSP actually got legislative teeth

A frequent Mains ask is to evaluate how far DPSP have actually been realised. The safest, evidence-based answer names specific legislation against specific Articles rather than asserting vague "progress."

Directive PrincipleLegislative follow-through
Art. 39A — free legal aidLegal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (NALSA and State/District Legal Services Authorities).
Art. 40 — village panchayats73rd Amendment, 1992 — Panchayati Raj Institutions given constitutional status (Part IX).
Municipalities (Art. 40's urban parallel)74th Amendment, 1992 — Part IXA, urban local bodies.
Art. 48A — environmentEnvironment (Protection) Act, 1986; National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.
Art. 43 — living wageMinimum Wages Act (pre-dates the Constitution, 1948) and successor Code on Wages, 2019.
Art. 44 — Uniform Civil CodeNot enacted at Union level; Uttarakhand became the first State to notify a UCC, 2024.
✚

Government schemes as DPSP in action — a distinct, newer MCQ pattern: instead of naming legislation, the question names a scheme and asks which Article it reflects. Four exam-tested pairings worth memorising as a set:

Scheme / initiativeArticle it gives effect to
Janani Suraksha YojanaArticle 39 — reduces maternal/neonatal mortality, part of securing a good standard of living for women.
Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986Article 39(f) — protects childhood from exploitation, secures opportunities for education and development.

Tested directly in Week 2 — scheme-ministry-Article triplets are now a confirmed pattern.

Trap: a coaching-test question offered all four as options and asked which reflect DPSP objectives — the correct answer was all of them. Don't assume only the most obviously "socialist"-sounding scheme (Janani Suraksha Yojana) qualifies; environment and foreign-policy schemes count too, since Articles 48A and 51 are DPSP articles as well.

07

Criticism and significance

A balanced Mains answer states both sides precisely, with the correct name attached to each view — examiners reward accurate attribution over generic praise or criticism.

CriticismCounter / significance
Ivor Jennings called them a "manifesto of aims and aspirations" — not real law.Ambedkar's reply in the Constituent Assembly: the State remains politically accountable to the electorate for failing them, even without judicial sanction — a different, not absent, form of enforcement.
K.C. Wheare dismissed them as "pious aspirations," and K.T. Shah wanted them justiciable.Granville Austin calls Fundamental Rights and DPSP together the "conscience of the Constitution" — each meaningless in isolation from the other.
No logical arrangement — the list reads as an unordered omnibus.Courts have repeatedly used Article 37 to read DPSP into the interpretation of Fundamental Rights themselves (e.g., environmental and right-to-livelihood readings of Article 21), giving them real interpretive force despite non-justiciability.
08

Mains craft — model intro & value-additions

Model intro line (liftable): "Borrowed from the Irish Constitution of 1937 and rendered legally unenforceable by Article 37, the Directive Principles of State Policy were nonetheless intended by the framers to be, in Ambedkar's words, as fundamental to governance as the Fundamental Rights are to the individual — a tension that took three decades and the Minerva Mills judgment of 1980 to finally resolve."

✚

Value-additions to drop into any answer: (1) Name-drop precisely — Granville Austin's "conscience of the Constitution" phrase for the FR-DPSP-FD triad; (2) Analogy — DPSP as the Constitution's "compass" (direction, not compulsion) versus Fundamental Rights as its "guardrails" (compulsion, judicially enforced); (3) Current link — the Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code (2024) as a live, citable example of a decades-dormant Directive Principle (Article 44) suddenly being operationalised at the State level.

"Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are not adversaries but complementary halves of a single constitutional vision." Discuss.

Directive: "Discuss" a reconciliation claim. The honest structure is historical: they were treated as adversaries, and were then reconciled. Showing that arc — conflict → resolution — is stronger than simply asserting complementarity.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Austin's "conscience"
  2. The conflict phase
  3. The reconciliation
  4. How they operate together today
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Granville Austin called Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles together the "conscience of the Constitution" — Part III securing political democracy, Part IV social and economic democracy.

The conflict phase

Champakam Dorairajan (1951) held FRs prevail where they conflict, prompting the First Amendment. The 25th Amendment (1971) inserted Article 31C, immunising laws implementing Article 39(b)/(c) from Articles 14 and 19; the 42nd Amendment (1976) widened this to all DPSP.

The reconciliation

Kesavananda Bharati (1973) upheld Article 31C's core but struck its judicial-review ouster. Minerva Mills (1980) struck down the 42nd's widening, holding the balance between Parts III and IV to be itself part of the basic structure. Property Owners Association (2024, nine judges) narrowed Article 39(b): not all private property is a "material resource of the community".

How they now work together

Courts read DPSP into rights — Article 21 embraces livelihood, health, clean environment and education, and Article 21-A converted the Article 45 directive into an enforceable right.

The claim therefore holds, but as a judicially constructed settlement rather than an original given.

Last-mile revision — Directive Principles of State Policy

  • Part IV, Articles 36–51 — borrowed from Ireland, 1937 (via Spain).
  • Article 37: non-justiciable, but "fundamental in the governance of the country."
  • Three categories: Gandhian (Art. 40, 43, 43B, 46, 47), Socialist (Art. 38, 39, 41, 43A), Liberal-Intellectual (Art. 44, 45, 48, 48A, 50).
  • Champakam Dorairajan (1951) → 1st Amendment → Art. 31C (25th Amdt, 1971) → Kesavananda (1973, upheld for 39(b)/(c) only) → 42nd Amdt (1976) widened Art. 31C to all DPSP → Minerva Mills (1980) struck that down — harmonious balance is basic structure.
  • 86th Amendment (2002) — one amendment touching three Parts: DPSP Art. 45 rewritten, FR Art. 21A added, FD Art. 51A(k) added.
  • 97th Amendment (2011) — Art. 43B, cooperative societies, the newest DPSP.
  • Implemented via: Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 (Art. 39A); 73rd/74th Amendments 1992 (Art. 40); Environment Protection Act 1986 (Art. 48A).
  • Article 44 Uniform Civil Code — still not enacted at Union level; Uttarakhand, 2024, first State to do so.
  • Granville Austin: FR + DPSP = "conscience of the Constitution."
  • Genuine Prelims repeater fact: "Welfare State" ideal → DPSP (asked 2015 and 2020).
