Emergency Provisions

Part XVIII, Articles 352–360 — the machinery that lets a federal, rights-based Constitution turn temporarily unitary and rights-restricted. Two full days of the study plan, and the densest cluster of exact numbers, majorities and time limits in the entire syllabus.

Articles 352–360 38th → 42nd → 44th Amendment S.R. Bommai (1994) Arts 358 vs 359 Never-used Art 360
3types — and almost every question turns on telling them apart
◆ Why this note is built as one, not three

The study plan gives Emergency two days, and the temptation is to learn the three types one after another. Resist it. UPSC almost never asks "what is a National Emergency" — it asks which of the three requires a special majority, which can continue indefinitely, which suspends Fundamental Rights, which has never been used. Every one of those is a comparison, and a comparison cannot be learnt from three separate notes read a day apart.

So this note front-loads the master comparison table in Section 01 and then goes deep on each type. Read Section 01 first, come back to it last, and treat it as the thing you actually revise.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

A Constitution has a hard problem to solve. It must protect rights and share power between the Centre and the States in normal times — but it must also survive a war, a collapse of government in a State, or a financial breakdown, none of which can be handled by a government that has to negotiate for every decision. The framers' answer was to build an emergency switch into the document itself: a set of provisions that temporarily concentrate power at the Centre and, in one case, suspend some rights — and then, crucially, switch off.

Ambedkar defended this openly in the Constituent Assembly. His argument was that the Indian Constitution is unitary in emergency and federal in normal times, and that this flexibility was a strength, not a betrayal — the alternative being either a rigid federation that cannot act in a crisis, or a revolution when it fails. K.T. Shah and H.V. Kamath attacked the provisions bitterly as an invitation to dictatorship.

The Emergency of 1975–77 proved both sides partly right. The switch worked exactly as designed, and it was abused exactly as feared. Almost everything you need to learn about Part XVIII is the story of the Constitution being tightened afterwards so that it could never happen the same way again — which is why the 44th Amendment (1978) appears in nearly every answer in this note.

The five swaps that decide most questions. (1) Majority: special for 352, simple for 356 and 360 — candidates reverse this constantly. (2) Deadline: one month for 352, two months for the other two. (3) Outer limit: only 356 has one (3 years); 352 renews forever and 360 needs no renewal at all. (4) Rights: only 352 touches Fundamental Rights. (5) Lok Sabha's veto: the power to force revocation by resolution exists only for 352.

What the 44th Amendment actually changed — the highest-yield list in this note

✚
Six changes, all 1978, all testable. (1) "Internal disturbance" replaced by "armed rebellion" as a ground under Art 352. (2) Proclamation only on the written recommendation of the Cabinet. (3) Approval majority raised to a special majority (it had been simple). (4) Renewal cut to every 6 months (it had been one year). (5) The Lok Sabha given power to force revocation, requisitionable by one-tenth of members. (6) Articles 20 and 21 made non-suspendable under Art 359, and Art 358 confined to war or external aggression. It also removed the 38th Amendment's bar on judicial review.
02

National Emergency — Article 352

🌱 The story, in order

1962. China crosses the border. A National Emergency is proclaimed on the ground of external aggression and, remarkably, is left in force until 1968 — long after the fighting ends. 1971. War with Pakistan; a second proclamation. It is still running when, in June 1975, a third is proclaimed on the ground of internal disturbance — so for a period India was under two overlapping emergencies at once.

The 1975 proclamation is the one that matters. Elections were postponed, opposition leaders detained, the press censored, and in ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976) the Supreme Court held 4:1 that during an Emergency a detainee had no right even to move a court for habeas corpus — the most criticised judgment in Indian constitutional history. Justice H.R. Khanna alone dissented, and was passed over for Chief Justice as a result.

The Janata government elected in 1977 wrote the 44th Amendment to make each of those steps harder. And in Puttaswamy (2017), forty-one years later, the nine-judge bench formally declared ADM Jabalpur overruled.

That story is the syllabus. Every mechanic below is a response to one paragraph of it.
PointDetail
GroundsWar, external aggression, or armed rebellion — and the President may act on the imminent danger of any of them, before it actually occurs. When declared on war or external aggression it is called an External Emergency; on armed rebellion, an Internal Emergency.
Territorial extentMay cover the whole of India or only a part of it — this flexibility was added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976.
Cabinet safeguardThe President may proclaim only on the written recommendation of the Cabinet (44th Amendment). In 1975 the proclamation was issued on the Prime Minister's advice alone, with the Cabinet informed afterwards — this clause exists to make that impossible.
ApprovalBoth Houses, within one month, by a special majority — a majority of the total membership of the House and two-thirds of members present and voting. Note this is the same special majority as Article 368.
DurationSix months at a time, extendable indefinitely by fresh approval every six months. There is no outer limit — the check is procedural, not temporal.
If the Lok Sabha is dissolvedIf a proclamation is issued while the Lok Sabha stands dissolved, or it is dissolved during the one month without approving, the proclamation survives until 30 days from the first sitting of the reconstituted Lok Sabha — provided the Rajya Sabha has approved it meanwhile.
RevocationBy the President at any time, by a subsequent proclamation, needing no parliamentary approval. Separately, the Lok Sabha alone may pass a simple-majority resolution disapproving continuance, and a special sitting must be held if one-tenth of its members give written notice — a 44th Amendment innovation and a favourite question.
Judicial reviewThe 38th Amendment (1975) made the proclamation immune from review; the 44th Amendment removed that immunity. In Minerva Mills (1980) the Court confirmed a proclamation may be challenged for mala fide or on wholly extraneous grounds.

What actually changes on the ground

DimensionEffect while a National Emergency is in force
Centre–State executive relationsThe Union may give directions to any State on any matter — the State government is not suspended, but its executive power becomes subordinate to Union direction. This is the change that makes the federation "unitary" in Ambedkar's phrase.
Legislative powerParliament may legislate on any State List subject. Such laws lapse six months after the Emergency ends. The State legislature is not dissolved and may still legislate — but Union law prevails in a conflict.
Financial relationsThe President may modify the distribution of revenues between Centre and States, by order, until the end of the financial year in which the Emergency ceases.
Life of the legislatureThe Lok Sabha's term may be extended one year at a time, with no cumulative limit while the Emergency lasts — but never beyond six months after it ends. The same applies to State Assemblies. The 1976 extension of the Lok Sabha's life used exactly this power.
Fundamental RightsArticles 358 and 359 operate — the whole of Section 03 below.
03

Effects on rights — Articles 358 and 359

These two Articles look similar and are examined almost entirely on the differences between them. Learn them as a pair, never separately.

Point of differenceArticle 358Article 359
What it doesSuspends the rights themselves under Article 19 — the State is freed to make laws and take executive action inconsistent with Art 19.Suspends only the right to move a court to enforce specified rights. The rights remain alive; the remedy is frozen.
Which rightsArticle 19 only, and automatically.Any Fundamental Rights specified in the Presidential order — except Articles 20 and 21, which the 44th Amendment placed permanently beyond reach.
TriggerOperates automatically the moment the Emergency is proclaimed.Requires a separate Presidential order; it does not follow automatically.
Ground restrictionOnly when the Emergency is on grounds of war or external aggression — not armed rebellion (44th Amendment).Available on all three grounds, including armed rebellion.
Territorial extentExtends to the whole of India (or the part covered by the proclamation).May be confined to part of India, as specified in the order.
Laws made under itLaws made during the period become inoperative when the Emergency ends — but acts already done under them cannot be challenged even afterwards.Same: the immunity covers acts done during the operation of the order.
!
The one-line discriminator. 358 suspends the right; 359 suspends the remedy. Everything else follows from that. And the two facts most often inverted: 358 is automatic but only for war/external aggression; 359 is not automatic but available on all grounds. Arts 20 and 21 are safe under both — that is the 44th Amendment's single most important line, and it is the direct legislative answer to ADM Jabalpur.
04

President's Rule — Article 356

Used over 125 times, and for four decades the sharpest weapon in Centre–State politics. The law here is almost entirely judge-made — the text has barely changed since 1950.

PointDetail
GroundThe President is satisfied that the government of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution — on receipt of a report from the Governor "or otherwise". Those two words mean a Governor's report is not legally necessary.
Related provisionArticle 365 — a distinct route: if a State fails to comply with a Union direction, the President may hold that the government cannot be carried on constitutionally. Article 365 is the trigger; 356 is the instrument.
ApprovalBoth Houses, within two months, by simple majority.
DurationSix months at a time, to a maximum of three years. Beyond one year, extension requires both: a National Emergency in force in the whole country or that State, and the Election Commission certifying that elections cannot be held. This two-condition rule came in with the 44th Amendment and is heavily tested.
EffectsThe President assumes the State's executive functions; the State Assembly is suspended or dissolved; Parliament legislates for the State and may delegate that power to the President; the President may authorise expenditure from the State's Consolidated Fund when the Lok Sabha is not in session.
What it does not touchThe President cannot assume the powers of the State High Court, and Fundamental Rights are unaffected. Both are standard trap statements.

The case law — where the real restraint came from

✚
The arc: State of Rajasthan (1977) took a narrow view of reviewability → S.R. Bommai (1994), nine judges, transformed the position → Rameshwar Prasad (2006) applied it → Nabam Rebia (2016) narrowed a related Governor power. Bommai's four holdings, in the order they get asked: (1) majority must be tested on the floor of the House, not by the Governor's subjective assessment; (2) the proclamation is justiciable and may be struck down for mala fide or irrelevant grounds; (3) the Assembly may not be dissolved until both Houses of Parliament approve the proclamation — dissolution before approval is unconstitutional; (4) a dissolved Assembly can be revived if the proclamation is struck down. Bommai also held secularism to be part of the basic structure, which is why it appears in Citizenship and Federalism answers too.
!
The remedy gap, worth a line in any Mains answer. In Rameshwar Prasad (2006) the Court held the Bihar dissolution unconstitutional and mala fide — and then declined to restore the Assembly, because fresh elections had already been notified. Judicial review of Article 356 is real, but it arrives after the political fact. The Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions both recommended that 356 be used only as a last resort, with a warning issued first; neither recommendation has been enacted.
05

Financial Emergency — Article 360

Never once proclaimed — and its disuse is itself the examinable point.

PointDetail
GroundThe President is satisfied that a situation has arisen threatening the financial stability or credit of India or of any part of its territory.
Approval and durationSimple majority in both Houses within two months. Once approved it continues indefinitely — no periodic renewal, unlike both other emergencies. Revocation is by the President alone.
Effect on StatesThe Union may direct States to observe specified canons of financial propriety; all Money Bills and other financial Bills passed by a State legislature may be required to be reserved for the President's consideration.
Effect on salariesThe President may direct the reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving the Union or a State — expressly including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. This is one of the very few situations in which judicial salaries may be cut, and it is the most-asked fact about Article 360.
Fundamental RightsUnaffected.
Judicial reviewAvailable — the 38th Amendment's bar was removed by the 44th.
✚
Why it has never been used, and why that matters. Not in 1991, when foreign reserves covered about three weeks of imports and gold was physically pledged abroad. Severe fiscal stress has instead been managed through ordinary instruments — FRBM Act discipline, borrowing limits under Article 293, and Finance Commission transfers. The constitutional reading is that a provision this centralising has become a last resort by convention even though its text remains fully available — a good closing line for a Mains answer, because it shows the difference between what a Constitution permits and what a constitutional culture allows.

Prelims Questions

Genuine PYQs first, then practice questions in identical format. Attempt each before revealing — the retrieval is the point.

2018UPSC Prelims · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Parliament of India can place a particular law in the Ninth Schedule of the Constitution of India.
  2. The validity of a law placed in the Ninth Schedule cannot be examined by any court and no judgement can be made on it.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (a) — 1 only
  • Included here because the Ninth Schedule question is the same species as an emergency question: a claim of absolute immunity that a later judgment destroyed.
  • I.R. Coelho (2007) held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 remain open to challenge on basic-structure grounds. "Cannot be examined by any court" is therefore false.
  • Carry the pattern into Part XVIII: the 38th Amendment claimed the same immunity for emergency proclamations, and the 44th removed it.
Practice · Statements

With reference to a proclamation of National Emergency under Article 352, consider the following statements:

  1. It can be proclaimed only on the written recommendation of the Cabinet.
  2. It must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a simple majority.
  3. It can be revoked by the President without parliamentary approval.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (b) — only two
  • Statement 1 is correct — a 44th Amendment safeguard, written precisely because the 1975 proclamation was issued on the Prime Minister's advice alone.
  • Statement 2 is wrong on the majority. The deadline of one month is right; the majority required is a special majority, not simple. Simple majority applies to Articles 356 and 360.
  • Statement 3 is correct — revocation needs no approval at all, for any of the three emergencies.
Practice · Negative

Which one of the following statements about the Financial Emergency under Article 360 is NOT correct?

  • (a) It requires approval by both Houses within two months by a simple majority
  • (b) Once approved, it continues indefinitely without periodic parliamentary renewal
  • (c) The salaries of Supreme Court and High Court judges may be reduced while it is in force
  • (d) Fundamental Rights under Article 19 stand automatically suspended
Answer: (d) is NOT correct
  • Automatic suspension of Article 19 belongs to Article 358, which operates only during a National Emergency declared on war or external aggression. A Financial Emergency leaves Fundamental Rights entirely untouched.
  • (a), (b) and (c) are all accurate — and (c) is the single most-asked fact about Article 360.
  • Circle the word NOT before you evaluate. Test each option as true or false normally, then flip at the end.
Practice · Statements

With reference to Articles 358 and 359, consider the following statements:

  1. Article 358 operates automatically on the proclamation of a National Emergency on any of the three grounds.
  2. Article 359 requires a separate order of the President specifying the rights whose enforcement is suspended.
  3. Neither Article permits the suspension of Articles 20 and 21.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 2 and 3 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (b) — 2 and 3 only
  • Statement 1 is wrong on one word. Article 358 is automatic, but only where the Emergency rests on war or external aggression — the 44th Amendment removed armed rebellion from its scope. "Any of the three grounds" is the trap.
  • Statement 2 is correct — Article 359 never operates by itself.
  • Statement 3 is correct — Articles 20 and 21 are beyond both, the direct legislative answer to ADM Jabalpur.
Practice · How many

Consider the following consequences:

  1. The State Legislative Assembly is dissolved or suspended.
  2. Parliament acquires power to legislate for the State.
  3. The President assumes the powers of the State High Court.
  4. Fundamental Rights in the State stand suspended.

How many of the above follow from a proclamation under Article 356?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (b) — only two
  • 1 and 2 follow. The Assembly is suspended or dissolved, and Parliament legislates for the State.
  • 3 does not. The President may assume the State's executive and legislative functions but never the powers of the High Court — an express limit and a standard distractor.
  • 4 does not. Only a National Emergency touches Fundamental Rights.
Practice · Assertion–Reason

Statement-I: A proclamation under Article 356 cannot ordinarily be continued beyond one year.

Statement-II: Extension beyond one year requires both a National Emergency in force and an Election Commission certification that elections cannot be held.

Which one of the following is correct?

  • (a) Both are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (b) Both are correct but Statement-II is not the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
  • (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct
Answer: (a)
  • Both statements are correct, and the second is precisely why the first is true — the two conditions imposed by the 44th Amendment are what make continuation beyond a year extraordinary rather than routine.
  • Note the word "ordinarily" in Statement-I. Without it the statement would be false, since extension to a maximum of three years is possible.
  • The maximum is three years; the two-condition gate applies to everything past the first year.
Practice · Direct

The power of the Lok Sabha to force revocation of a National Emergency by resolution may be triggered by a written notice of at least:

  • (a) One-fourth of its members
  • (b) One-tenth of its members
  • (c) Fifty members
  • (d) One-half of its members
Answer: (b) — one-tenth
  • A 44th Amendment safeguard: on written notice from one-tenth of Lok Sabha members, a special sitting must be held within fourteen days to consider a disapproval resolution, which passes by simple majority.
  • Do not confuse the fractions: one-fourth is for signing a presidential impeachment charge, fifty members is the support needed to admit a no-confidence or adjournment motion.
  • This power belongs to the Lok Sabha alone — the Rajya Sabha has no equivalent.
Practice · Pairs

Consider the following pairs:

  1. 38th Amendment — made an emergency proclamation immune from judicial review
  2. 42nd Amendment — permitted a National Emergency to be confined to a part of India
  3. 44th Amendment — replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion"

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (c) — all three
  • All three are correct, and the sequence is the story: the 38th (1975) and 42nd (1976) loosened the provisions; the 44th (1978) rebuilt them.
  • Note the instinct this question is designed to defeat — the reflex to assume at least one pair in a set must be wrong. "All correct" is a real answer and appears regularly in genuine papers.
  • If a safeguard in Part XVIII is not obviously dated, the safest single guess is the 44th Amendment.

Mains Questions

Written to the standard set in MAINS_ANSWER_CRAFT.md — budget-checked, with the doctrinal arc opening the body and the cut/bought trade shown.

M30Practice · Week 415 marks · 250 words

"The emergency provisions of the Indian Constitution have been criticised as an invitation to dictatorship, yet the record suggests the remedy has come from amendment and adjudication rather than repeal." Critically examine.

Directive: "Critically examine." Deconstruct the criticism and deliver a verdict. An answer that only attacks or only defends Part XVIII is capped, however well written.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Ambedkar's design and the Assembly's objection
  2. The criticism, evidenced by 1975–77
  3. The repair — amendment and adjudication
  4. What remains unfixed
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Part XVIII makes the Constitution unitary in emergency and federal in normal times — Ambedkar's own defence in the Constituent Assembly, against K.T. Shah and H.V. Kamath, who called the provisions an invitation to dictatorship.

The criticism, evidenced

The 1975 proclamation vindicated the critics: issued on the Prime Minister's advice without Cabinet recommendation, on the elastic ground of "internal disturbance"; the Lok Sabha's life extended; and in ADM Jabalpur (1976) the Court held 4:1 that even habeas corpus was unavailable, Khanna J. alone dissenting. The 38th Amendment had already barred judicial review of the proclamation, and the 42nd widened the machinery further.

The repair — amendment

The 44th Amendment (1978) rebuilt every failed safeguard: "armed rebellion" replaced "internal disturbance"; proclamation only on the written recommendation of the Cabinet; approval by special majority within a month; renewal every six months; revocation forceable by the Lok Sabha on one-tenth notice; and Articles 20 and 21 placed beyond suspension.

The repair — adjudication

S.R. Bommai (1994) did for Article 356 what the 44th did for 352: floor test, justiciability, and no dissolution before parliamentary approval. Minerva Mills (1980) confirmed review for mala fides, and Puttaswamy (2017) formally overruled ADM Jabalpur.

What remains unfixed

Article 356 has still been used over 125 times; remedies arrive late, as Rameshwar Prasad (2006) showed when a mala fide dissolution went unreversed; and Sarkaria and Punchhi on last-resort use remain unimplemented.

Verdict

The criticism was sound as to the original text and is largely answered as to the present one. Repeal was never the right remedy — a Constitution without an emergency switch fails differently. What the record shows is that procedural safeguards and judicial review, not abolition, are what make an emergency power survivable.

Last-mile revision — Emergency Provisions

  • Majorities: Art 352 = special majority in 1 month. Arts 356 and 360 = simple majority in 2 months.
  • Duration: 352 = 6 months at a time, no outer limit. 356 = 6 months at a time, max 3 years. 360 = indefinite, no renewal.
  • 356 beyond 1 year needs BOTH a National Emergency in force AND EC certification that elections cannot be held (44th Amdt).
  • Revocation: President alone, no approval needed, for all three. Only for 352 can the Lok Sabha force it — special sitting on 1/10th notice, simple majority.
  • Grounds 352: war, external aggression, armed rebellion (+ imminent danger). "Armed rebellion" replaced "internal disturbance" — 44th Amdt.
  • Art 358 suspends the right (Art 19 only, automatic, war/external aggression only, whole of India). Art 359 suspends the remedy (specified rights by Presidential order, all three grounds, may be partial). Arts 20 and 21 never.
  • ADM Jabalpur (1976) 4:1, Khanna J. dissenting — overruled in Puttaswamy (2017).
  • 44th Amdt six safeguards: armed rebellion · written Cabinet recommendation · special majority · 6-month renewal · Lok Sabha revocation · Arts 20/21 protected. It also undid the 38th's bar on judicial review.
  • 42nd Amdt (1976): Emergency may be confined to part of India.
  • 356 effects: Assembly suspended/dissolved; Parliament legislates. NOT the High Court's powers; NOT Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 365 — failure to comply with a Union direction; the trigger, with 356 as the instrument.
  • Bommai (1994), 9 judges: floor test · justiciable · no dissolution before parliamentary approval · Assembly revivable. Also secularism = basic structure.
  • Art 360: never used, not even 1991. Judges' salaries may be reduced. State Money Bills reserved for the President. FRs untouched.
  • Lok Sabha term extendable 1 year at a time during a National Emergency, never beyond 6 months after it ends.
  • Times used: 352 → three (1962, 1971, 1975). 356 → 125+. 360 → zero.
