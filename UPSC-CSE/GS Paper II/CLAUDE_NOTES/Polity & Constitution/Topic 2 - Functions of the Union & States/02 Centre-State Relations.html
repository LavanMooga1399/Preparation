Centre-State Relations

Senior-mentor notes on Part XI and XII of the Constitution — how legislative, administrative and financial power is actually split between the Union and the States, and how that split has drifted since 1950 through the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions, GST, and the Finance Commission.

Articles 245-255 Article 254 Repugnancy Articles 268-293 Finance Commission GST Council
3relations — legislative, administrative, financial
◆ Why this note exists

Note 01 (Federal System) fixed the vocabulary — federal vs unitary features, the four scholarly labels. This note applies that vocabulary to the actual constitutional machinery: which government can legislate on what, who administers what, and who collects and spends which money. It is the direct continuation of Week 2, Day 5 in the study plan.

Centre-State relations is also the single most frequently Mains-tested sub-theme in this Topic — cooperative vs competitive federalism, GST Council disputes, and the Finance Commission's evolving formula are all live current-affairs threads that hang off the constitutional skeleton built in this note.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Note 01 established that India splits power between the Centre and the States, with the Centre holding the stronger hand. This note is where that split gets made concrete, across three separate questions that keep coming up whenever the Centre and a State disagree about something: who is allowed to make the law on this subject (legislative relations)? Once a law exists, who actually carries it out on the ground — inspectors, police, officials (administrative relations)? And who collects the tax money involved, and who ends up keeping it (financial relations)?

Most of the time these three questions have a clean, list-based answer — the Seventh Schedule simply tells you, subject by subject, whether it belongs to the Union List, the State List, or the Concurrent List (both can legislate). This note's real value is in the messier cases: situations the plain three-list system doesn't cleanly cover, where the Constitution had to build a special extra rule — Parliament reaching into State territory during a national emergency, or a State law and a Union law on the same Concurrent List subject actually contradicting each other. Those special-case rules (Articles 249-254 for legislation, 256-263 for administration, 268-293 for money) are where nearly all of this note's exam value lives, precisely because they are the exceptions, not the routine.

02

Legislative relations — Articles 245 to 255

Part XI, Chapter I of the Constitution governs who can make laws on what — the Seventh Schedule's three lists are the mechanical backbone, but five special-circumstance provisions let Parliament reach into State territory beyond the plain list division.

ProvisionWhat it does
Article 245Territorial extent — Parliament can legislate for all of India or any part; a State legislature only for its own State. Only Parliament can make extraterritorial legislation — laws operating on Indian citizens and their property anywhere in the world; no State law can have extraterritorial operation (except via the territorial-nexus doctrine). A Week 2 question tested this Parliament-only rule directly.
Article 246 & 7th ScheduleUnion List (Parliament exclusively), State List (State legislature ordinarily), Concurrent List (both — Article 254 resolves conflicts).
Article 249Rajya Sabha, by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting, can empower Parliament to legislate on a State List subject "in the national interest" for up to a year at a time.
Article 250During a national Emergency (Article 352), Parliament can legislate on any State List subject for the whole country.
Article 252If two or more States consent, Parliament can legislate on a State subject for those States alone — other States may adopt the law later.
Article 253Parliament can legislate on any subject, including State List ones, to implement an international treaty, agreement or convention.
Article 254The doctrine of repugnancy — a State law on a Concurrent List subject that conflicts with a Union law is void to that extent, unless it received the President's assent under Article 254(2), in which case it prevails in that State (though Parliament can still override it later with fresh legislation).
⚠️

Common trap: Article 254(2)'s Presidential-assent exception is frequently tested precisely because it looks like it hands the State a permanent win — it does not. Parliament retains the power to legislate afresh on the same matter at any time, which would override even a Presidential-assented State law. The State's win is conditional, not final.

✚

Seventh Schedule — where four commonly-confused subjects actually sit. Knowing the three-list structure exists isn't enough; MCQs test specific subject placements, and these four are the ones most often used as traps because their "obvious" list isn't the correct one:

SubjectActual listWhy it trips people up
BankingUnion List (Entry 45)Feels like it could be economic/State given local bank branches, but banking regulation is Union-only.
ElectricityConcurrent List (Entry 38)Commonly mis-assumed to be State-only because State Electricity Boards run distribution — generation/transmission/distribution as a subject is Concurrent.
ForestsConcurrent List (Entry 17A)Was originally a State subject; moved to Concurrent by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 — the same amendment that added the Fundamental Duties (Note 09) and Article 48A (environment, Note 08).
EducationConcurrent List (Entry 25)Also moved from State to Concurrent by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 — same year, same logic as Forests.

Pattern to remember: Forests and Education share an origin story — both started as pure State List subjects and were deliberately shifted to Concurrent by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, to give the Union a formal say. If a question's trap option claims either is "State List only," that's almost always testing whether you know this 1976 shift.

✚

Doctrine of Colourable Legislation (a Week 2 question tested the definition). The maxim: "what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly." If a legislature lacks competence over a subject, it cannot capture it by dressing the law up as something within its powers — courts look at the law's substance, not its form or label, and strike it down if the "true nature and character" falls in the forbidden field. Classic illustration: K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo (1953) framed the test; a State cannot, say, legislate on a Union subject by disguising it as a tax measure within its own list. Distinguish it from its neighbours: pith and substance (incidental overlap is forgiven if the law's core is within competence) is the doctrine that SAVES laws; colourable legislation is the doctrine that KILLS them — a ready-made pairs trap.

03

Administrative relations — Articles 256 to 263

Beyond who can legislate, the Constitution also governs who executes and administers — and gives the Union several levers to direct, and in extreme cases override, State administration.

ProvisionWhat it does
Article 256-257State executive power must be exercised so as not to impede Union laws; the Union can direct a State on specific matters, including constructing/maintaining means of communication of national or military importance.
Article 258The President may, with State consent, entrust Union executive functions to a State (and vice versa under Article 258A after the 7th Amendment).
All-India ServicesIAS/IPS/IFoS officers are recruited and trained by the Union but serve the States — a structural administrative link (Note 01, Section 03).
Article 261Full faith and credit across India for public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State (developed further in Note 03).
Article 263Empowers the President to establish an Inter-State Council to investigate and advise on disputes and coordination between States (developed fully in Note 03).
Article 355Duty of the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance — the constitutional basis invoked before deploying central forces or, historically, before imposing President's Rule.
04

Financial relations — Articles 268 to 293

Part XII governs who levies, who collects, and who ultimately keeps tax revenue — and this is the sub-head with the most active current-affairs overlay, thanks to GST and successive Finance Commissions.

Body / provisionRole
Finance Commission (Article 280)A quasi-judicial body — Chairman + 4 members, appointed by the President every five years or as needed — recommends vertical devolution (Centre-to-States share of net tax proceeds) and horizontal devolution (the formula splitting the States' share among themselves), plus grants-in-aid under Article 275.
GST Council (Article 279A)Inserted by the 101st Amendment, 2016; chaired by the Union Finance Minister, with every State's minister as a member; decides GST rates and structure by three-fourths weighted majority (Centre 1/3, States 2/3 combined).
Article 282Both Union and States can make discretionary grants for any public purpose, even outside their own legislative competence — the constitutional basis for many Centrally Sponsored Schemes.
Article 293States can borrow, but if indebted to the Union, need Union consent to raise further loans — a structural financial dependency lever. Live edge: Kerala's Article 131 suit against Union borrowing ceilings was referred to a Constitution Bench in 2024 — fiscal autonomy is now a justiciable question, not just a political grievance.
✚

Recent-judgment layer — Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022): what the GST Council's recommendations actually are. The Supreme Court held that GST Council recommendations are not binding on the Union or the States — they carry persuasive value, but Article 279A creates a body whose decisions become law only when governments act on them, because both Parliament and State legislatures retain simultaneous power to legislate on GST (Article 246A). The judgment's most quotable line for Mains: Indian federalism is "a dialogue between cooperative and uncooperative federalism" in which States can use varied means of persuasion. Trap this defuses: a statement claiming "GST Council decisions are binding on all States" is wrong — the system works by fiscal-political alignment, not legal compulsion.

✚

Cross-link — the Governor/assent battleground: the other live Centre-State flashpoint, the 2025 Bill-assent timeline saga (TN Governor case → Article 143 reference → Constitution Bench walk-back), is covered in full in Topic 6, Note 01, Section 05 — cite it in any "friction points in Centre-State relations" answer alongside the fiscal examples here.

05

Trends in Centre-State relations

Two commissions and one tax reform have shaped how the machinery above is actually used in practice — this is the section Mains rewards most, since "trends" questions expect movement over time, not just a static list of articles.

●
Sarkaria Commission (1983-88): recommended greater consultation via the Inter-State Council, restraint in using Article 356, and more careful appointment of Governors — many recommendations remain only partially implemented.
●
Punchhi Commission (2007-10): revisited Sarkaria for a changed context (coalition governments, economic liberalisation), proposing localised emergency provisions and clearer Governor-appointment criteria.
●
Cooperative federalism: the post-2014 framing (NITI Aayog replacing the Planning Commission, GST Council as a joint decision body) emphasising States as partners, not merely implementing agencies.
●
Competitive federalism: States increasingly compete with each other for investment, rankings (Ease of Doing Business) and Central scheme allocations — a newer dynamic layered on top of cooperative structures.

M172020 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

How far do you think cooperation, competition and confrontation have shaped the nature of federation in India? Cite some recent examples to validate your answer.

Directive: "How far… cite recent examples." A degree question with a mandatory evidence requirement. The three words in the question — cooperation, competition, confrontation — are your three sub-headings. Using them verbatim proves compliance instantly to a skimming examiner.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: all three coexist
  2. Cooperation — with examples
  3. Competition — with examples
  4. Confrontation — with examples
  5. Degree verdict + way forward

Model answer

Indian federalism has never been shaped by a single dynamic: cooperation, competition and confrontation operate simultaneously, and the balance among them shifts with political circumstance.

Cooperation

The GST Council (Article 279A) pools sovereignty over indirect taxation — the Union holding one-third of the weighted vote, the States two-thirds. NITI Aayog replaced directive planning with consultation; the Inter-State Council (Article 263) and Zonal Councils provide standing dialogue; and the COVID-19 response required continuous Centre–State coordination.

Competition

The Union ranks States on ease of doing business, health and SDG indices, driving reform through reputational pressure; States compete for investment; and the 15th Finance Commission's formula rewards demographic performance and tax effort. This is efficiency-enhancing, but risks a race to the bottom on labour and environmental standards and penalises weaker starting capacity.

Confrontation

Territorial: the Article 370 abrogation, upheld in In re Article 370 (2023). Administrative: the Delhi services dispute — the 2023 Constitution Bench ruling followed within months by the GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023 — and the Governor's withholding of assent to State Bills, litigated through 2025. Fiscal: rising cesses and surcharges shrinking the divisible pool, and Kerala's Article 131 suit over borrowing ceilings.

Verdict

All three have shaped the federation substantially but unevenly: cooperation dominates fiscal-administrative machinery, competition drives policy performance, and confrontation surfaces wherever constitutional silences exist. Mohit Minerals (2022) captured it exactly — Indian federalism is "a dialogue between cooperative and uncooperative federalism." Strengthening the Inter-State Council and implementing the Punchhi Commission on the Governor would convert confrontation into structured negotiation.

Value additions used: Art 279A vote weighting (1/3 : 2/3) · Art 263 · 15th Finance Commission · In re Article 370 (2023) · Delhi services 2023 + GNCTD Amendment Act · the 2025 assent saga · Kerala's Art 131 suit · Mohit Minerals (2022) quotation · Punchhi Commission.
Budget check: 248 / 250 words. Cut: the "referred to a Constitution Bench" tail on Kerala's suit and the disadvantaged-States explanation, compressed to a clause. Bought: nothing new — length only, 282 → budget. The answer already carried nine datable specifics; the risk here was overshoot, not thinness.

M18Practice10 marks · 150 words

Cooperative federalism and competitive federalism are not contradictory but complementary trends in India's Centre-State relations. Discuss.

Directive: "Discuss" a complementarity claim. Show the apparent tension first, then resolve it. A 150-word answer cannot do both at length — compress by giving each trend two lines and spending the remaining space on how they interlock.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: define both
  2. Why they look contradictory
  3. Why they are complementary
  4. The caveat
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Cooperative federalism means Centre and States acting as partners in shared institutions; competitive federalism means States competing with one another for investment, performance and recognition.

The apparent contradiction

Cooperation implies consensus and pooled decision-making; competition implies rivalry and divergence — and may widen inter-State inequality, since better-resourced States win more often.

Why they are complementary

They operate on different axes. Cooperation is vertical — Centre with States, through the GST Council (Art 279A), the Inter-State Council (Art 263) and NITI Aayog. Competition is horizontal — State against State, through ease-of-doing-business, SDG and health rankings. The same institution often drives both: NITI Aayog convenes States cooperatively and ranks them competitively. Cooperation sets the common rules within which competition is fair; competition supplies the performance incentive consensus alone cannot generate.

The caveat

Complementarity holds only if competition is equitably framed — hence the 15th Finance Commission's balance of equity against performance-based transfers.

They are therefore two instruments of one design: cooperation builds the platform, competition raises the standard.

Value additions used: the vertical / horizontal axis distinction · Arts 279A and 263 · NITI Aayog's dual role · 15th Finance Commission's equity-performance balance.
Budget check: 162 / 150 words. Cut: the race-to-the-bottom risk, which M17 already carries in full. Bought: nothing new — length only. Note the cross-answer discipline: when two answers in the same topic can carry a point, give it to the one with the larger budget.
What would cost you marks: defining both and stopping — the marks are in the mechanism of complementarity; ignoring the equity caveat, which is what makes the answer balanced.
◆ One-page recall sheet

Last-mile revision — Centre-State Relations

  • Legislative: Art 246 + 7th Schedule (Union/State/Concurrent). Art 254 — Union law prevails on Concurrent List conflict, unless State law has Presidential assent (still overridable later).
  • Parliament can legislate on State subjects via: Art 249 (Rajya Sabha 2/3, national interest), Art 250 (Emergency), Art 252 (State consent), Art 253 (international treaty).
  • Administrative: Art 256-257 (Union directions to States), Art 258/258A (mutual delegation), Art 261 (full faith & credit), Art 263 (Inter-State Council), Art 355 (Union's protective duty).
  • Financial: Art 280 Finance Commission (vertical + horizontal devolution, grants-in-aid under Art 275); Art 279A GST Council (Centre 1/3, States 2/3 weighted vote, 3/4 majority to decide).
  • Sarkaria Commission (1983) and Punchhi Commission (2007) — both reviewed Centre-State balance; recommendations only partially implemented.
  • Cooperative federalism (NITI Aayog, GST Council) vs Competitive federalism (States competing for investment/rankings) — the current live tension.
  • Genuine Mains PYQ: 2020, cooperation/competition/confrontation shaping Indian federation.
