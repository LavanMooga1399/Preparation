Inter-State Relations

Senior-mentor notes on how States deal with each other rather than with the Centre — water disputes and their tribunals, the Inter-State Council, mutual recognition of each other's public acts and judicial proceedings, free trade & commerce across State lines, and the Zonal Councils that quietly keep neighbouring States talking.

Article 262 Article 263 Article 261 Articles 301-307 Zonal Councils
5zones under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956
◆ Why this note exists

Notes 01 and 02 covered the Union-State axis of federalism. This note covers the other axis — State-to-State — which UPSC tests just as often, especially through current-affairs-driven water-sharing disputes and the periodic revival of Inter-State Council meetings. This closes out the three-note Topic 2 batch built for Week 2, Days 4-6 of the study plan.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Every note in this Topic so far has been about the Centre versus a State. This one is about something different: 28 States sharing one country, and inevitably bumping into each other — a river that flows through three States, a court judgment in one State that needs to be recognised in another, goods that need to cross a State border freely rather than face a local tax barrier. None of these problems involve the Centre taking sides against a State; they are States needing rules to deal fairly with each other.

The Constitution's solution, mostly, is to hand the Centre a neutral referee role rather than a rulebook that decides every case in advance: Parliament can set up a water-dispute Tribunal when States can't agree (Article 262), the President can set up a standing forum for States to talk things through (the Inter-State Council, Article 263), and a separate, purely administrative set of five regional Zonal Councils exists to keep neighbouring States talking informally, without any binding power at all. None of these bodies can force an outcome the way a court judgment can — they exist to make cooperation easier, not to compel it, which is exactly why "how much teeth does this body actually have" is the recurring exam question across this entire note.

02

Inter-State Water Disputes — Article 262

Rivers do not respect State boundaries, and water is a State subject (Entry 17, State List) except for the regulation of inter-State rivers, which Parliament can occupy (Entry 56, Union List) — this split is exactly why Article 262 exists.

ProvisionWhat it does
Article 262(1)Parliament may by law provide for the adjudication of any dispute relating to the waters of, or in, any inter-State river or river valley.
Article 262(2)Parliament may by law bar the Supreme Court and other courts from exercising jurisdiction over such a dispute — an unusual, explicit ouster of judicial review for this one category.
Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956The enabling law under Article 262 — allows the Union government to set up an ad hoc Water Disputes Tribunal when a State's request cannot be resolved through negotiation.
Tribunal compositionA Chairman and two other members nominated by the Chief Justice of India from among sitting Supreme Court or High Court judges.
EffectOnce notified, a Tribunal's decision has the force of a Supreme Court decree — but enforcement in practice has been the recurring weak link (no independent enforcement machinery of its own).
FinalityUnder the original 1956 Act's design, a Water Disputes Tribunal's award was meant to be final, with no appeal to the Supreme Court — Article 262(2) exists precisely to let Parliament exclude ordinary court appeals from these disputes (Section 02's trap box above). In practice, parties have still approached the Supreme Court via writ petitions challenging a Tribunal's process rather than a direct "appeal," which is the nuance examiners sometimes probe.
Reform debate (pros/cons/way forward)The standing criticism of the ad-hoc-tribunal model: decades-long delays (Cauvery's tribunal took 17 years to a final award), no enforcement machinery, and serial litigation despite "finality." The proposed fix — the Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Bill — would replace ad hoc tribunals with one standing permanent Tribunal with multiple benches, preceded by a mandatory Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC) stage, with fixed timelines for awards. For: speed, institutional memory, consistent jurisprudence. Against: water is a State List subject (Entry 17) and States fear a permanent central body tilting the field; timelines without enforcement teeth may replicate the old problem. Way forward (liftable): pair the permanent tribunal with a transparent, basin-wide data authority — most water disputes are at bottom disputes about contested flow data.
River Boards Act, 1956 — the forgotten twinParliament passed two laws under Article 262's neighbourhood in 1956, and a Week 2 question paired them: the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act (Union government constitutes ad hoc Tribunals — adjudicatory) and the River Boards Act (Union government may establish River Boards to advise on the regulation and development of an inter-State river, on request of the State governments — purely advisory, and in practice a dead letter: no River Board has ever been created). Trap pairing: Disputes Act = tribunal = binding award; River Boards Act = board = advice only, never used.
⚠️

Common trap: Article 262(2)'s court-ouster power is often confused with a blanket bar — it only applies to the specific dispute Parliament has legislated to route through a Tribunal. General constitutional questions arising incidentally (e.g., a fundamental-rights challenge) are not automatically barred from ordinary courts.

03

Inter-State Council — Article 263

Article 263 is the constitutional basis; the Council itself is not automatic — it needed a Presidential order to actually come into being, decades after 1950.

PointDetail
Constitutional basisArticle 263 empowers (does not mandate) the President to establish an Inter-State Council if it appears that doing so would serve the public interest.
EstablishmentActually set up in 1990 by a Presidential Order, following a recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission (Note 02, Section 05) — a 40-year gap between the constitutional power and its use.
CompositionChaired by the Prime Minister; members include Chief Ministers of all States and of Union Territories with a Legislative Assembly, and Administrators of UTs without one.
FunctionsInvestigating and discussing subjects of common interest between the Union and States, or among States; making recommendations for better coordination of policy.
Decision-makingBy consensus — meant to be a recommendatory forum, not a voting body with binding power.
Meeting frequencyMeant to meet at least three times a year, though in practice it has met infrequently — a real gap of nearly a decade preceded its most recent revival, discussed alongside the pending Sarkaria and Punchhi Commission recommendations.
04

Public Acts, Records & Judicial Proceedings — Article 261

The least-tested but most conceptually clean provision in this note — it exists to stop India's States from behaving like separate legal jurisdictions that ignore each other's official acts.

◆

Article 261, in three parts: (1) full faith and credit shall be given throughout India to public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State; (2) Parliament may by law prescribe the manner of proving such acts, records and proceedings, and their effect; (3) a final civil-court judgment or order from one part of India is executable anywhere in India according to law — a decree from a Mumbai court, for instance, does not need to be re-litigated to be enforced in Kolkata.

05

Inter-State Trade & Commerce, and Zonal Councils

The final two elements of this note — one constitutional (freedom of trade), one purely statutory (the zones) — both exist to stop India's internal market and administration from fragmenting along State lines.

Inter-State trade & commerce — Articles 301-307

ArticleWhat it does
Article 301Trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India shall be free — the constitutional foundation of India as a single economic unit.
Article 302Parliament can impose restrictions on this freedom in the public interest.
Article 303Neither Parliament nor a State legislature can give preference to one State over another, or discriminate between States, in trade/commerce matters — subject to Article 302's public-interest exception in cases of scarcity.
Article 304A State legislature can impose reasonable restrictions in the public interest, including non-discriminatory taxes on goods from other States, but needs the President's prior sanction for certain restrictive Bills.

Zonal Councils

PointDetail
Legal statusStatutory, not constitutional — created under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, unlike the constitutionally-rooted Inter-State Council.
CompositionUnion Home Minister as common Chairman; the Chief Minister of each member State takes a one-year turn as Vice-Chairman; other ministers and UT Administrators from the zone also sit on it.
PowersPurely advisory/recommendatory — a forum for cooperative federalism, not a binding decision-making body.
North Eastern CouncilA separate, sixth regional body for the Northeast, set up under its own North Eastern Council Act, 1971 — not one of the original five zones and not a UPSC trap to confuse with them.
NITI Aayog's roleNITI Aayog nominates a person to serve as an advisor to each of the five Zonal Councils — this advisor attends meetings but has no voting rights. Each zone's Chief Secretary and Development Commissioner also sit as advisors alongside.
OriginIdea proposed by PM Jawaharlal Nehru in 1956 during the States Reorganisation Commission debates — grouping States into zones was meant to ease linguistic tensions and encourage cooperative, regional-scale planning.
06

PYQ analysis

Trend verdict: Prelims tests this note as a "which of these bodies is constitutional vs statutory" statement-MCQ — confirmed genuine and recent (2025), built on exactly the distinction Section 03 and Section 05 draw between the Inter-State Council (constitutional, Article 263) and Zonal Councils (statutory, 1956 Act).

2025Prelims

"With reference to India, consider the following: I. The Inter-State Council II. The National Security Council III. Zonal Councils — How many of the above were established as per the provisions of the Constitution of India?"

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All the three
  • (d) None
Verdict: direct hit — Ans. (a) only one
  • Only one body listed is constitutional — the Inter-State Council, Article 263.
  • The National Security Council is an executive creation (1999), with no constitutional or statutory basis.
  • Zonal Councils are statutory, under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 — see Sections 03 and 05.
⚠️

Integrity note: Several older "which of these statements about Zonal Councils is correct" MCQs are widely circulated across coaching-site question banks, but this note cites only the 2025 question above, which could be independently confirmed with an exact year, source and answer key. Treat any other Zonal Council or Inter-State Council MCQ encountered elsewhere as unverified practice material unless you can confirm it against an official UPSC paper yourself.

M19Practice10 marks · 150 words

Discuss the constitutional and statutory mechanisms available in India for resolving inter-State disputes, with reference to river-water disputes.

Directive: "Discuss… with reference to." The tail narrows the illustration to river-water disputes, but the question asks about mechanisms generally — so cover the full architecture, then illustrate through water. Do not write only about water.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: constitutional vs statutory split
  2. Constitutional mechanisms
  3. Statutory mechanisms
  4. Why they underperform
  5. Reform + verdict

Model answer

The Constitution handles inter-State friction through Articles 262–263, supplemented by statute — a design that prefers tribunals and negotiation to ordinary litigation.

Constitutional mechanisms

Article 262 empowers Parliament to provide for adjudication of inter-State river-water disputes and, under 262(2), to bar the jurisdiction of all courts including the Supreme Court. Article 263 allows an Inter-State Council — established in 1990 on the Sarkaria Commission's recommendation. Article 131 gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over inter-State disputes generally.

Statutory mechanisms

Two Acts of 1956: the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, under which ad hoc Tribunals are constituted (Chairman plus two members nominated by the CJI), the notified award carrying the force of a Supreme Court decree; and the River Boards Act, under which not one Board has ever been created.

Why they underperform

Delay — the Cauvery tribunal took some 17 years; no independent enforcement machinery; and "finality" undermined by writ petitions. Cauvery, Krishna, Mahadayi and Vansadhara remain live. A permanent tribunal with fixed timelines would help, but only if paired with a basin-wide data authority — most water disputes are ultimately disputes about contested flow data.

Value additions used: Arts 262, 262(2), 263, 131 · Sarkaria Commission → Inter-State Council 1990 · both 1956 Acts · tribunal composition and decree-force of the award · no River Board ever created · Cauvery's 17-year delay · permanent tribunal + basin data authority.
Budget check: 182 / 150 words. Cut: Articles 301–307 and Zonal Councils (off-question), "Part XI", Narmada from the live list, and the President's role in constituting the Council. Bought: nothing new — length only, 242 → budget. The lesson worth keeping: Arts 301–307 were correct but outside the question's river-water framing, and correct-but-off-question content is the commonest way a well-prepared candidate loses marks.
What would cost you marks: writing only about water and ignoring the general architecture; forgetting the River Boards Act, which is the classic paired trap with the Disputes Act.
◆ One-page recall sheet

Last-mile revision — Inter-State Relations

  • Art 262: Parliament can adjudicate inter-State river water disputes, and can bar courts from that specific dispute (262(2)).
  • Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 — enables ad hoc Tribunals; Chairman + 2 members, sitting SC/HC judges nominated by CJI.
  • Live disputes to name: Cauvery, Krishna, Mahadayi/Mandovi.
  • Art 263: Inter-State Council — constitutional, but discretionary (President "may" establish). Actually set up only in 1990, on Sarkaria Commission's recommendation. Chaired by PM; decisions by consensus.
  • Art 261: full faith & credit for public acts, records, judicial proceedings across all of India — a decree from one State is executable in every other State.
  • Arts 301-307: freedom of trade & commerce throughout India (301); Parliament can restrict in public interest (302); no State-vs-State discrimination (303); States can tax non-discriminately with Presidential sanction (304).
  • Zonal Councils: statutory (States Reorganisation Act, 1956), not constitutional. 5 zones — North, South, East, West, Central. Union Home Minister is common Chairman. North Eastern Council is separate (1971 Act) — not one of the 5.
  • Genuine Prelims repeater: 2025 — only the Inter-State Council among ISC/NSC/Zonal Councils is a constitutional body.
