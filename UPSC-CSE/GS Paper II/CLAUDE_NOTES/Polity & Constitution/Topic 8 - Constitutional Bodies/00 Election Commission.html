Election Commission of India

Article 324 — one article for the whole institution, and almost every examinable fact is an asymmetry: between the Chief Election Commissioner and the other two, between what the Constitution says and what the 2023 Act does, and between what the Commission controls and what it does not.

Article 324 Multi-member since 1993 Anoop Baranwal, 2023 CEC Act, 2023 Six years or 65
1article — and the security of only one of the three Commissioners
◆ Where the marks are, and where they are not

Do not spend time on election procedure. The examiner asks about the institution: who appoints, who can remove whom, what the Commission decides and what it does not. Prelims 2017 tested exactly three things — whether the ECI is a five-member body, who fixes the election schedule, and who resolves disputes over party splits and mergers. All three are institutional.

The single highest-value idea in this note is the asymmetry of removal: the CEC has the security of a Supreme Court judge; the two Election Commissioners have none of their own. That one sentence has produced questions repeatedly, and it is also the hinge of every Mains answer on the Commission's independence.

Week 6, Day 3 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work Ch. 5–6 and Laxmikanth Chapter 43.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Somebody has to run elections, and that somebody cannot be the government of the day, because the government is a contestant. So the Constitution creates an independent body — the Election Commission — and gives it superintendence, direction and control of the whole electoral process for Parliament, the State legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President.

Notice what is not on that list: local body elections. Panchayat and municipal elections are run by State Election Commissions, which are entirely separate bodies created by Articles 243K and 243ZA. Confusing the two is the most common single error in this chapter.

For its first forty years the Commission was one man. It became a three-member body in 1993 and has stayed that way. All three have equal say — decisions are by majority — but only the Chief Election Commissioner is hard to remove. That mismatch between equal power and unequal protection is the institution's central design flaw and the thing to carry into any answer.

01

Composition & appointment — Article 324

PointDetail
CompositionArt 324(2) — a Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners as the President may from time to time fix. The Constitution fixes no number; the strength is at the President's discretion.
Current strengthThree — the CEC and two Election Commissioners. It has been three continuously since 1 October 1993.
The historySingle-member from 1950. Made three-member briefly in October 1989 (before the 1989 general election), reverted to single-member in January 1990, and made three-member again in October 1993. A statement saying it "has always been" multi-member, or that it became so in 1950, is false.
Decision-makingBusiness is transacted unanimously wherever possible; in case of difference, by majority. The CEC has no casting vote and no veto — he is primus inter pares, first among equals, not a superior.
Regional CommissionersArt 324(4) — the President may, after consulting the Election Commission, appoint Regional Commissioners to assist the Commission before a general election. Their conditions of service are determined by the President after consulting the Election Commission.
TenureSix years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier. This is prescribed by statute, not by the Constitution.
QualificationsThe Constitution prescribes none. Under the 2023 Act, a person must be or have been of the rank of Secretary to the Government of India, and be a person of integrity with knowledge of and experience in the management and conduct of elections.
📖 The story — how appointments changed in three years

For seventy-three years, Article 324(2) said appointments would be made by the President "subject to the provisions of any law made in that behalf by Parliament" — and Parliament simply never made that law. In practice the President appointed on the advice of the Council of Ministers, which meant the government of the day picked the umpire.

2 March 2023 — Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India. A five-judge Constitution Bench, led by Justice K.M. Joseph, held that the constitutional silence could not continue indefinitely. Until Parliament legislated, appointments would be made by the President on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and the Chief Justice of India. Where there is no recognised Leader of the Opposition, the leader of the single largest opposition party takes that place.

December 2023 — Parliament answered. The Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023 exercised the very power Art 324(2) had reserved. It kept a three-member selection committee but replaced the Chief Justice of India with a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister — giving the executive a two-to-one majority on the committee that chooses its own umpire. A Search Committee headed by the Cabinet Secretary shortlists five names for it.

Where it stands. The Act is under challenge before the Supreme Court on the ground that it defeats the very safeguard Anoop Baranwal created; the Court declined to stay it before the 2024 general election. Gyanesh Kumar, who took office on 19 February 2025 as the 26th CEC, is the first appointed under the Act.

!
The distinction the examiner will build on. Anoop Baranwal (2023) prescribed PM + LoP + CJI. The 2023 Act prescribes PM + LoP + a Union Cabinet Minister. A statement saying the Chief Justice of India sits on the selection committee describes the judgment, not the law in force — and is therefore false today. The Court's formula applied only "until Parliament makes a law", and Parliament made one.

Removal & independence — the asymmetry

Chief Election CommissionerThe two Election Commissioners
Removable only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court — a parliamentary address by special majority in both Houses, for proved misbehaviour or incapacity.Removable by the President — but only on the recommendation of the CEC.
Service conditions cannot be varied to his disadvantage after appointment.The same protection applies to their conditions of service.
Cannot be removed at the pleasure of the executive.Can be removed on one man's recommendation — which is the whole criticism.
!
Read the safeguard carefully — it protects the Commissioners from the executive and exposes them to the CEC. The requirement that the CEC recommend removal was designed to stop the government sacking an inconvenient Commissioner. But it leaves the two Commissioners dependent on the CEC, while giving them an equal vote against him. Equal power, unequal protection. The 2nd Administrative Reforms Commission and the Law Commission's 255th Report (2015) have both recommended extending the CEC's protection to all three.

Note also what is absent: the Constitution does not bar a retired Election Commissioner from further government appointment, and it does not prescribe a fixed term — the six-year/65-year rule is statutory. Both are standing criticisms and both are examinable as "the Constitution does not provide…" statements.

03

Powers & functions — and the boundary

CategoryWhat the Commission does
AdministrativeDelimits constituencies as per the Delimitation Commission Act; prepares and periodically revises electoral rolls; notifies the dates and schedule of elections and scrutinises nomination papers; grants recognition to political parties and allots election symbols; enforces the Model Code of Conduct; and registers political parties under section 29A of the RPA, 1951.
AdvisoryAdvises the President on the disqualification of a sitting member of Parliament, and the Governor on a sitting member of a State legislature. In both cases the opinion is binding on the recipient. It also advises the President on whether elections can be held in a State under President's Rule.
Quasi-judicialDecides disputes over the recognition of parties and allotment of symbols, including disputes arising from splits and mergers — settled by the Supreme Court in Sadiq Ali v. ECI (1972).
What it does not doLocal body elections (State Election Commissions, Arts 243K and 243ZA). Election petitions — these are decided by the High Court under the RPA, not by the Commission. And it does not register voters itself at the counter; that is done by electoral registration officers under its superintendence.
!
Three boundary facts that decide questions. (1) The ECI conducts elections to Parliament, State legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President — not to panchayats and municipalities, which belong to the State Election Commission under Arts 243K/243ZA. (2) The Commission fixes the election schedule — the Ministry of Home Affairs does not, and a 2017 question offered exactly that distractor. (3) An election petition challenging a completed election is heard by the High Court, with appeal to the Supreme Court; the Commission has no role once results are declared.
◆
The State Election Commission is a genuinely separate institution. Created by the 73rd and 74th Amendments, it consists of a State Election Commissioner appointed by the Governor, and he may be removed only in the manner and on the grounds prescribed for a High Court judge. His conditions of service cannot be varied to his disadvantage after appointment. It handles panchayat and municipal elections only. Every one of these facts is a near-mirror of the ECI's — which is precisely why they get swapped in a question.
◆
Delimitation, since it sits next door. Delimitation Commissions have been constituted four times — 1952, 1963, 1973 and 2002 (asked in Prelims 2024). Their orders have the force of law and cannot be questioned in any court. The 84th Amendment (2001) froze the allocation of Lok Sabha seats among States on the 1971 census, and the 87th Amendment (2003) allowed readjustment of constituencies within States on the 2001 census. The freeze runs until the first census after 2026.

Pass 2 — the adjacent surface

◆ Why this section exists

The Week 4 postmortem found eleven out-of-note questions against a ceiling of one or two. Not one of the eleven appeared in the schedule's “Important Topics” column. Every one sat one step outside it — an adjacent statutory body, a named committee, a who-chairs-what, or a State-level mirror of a Union body. Writing these sections deliberately is what cut Week 5's out-of-note losses from eleven to three.

So: the schedule says “Election Commission”. This section is everything a setter reaches for next.

The machinery below the Commission — nobody's schedule lists it

The Commission has no independent cadre of its own. It works through officers borrowed from the Union and State governments, and the exam asks who they are.

!
The pattern to hold. Almost every officer here is designated by the Commission in consultation with the State Government — not appointed by the State, and not by the Governor. A statement putting the State Government in the appointing seat is the standard distractor. The 2nd ARC flagged exactly this dependence on borrowed staff as a structural weakness.

The Special Intensive Revision — the live one

📖 The story — how roll revision became a constitutional dispute

Electoral rolls are ordinarily updated by summary revision — you apply, an officer verifies, the roll changes at the margins. An intensive revision is the heavy version: enumerators go house to house and the roll is rebuilt rather than edited.

In June 2025 the Commission ordered a Special Intensive Revision (SIR) beginning with Bihar, ahead of that State's Assembly election. Existing electors were asked to establish eligibility afresh, with documentary proof; the stated purpose was to remove the dead, the permanently shifted, duplicates and non-citizens. The objection was that it inverted the burden of proof — putting the onus on the voter to prove entitlement to a right already held, with exclusion as the penalty for failure.

Phase II ran from October 2025 to April 2026 across nine States and three Union Territories — Chhattisgarh, Goa, Gujarat, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, and Andaman & Nicobar, Lakshadweep and Puducherry. Phase III covers the remaining States and UTs, including Delhi.

27 May 2026 — Association for Democratic Reforms v. Election Commission of India. The Supreme Court upheld the SIR, holding it within the Commission's statutory mandate under the RPA, 1950 and consistent with its obligation under Article 324 to secure free and fair elections. The challenge failed.

!
Get the holding the right way round. The SIR was upheld, not struck down — and it was upheld on RPA 1950 + Article 324, the roll-preparation power, not on any new doctrine. A statement saying the Court restrained the Commission, or decided the case under Article 326, is false. Note also the contrast with 2024, when the Court struck down the electoral bonds scheme: the Commission's administrative powers were affirmed in 2026, while an opacity-creating funding scheme was struck down two years earlier. Same institution, opposite outcomes, different subject matter.

Adjacent bodies, orders and statutes

ItemWhat the setter wants
Delimitation CommissionA statutory body under a Delimitation Act — not a constitutional one. Chaired by a serving or retired Supreme Court judge, with the Chief Election Commissioner and the State Election Commissioner of the State concerned as members. Its orders have the force of law and cannot be questioned in any court.
Model Code of ConductNot statutory. It has no legal backing and rests on consensus among parties plus the Commission's moral authority. It comes into force the moment the election schedule is announced and lasts till the results. The Commission has asked for statutory status; the Standing Committee on Personnel (2013) recommended it, the Commission itself has opposed making it law, preferring speed over litigation.
Symbols Order, 1968The Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968 — the instrument under which parties are recognised and symbols reserved and allotted, and under which split and merger disputes are decided. Upheld in Sadiq Ali (1972).
Registration vs recognitionRegistration is under s.29A of the RPA, 1951 and is open to any party meeting the conditions. Recognition as a State or National party is under the Symbols Order, on vote-share and seat thresholds. Every registered party is not a recognised party — a favourite one-word trap.
NOTAIntroduced after PUCL v. Union of India (2013). It is an option on the ballot only — it does not void the election. Even if NOTA polls the highest, the candidate with the most valid votes still wins.
Electoral bondsStruck down 15 February 2024 as violating Article 19(1)(a) — the voter's right to information — and failing the proportionality test. Pair this with Art 324 as the two poles of the funding-transparency debate.
Section 126, RPA 1951The silence period — no public campaigning in the 48 hours ending with the close of poll.
Expenditure ceilingA ceiling applies to the candidate, not to the party — the gap that makes the ceiling porous. Prescribed by rules made by the Government, on the Commission's recommendation; revised to ₹95 lakh for a large-State Lok Sabha seat and ₹40 lakh for a large-State Assembly seat in 2022.
Star campaignersUnder s.77 of the RPA, 1951, travel expenditure of recognised star campaigners is not counted in the candidate's account — a recognised party may name up to 40, an unrecognised registered party up to 20.
Security depositForfeited if the candidate polls less than one-sixth of the valid votes.
NRI votings.20A of the RPA, 1950, inserted in 2010 — an overseas Indian may be enrolled, but must vote in person in the constituency. Proxy and postal voting for NRIs have been recommended and not enacted.
◆
What the Commission conspicuously does not decide — the whole family in one place. Anti-defection disqualification is decided by the Speaker or Chairman under the Tenth Schedule, not by the Commission. An election petition goes to the High Court. Local body elections belong to the State Election Commission. Delimitation is done by the Delimitation Commission, on which the CEC merely sits as a member. And the expenditure ceiling is notified by the Government, not the Commission. Four different authorities, all one step from the Commission, all standard distractors.

(A) Genuine previous-year questions

2017UPSC Prelims · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Election Commission of India is a five-member body.
  2. Union Ministry of Home Affairs decides the election schedule for the conduct of both general elections and bye-elections.
  3. Election Commission resolves the disputes relating to splits/mergers of recognised political parties.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) 2 and 3 only
  • (d) 3 only
Answer: (d) — 3 only
  • Statement 1 is wrong on the number: the Commission is a three-member body — the CEC and two Election Commissioners. Note also that the Constitution fixes no number at all.
  • Statement 2 is wrong on the authority: the Election Commission notifies the schedule, not the Ministry of Home Affairs. This is the single most reusable distractor in the chapter.
  • Statement 3 is correct and is the Commission's quasi-judicial function, from Sadiq Ali (1972).
2024UPSC Prelims · Direct

How many Delimitation Commissions have been constituted by the Government of India till December 2023?

  • (a) One
  • (b) Two
  • (c) Three
  • (d) Four
Answer: (d) — Four
  • 1952, 1963, 1973 and 2002. Learn them as a run of four dates.
  • Attach the two amendments: the 84th (2001) froze inter-State seat allocation on the 1971 census; the 87th (2003) permitted readjustment within States on the 2001 census.
  • The freeze runs until the first census taken after 2026 — which makes this a live current-affairs hook, not a dead fact.
2020UPSC Prelims · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. According to the Constitution of India, a person who is eligible to vote can be made a minister in a State for six months even if he/she is not a member of the Legislature of that State.
  2. According to the Representation of People Act, 1951, a person convicted of a criminal offence and sentenced to imprisonment for five years is permanently disqualified from contesting an election even after his release from prison.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (d) — Neither 1 nor 2
  • Statement 1 is wrong on a qualification: a non-member minister must be qualified to be a member of the legislature, which is more than being eligible to vote — the age thresholds alone differ (18 to vote, 25 for the Assembly).
  • Statement 2 is wrong on "permanently": under section 8 of the RPA, 1951 disqualification runs for the period of imprisonment plus six years from release — not for life.
  • Both failures are single-word failures. This is the qualifier family in its purest form.
2017UPSC Prelims · Direct

For election to the Lok Sabha, a nomination paper can be filed by

  • (a) anyone residing in India
  • (b) a resident of the constituency from which the election is to be contested
  • (c) any citizen of India whose name appears in the electoral roll of a constituency
  • (d) any citizen of India
Answer: (c)
  • The requirement is enrolment as an elector in some constituency — not residence in the constituency being contested, and not mere citizenship.
  • Note the contrast that follows from it: a candidate for the Lok Sabha need not be a resident of the constituency, whereas a candidate for the Rajya Sabha need not be a resident of the State either, since the domicile requirement was removed in 2003 and upheld in Kuldip Nayar (2006).

Practice · Negative

Which one of the following statements about the Election Commission of India is NOT correct?

  • (a) The Constitution does not fix the number of Election Commissioners

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (b) — 2 only
  • The Code is not statutory. It rests on party consensus and the Commission's moral authority — which is exactly why enforcement is quick but sanctions are weak.
  • Statement 2 is correct: it operates from the announcement of the schedule until the results.
  • Worth remembering the twist — the Commission itself opposed giving the Code statutory status, preferring speed to litigation. Reform proposals are not always sought by the body being reformed.
Practice · Pairs

Consider the following pairs:

  1. Registration of a political party — Section 29A, RPA 1951
  2. Recognition as a National party — Election Symbols Order, 1968
  3. Silence period of 48 hours — Section 126, RPA 1951
  4. Disqualification on defection — decided by the Election Commission

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c) — only three
  • Pairs 1, 2 and 3 are correct.
  • Pair 4 is false: defection is decided by the Speaker or Chairman under the Tenth Schedule. The Commission advises on other disqualifications of a sitting member under Arts 103/192 — and that advice binds.
  • Keep registration and recognition apart: registration is statutory and open; recognition is under the Symbols Order and threshold-based.
Practice · Negative

Which one of the following statements is NOT correct?

  • (a) NOTA was introduced following the Supreme Court's decision in PUCL (2013)
  • (b) If NOTA secures the highest number of votes, the election in that constituency is annulled
  • (c) A candidate forfeits the security deposit on polling less than one-sixth of the valid votes
  • (d) The electoral bonds scheme was struck down in February 2024
Answer: (b) is NOT correct
  • NOTA is an expressive option only. The candidate polling the most valid votes wins regardless of the NOTA count — there is no re-election trigger.
  • This is the single most common misconception about NOTA and therefore prime question material.
  • The electoral bonds strike-down rested on Art 19(1)(a), the voter's right to information, and on proportionality.

(C) Trap statements — True or False

◆ Why this bucket exists, and how to work it

Twenty-one of the fifty questions in the Week 4 paper were “how many of the above” — 42 per cent — and that format scored 52 per cent. It cannot be solved by elimination: every statement has to be judged on its own. So the skill the paper actually rewards is not answering questions, it is marking a single statement true or false.

That is all this bucket is. Twenty statements from this note, one at a time. Decide before you click. If you find yourself reasoning about how many ought to be true, stop — that instinct is what the format punishes.

Roughly half are false, and most of the false ones are wrong by one word: an authority, a number, or a qualifier. That is how this setter builds them.

Trap 1 · True or False

The Constitution fixes the number of Election Commissioners at three.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Art 324(2) leaves the number to the President. Three is the current strength, not a constitutional requirement.
Trap 2 · True or False

The Chief Election Commissioner can be removed in the same manner as a judge of the Supreme Court.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Parliamentary address by special majority, for proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The protection stops at his office alone.
Trap 3 · True or False

An Election Commissioner may be removed by the President at his discretion.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Only on the recommendation of the CEC. Dropping those six words is the standard way this statement is made false.
Trap 4 · True or False

The Chief Election Commissioner has a casting vote when the Commission is divided.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Decisions are by majority; he has no casting vote and no veto. Primus inter pares.
Trap 5 · True or False

The Election Commission conducts elections to panchayats and municipalities.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • State Election Commissions, under Arts 243K and 243ZA. The most common single error in this chapter.
Trap 6 · True or False

The Election Commission conducts elections to the offices of President and Vice-President.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Parliament, State legislatures, and the two offices. Only local bodies fall outside.
Trap 7 · True or False

The State Election Commissioner is appointed by the Chief Election Commissioner.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • By the Governor. The two commissions are institutionally unconnected — which is exactly why the setter joins them.
Trap 8 · True or False

The State Election Commissioner is removed in the manner prescribed for a High Court judge.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • High Court judge for the SEC; Supreme Court judge for the CEC. A number-with-a-twin in institutional form.
Trap 9 · True or False

The tenure of six years or 65 years of age is laid down in the Constitution.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Statutory. The Constitution prescribes no term, no qualifications and no post-retirement bar.
Trap 10 · True or False

The Constitution bars a retired Election Commissioner from further government appointment.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • No such bar exists. It is a standing criticism and a recurring reform proposal — not the present position.
Trap 11 · True or False

In Anoop Baranwal the Supreme Court included the Chief Justice of India in the selection committee.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • PM + LoP + CJI — but expressly only until Parliament made a law. True as a statement about the judgment; false as a statement about the law in force.
Trap 12 · True or False

Under the law currently in force, the Chief Justice of India sits on the committee that selects the CEC.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • The CEC Act, 2023 replaced the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister. Traps 11 and 12 are the same fact from opposite ends — read which one the stem is asking about.
Trap 13 · True or False

Expenditure of the Election Commission is charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Charged, therefore non-votable — one of the three constitutional independence safeguards.
Trap 14 · True or False

An election petition challenging a completed election is decided by the Election Commission.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • By the High Court, with appeal to the Supreme Court. Art 329 bars every other route of court interference.
Trap 15 · True or False

Disqualification on the ground of defection is decided by the Election Commission.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Speaker or Chairman, Tenth Schedule. The Commission advises on other disqualifications under Arts 103/192, and there its opinion binds.
Trap 16 · True or False

The Model Code of Conduct has statutory backing.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Consensus and moral authority only. Its speed is the reason the Commission has resisted statutory status.
Trap 17 · True or False

Four Delimitation Commissions have been constituted so far.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • 1952, 1963, 1973, 2002 — asked directly in Prelims 2024.
Trap 18 · True or False

The 84th Amendment froze the allocation of Lok Sabha seats among States on the basis of the 2001 census.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • The freeze is on the 1971 census. The 2001 census governs readjustment within States under the 87th Amendment. Two censuses, two amendments, one swap — a textbook number-with-a-twin.
Trap 19 · True or False

Every registered political party is a recognised political party.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Registration is under s.29A of the RPA, 1951; recognition is under the Symbols Order, 1968 and depends on vote and seat thresholds.
  • Watch for the absolute “every” — it is the signal to check for a carve-out.
Trap 20 · True or False

In 2026 the Supreme Court upheld the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Association for Democratic Reforms v. ECI, 27 May 2026 — within the Commission's mandate under the RPA 1950 and Art 324.

M47Practice · Week 615 marks · 250 words

"The independence of the Election Commission rests on one man's security of tenure and the goodwill of the executive." Critically examine in the light of recent legislative and judicial developments.

Directive: "Critically examine." Test the claim. It is largely true, and the marks lie in showing why — and in what the 2023 Act changed.
Skeleton
  1. What the Constitution guarantees
  2. The asymmetry
  3. The appointment question, 2023 to now
  4. Reforms
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Article 324 vests superintendence, direction and control of elections in the Commission, and surrounds it with three protections: removal of the CEC only by the process applicable to a Supreme Court judge; service conditions that cannot be varied to disadvantage; and expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund.

The asymmetry

The guarantee stops at one office. The two Election Commissioners — who hold an equal vote, since the CEC has no casting vote — may be removed by the President on the CEC's recommendation. Equal power, unequal protection. The 2nd ARC and the Law Commission's 255th Report (2015) both recommended extending the CEC's protection to all three.

Appointment

Article 324(2) always made appointment "subject to any law made by Parliament", and for seventy-three years no such law existed, so the executive chose the umpire. In Anoop Baranwal (2023) a Constitution Bench filled the vacuum with a committee of the PM, the Leader of the Opposition and the CJI — expressly until Parliament legislated. The CEC Act, 2023 then legislated, replacing the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister and restoring an executive majority of two to one. The Act is under challenge; the Court declined to stay it before the 2024 general election.

Reform

Three measures follow: extend the CEC's removal protection to all Commissioners; restore a neutral third member to the selection committee; and bar post-retirement office, which the Constitution presently does not.

The proposition holds. The Commission's independence is real but narrow and personal — attached to one officer and, since 2023, to a committee the executive controls. That is a fragile foundation for a referee.

Value additions used: Art 324 with all three safeguards · the no-casting-vote point · Anoop Baranwal (2 March 2023) · CEC Act, 2023 and the 2:1 committee · 2nd ARC and Law Commission 255th Report · the absence of a post-retirement bar.

Bought: the "equal power, unequal protection" formulation and the fact that the Court's remedy was expressly interim, which is what explains how a judgment was superseded so quickly without being overruled.
What would cost you marks: writing that the Supreme Court's committee is the law today — it is not, and that single error dates the whole answer; and listing the Commission's functions, which the question does not ask about.

Last-mile revision — Election Commission

  • Art 324 — superintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament, State legislatures, President and Vice-President. Not local bodies.
  • Composition: CEC + such number as the President may fix. Currently three. Multi-member since 1 Oct 1993; briefly in 1989–90. Decisions by majority; CEC has no casting vote.
  • Tenure: 6 years or 65, whichever earlier — statutory, not constitutional. No constitutional qualifications, no post-retirement bar.
  • Removal: CEC like a Supreme Court judge. Other ECs by the President on the CEC's recommendation. State Election Commissioner like a High Court judge.
  • Anoop Baranwal (2 Mar 2023) — PM + LoP + CJI, until Parliament legislates. CEC Act 2023 — PM + LoP + Union Cabinet Minister; Search Committee under the Cabinet Secretary shortlists five. Act under challenge; Gyanesh Kumar, 26th CEC, from 19 Feb 2025, is the first appointed under it.
  • Functions: administrative (rolls, schedule, symbols, recognition, Model Code) · advisory (disqualification of a sitting member — binding on President/Governor) · quasi-judicial (splits and mergers — Sadiq Ali, 1972).
  • Election petitions go to the High Court, not the Commission — Art 329 bars other court interference.
  • Art 325 one general roll, no exclusion on religion/race/caste/sex · Art 326 adult suffrage, 18 since the 61st Amendment, 1988 · 327 Parliament · 328 State legislature.
  • RPA 1950 — rolls, seats, delimitation. RPA 1951 — conduct, disqualifications, corrupt practices, election disputes; s.29A registration of parties; s.8 disqualification = imprisonment + 6 years, not permanent.
  • Delimitation Commissions: four — 1952, 1963, 1973, 2002. Orders have force of law, not questionable in any court. 84th Amdt (2001) froze seats on the 1971 census; 87th Amdt (2003) allowed intra-State readjustment on 2001. Freeze until the first census after 2026.
  • State Election Commission — Arts 243K / 243ZA, from the 73rd and 74th Amendments; Commissioner appointed by the Governor, removed like a High Court judge.
