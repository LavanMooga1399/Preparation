Week 1 · Topic 1 — Indian Constitution
M12019 · GS-II10 marks · 150 words

What is meant by the term 'constitutional morality'? How does one uphold constitutional morality?

Directive: "What is meant… / How does one…" — a two-part explain question, not a debate. Part 1 demands a precise definition with its origin; Part 2 demands concrete mechanisms. Splitting the answer physically into these two halves is itself a scoring move.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Grote's origin + Ambedkar's adoption
  2. What it means — four elements
  3. How it is upheld — institutions, judiciary, citizens
  4. Conclusion: Ambedkar's "has to be cultivated"

Model answer

Constitutional morality means adherence to the spirit and core values of the Constitution, not mere obedience to its text. Coined by George Grote (1846) and adopted by Ambedkar on 4 November 1948, it lay dormant for decades, was revived in Naz Foundation (2009), and matured in Navtej Singh Johar, Sabarimala and Joseph Shine (all 2018).

Its content

Constitutional supremacy over transient majorities; due process and institutional form; individual dignity against majoritarian pressure; and the primacy of constitutional over social morality — the Navtej formulation used to read down Section 377.

How it is upheld

By courts, striking down majoritarian practice and testing political conduct against it (Manoj Narula, 2014); by institutions observing constitutional forms — free elections, legislative deliberation, an independent CAG and Election Commission; by the executive exercising discretion within constitutional limits rather than political convenience; and by citizens, through Article 51A duties and constitutional literacy.

Ambedkar's caution is the centre of the answer: constitutional morality "is not a natural sentiment; it has to be cultivated."

Value additions used: Grote (1846) · Ambedkar 4 Nov 1948 · the dormancy→revival arc · Naz Foundation (2009) · Navtej Singh Johar / Sabarimala / Joseph Shine (2018) · Manoj Narula (2014) · Article 51A · the "cultivated" quotation.
Budget check: 165 / 150 words. Cut: the separate closing paragraph ("a Constitution can only be as good as those who work it") and the one-line description attached to each 2018 judgment. Bought: the dormancy→revival arc in the opening line, and Manoj Narula (2014) — which extends the doctrine from rights cases to political conduct.
What would cost you marks: attributing the phrase to Ambedkar rather than Grote (the single most common error here); answering only "what it means" and neglecting the "how to uphold" half; listing cases without stating what each held.
M22021 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

'Constitutional Morality' is rooted in the Constitution itself and is founded on its essential facets. Explain the doctrine of 'Constitutional Morality' with the help of relevant judicial decisions.

Directive: "Explain… with the help of relevant judicial decisions." Explain = clarify with detail and illustration; it is not a debate. But the tail imposes a hard requirement: the answer must be case-led. An answer without named judgments cannot score, however well written.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: rooted in Preamble + Parts III/IV
  2. Essential facets it draws on
  3. Judicial evolution — four cases with holdings
  4. The critique (adds balance)
  5. Conclusion: cultivated, not assumed

Model answer

Constitutional morality is the principle that governance must conform to the Constitution's underlying values, not merely its letter. It is rooted in the document itself — in the Preamble's promise of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, in the rights of Part III (notably Articles 14, 15 and 21), and in the goals of Part IV.

Essential facets it rests on

Constitutional supremacy; rule of law; due process; individual dignity; institutional trust; and the subordination of popular sentiment to constitutional guarantees.

The doctrinal arc

Coined by Grote (1846) and invoked by Ambedkar (4 November 1948), the doctrine lay unused until Naz Foundation (2009) revived it. Manoj Narula (2014) applied it to the criminalisation of politics; Government of NCT of Delhi (2018) converted it into "constitutional trust" governing Centre–State conduct; Navtej Singh Johar (2018) held constitutional morality prevails over social morality while reading down Section 377; Sabarimala (2018) deployed it against religious exclusion; Joseph Shine (2018) grounded the striking down of Section 497.

The competing view

Critics call the doctrine vague and judge-defined: no text fixes its content, so each bench may pour in its own values — an objection pressed by governments in the Sabarimala review. Defenders reply that all open-textured constitutional concepts work this way, and that its record is one of protecting rights against majoritarian pressure, which is precisely the counter-majoritarian function courts exist to perform.

Its legitimacy will therefore depend on principled, restrained use. As Ambedkar warned, constitutional morality "has to be cultivated" — by institutions as much as by citizens.

Value additions used: Preamble + Parts III/IV anchoring with Arts 14, 15, 21 · Grote (1846) · Ambedkar 4 Nov 1948 · Naz Foundation (2009) · Manoj Narula (2014) · Govt of NCT of Delhi (2018) "constitutional trust" · Navtej / Sabarimala / Joseph Shine (2018) · the vagueness critique · Ambedkar quotation.
Budget check: 251 / 250 words. Cut: the procedural detail of which court decided Naz, and loose connectives in the critique paragraph. Bought: the doctrine's origin (Grote, Ambedkar) so the arc is complete; Govt of NCT of Delhi (2018), which extends constitutional morality into federal conduct; and clause-level Article anchoring (14, 15, 21).
What would cost you marks: naming cases without their holdings; omitting the critique — a 15-mark "explain" answer with zero balance reads thin; forgetting to show the doctrine is rooted in the Constitution, which is the exact claim the question puts to you.
M32015 · GS-I12 marks · 200 words

It would have been difficult for the Constituent Assembly to complete its historic task of drafting the Constitution for Independent India in just three years but for the experience gained with the Government of India Act, 1935. Discuss.

Directive: "Discuss." A reasoned debate ending in a position. The statement is a claim — your job is to test it, not simply agree. So: evidence for the claim, then the significant qualification, then a verdict.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: the timeline fact (2 yrs 11 mths 18 days)
  2. What the 1935 Act supplied — five borrowings
  3. What it could not supply — the democratic core
  4. Verdict: scaffolding, not blueprint

Model answer

The Constituent Assembly framed a 395-article Constitution in 2 years, 11 months and 18 days. That speed is inexplicable without the administrative template already supplied by the Government of India Act, 1935, from which roughly two-thirds of the Constitution's structure derives.

What the 1935 Act supplied

The federal scheme with its three-fold division of powers, carried into Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule; provincial autonomy, matured into responsible government at both levels; the Federal Court, reconstituted as the Supreme Court under Article 124; the office of Governor; emergency provisions; and the public service commissions. Much drafting was therefore adaptation, not invention.

What it could not supply

The 1935 Act was a colonial statute without a soul: no Fundamental Rights, no Directive Principles, no universal adult franchise, no popular sovereignty. Those came from the Objectives Resolution (13 December 1946) and the Assembly's own deliberation. The speed also owed much to B.N. Rau's draft, the committee system, and Congress dominance enabling consensus.

The claim is therefore largely valid but incomplete. The 1935 Act was scaffolding that saved time on machinery; the Assembly supplied the democratic and rights-based architecture that made the document a Constitution rather than an administrative code — as Ambedkar replied to the "slavish imitation" charge, "nobody holds any patent rights in the fundamental ideas of a Constitution."

Value additions used: 2y 11m 18d · Art 246 + Seventh Schedule · Art 124 · Objectives Resolution 13 Dec 1946 · B.N. Rau draft · committee system · Ambedkar's patent-rights reply.
Budget check: 217 / 200 words. Cut: provincial bicameralism from the borrowings list, and the standalone sentence introducing the alternative explanations for the Assembly's speed. Bought: the exact date of the Objectives Resolution, and tighter phrasing that lets the "scaffolding vs architecture" verdict land as one clean sentence.
What would cost you marks: agreeing with the statement uncritically — "discuss" requires the counter-side; listing 1935 Act features without connecting each to a present-day Article; ignoring the other reasons for the speed.
M4Practice · Week 1 test10 marks · 150 words

How far does the Constitution of India owe its genesis to the Government of India Act, 1935? Discuss.

Directive: "How far… Discuss." A degree question. It must not end in yes or no — it must end in how much. Structure: the extent it is true → the extent it is not → the net degree stated in words.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: the ~two-thirds claim
  2. Extent of the debt — structural borrowings
  3. Limits of the debt — what 1935 lacked
  4. Verdict stated as a degree

Model answer

The Government of India Act, 1935 supplies roughly two-thirds of the Constitution's structure — a substantial but not total debt.

The extent of the debt

Structurally it is large: the federal scheme and three-fold division of powers (Article 246, Seventh Schedule); provincial autonomy; the Federal Court, reconstituted as the Supreme Court (Article 124); the office of Governor; emergency provisions; and the public service commissions.

The limits of the debt

Philosophically it is small. The Act had no Fundamental Rights, no Directive Principles, no universal adult franchise and no popular sovereignty; it was controlled devolution, not self-government. Those came from the Objectives Resolution (1946). Other features were borrowed elsewhere — Fundamental Rights from the USA, Directive Principles from Ireland, parliamentary government from Britain, suspension of rights during emergency from the Weimar Constitution.

The genesis is therefore substantially but not wholly traceable to 1935: the Act supplied the machinery, the Assembly the mandate. Ambedkar's defence stands — borrowing administrative form is legitimate, since "nobody holds any patent rights in the fundamental ideas of a Constitution."

Value additions used: two-thirds figure · Art 246 + Seventh Schedule · Art 124 · Objectives Resolution · borrowing sources (USA / Ireland / Britain / Weimar) · Ambedkar quotation.
Budget check: 170 / 150 words. Cut: the repeated "administrative vocabulary of Indian governance" phrase and the restated list of what the Assembly added. Bought: the Weimar source for emergency suspension of rights — a fourth borrowing source at the cost of six words.
What would cost you marks: ending with "yes, it owes its genesis" instead of a stated degree; writing the same content as M3 without adapting to the shorter word limit — at 150 words you must compress, not truncate.
M5Practice · Week 1 test10 marks · 150 words

When is Constitution Day celebrated in India? Elaborate the historical underpinnings of the Indian Constitution and their influence on the evolution of the Constitution.

Directive: two-part — a factual "when", then "elaborate". Elaborate = give a detailed account. The factual half must be answered in one line and precisely; the marks are all in the second half. Do not let the first half consume space.
Skeleton
  1. Date + why that date
  2. Colonial-statute lineage
  3. Nationalist lineage
  4. Influence on the final text

Model answer

Constitution Day (Samvidhan Divas) is observed on 26 November, marking the Assembly's adoption of the Constitution in 1949; it has been formally celebrated since 2015. Commencement followed separately on 26 January 1950.

Colonial-statute lineage

Regulating Act (1773) → Pitt's India Act (1784) → Charter Acts (1813–1853) → Indian Councils Acts (1861–1909) → Montagu–Chelmsford reforms (1919) introducing provincial dyarchy → Government of India Act, 1935, the direct template for federalism, provincial autonomy and the Federal Court.

Nationalist lineage

Nehru Report (1928) with its rights charter → Karachi Resolution (1931) on fundamental and economic rights → Cabinet Mission Plan (1946), under which the Assembly was constituted → Objectives Resolution (13 December 1946), which became the Preamble almost unchanged.

Their combined influence

The colonial statutes supplied administrative machinery; the nationalist stream supplied purpose — Fundamental Rights, universal adult franchise, and sovereignty vested in the people. The Constitution's character as a borrowed structure with an indigenous soul is the direct product of these two lineages meeting in 1946–49.

Value additions used: 26 Nov 1949 vs 26 Jan 1950 distinction · full statute chronology as an arrow-chain · Nehru Report 1928 · Karachi Resolution 1931 · Cabinet Mission 1946 · Objectives Resolution 13 Dec 1946 → Preamble.
Budget check: 155 / 150 words. Cut: prose connectives between the statutes — both lineages are now arrow-chains, which is faster to read and faster to write in the hall. Bought: the subject-matter of the Karachi Resolution (fundamental and economic rights), and the space to state the two-lineage verdict as its own sentence.
What would cost you marks: confusing adoption (26 Nov 1949) with commencement (26 Jan 1950); listing Acts as a bare chronology without saying what each contributed; forgetting the nationalist stream entirely — most answers cover only the colonial one.
M6Practice · Week 1 test10 marks · 150 words

"The Preamble is the manifestation of the basic fundamental values and philosophy on which the entire Constitution of India has been built." Discuss.

Directive: "Discuss" a quoted claim. Test it rather than merely endorse it. The strongest structure: how the claim is true (the values), how it is legally real (the case law), and the qualification (non-justiciability) — then a verdict.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: source in the Objectives Resolution
  2. The values it declares
  3. Its legal status — Berubari → Kesavananda → 2024
  4. The limitation — not enforceable
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Based on the Objectives Resolution (13 December 1946), the Preamble is the Constitution's own summary of itself — K.M. Munshi's "horoscope of our sovereign democratic republic."

The values it declares

Source of authority ("We, the People"); nature of the State — Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic; and the objectives of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. Each is operationalised elsewhere: Part III delivers liberty and equality, Part IV social and economic justice, Article 51A fraternity.

Its legal status — the arc

Berubari Union (1960) held the Preamble was not part of the Constitution; Kesavananda Bharati (1973) reversed this, holding it part of the Constitution and reflective of the basic structure; LIC of India (1995) reaffirmed it; Dr. Balram Singh (2024) upheld the 42nd Amendment's insertion of "socialist" and "secular", confirming the Preamble is amendable yet substantively protected.

The qualification

It is not directly enforceable and confers no power; its force is interpretive. The claim nonetheless holds — the Preamble is not decorative but the declared philosophy the rest of the document operationalises.

Value additions used: Objectives Resolution · Munshi's "horoscope" · four-component structure · the full arc Berubari (1960) → Kesavananda (1973) → LIC (1995) → Balram Singh (2024) · non-justiciability.
Budget check: 167 / 150 words. Cut: the "date of adoption" component and the expanded parenthetical on justice (social, economic, political). Bought: labelling the judicial sequence explicitly as the arc, and one clean interpretive-force sentence in place of two.
What would cost you marks: reproducing the Preamble's text instead of analysing it; omitting the Berubari→Kesavananda reversal, which is the single most examinable fact here; forgetting the non-enforceability qualification.
M72015 · GS-II12 marks · 200 words

The concept of cooperative federalism has been increasingly emphasised in recent years. Highlight the drawbacks in the existing structure and the extent to which cooperative federalism would answer the shortcomings.

Directive: "Highlight… and the extent to which…" Two demands: identify drawbacks, then deliver a degree judgement on whether cooperative federalism cures them. The second half is where most answers under-perform — they praise cooperative federalism instead of testing it.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: definition + why emphasised
  2. Drawbacks — legislative, administrative, fiscal
  3. What cooperative federalism cures
  4. What it cannot cure
  5. Degree verdict + way forward

Model answer

Cooperative federalism means Centre and States acting as partners in a common national enterprise rather than as rival power centres — an emphasis strengthened by GST, NITI Aayog and the pandemic response.

Drawbacks in the existing structure

Legislative: Union dominance through Articles 249, 250 and 253, and the repugnancy rule in Article 254. Administrative: misuse of Article 356 — checked but not eliminated by S.R. Bommai (1994) — and friction over the Governor's office, most recently the Bill-assent litigation of 2025. Fiscal: dependence on Union transfers, a shrinking divisible pool as cesses and surcharges rise, and borrowing limits under Article 293, now contested in Kerala's Article 131 suit.

What cooperative federalism answers

The GST Council (Article 279A) has delivered consensus-based tax decisions; the Inter-State Council (Article 263) and Zonal Councils enable dialogue; NITI Aayog replaced directive planning with consultation.

What it cannot answer

It is a convention, not a constitutional guarantee. In Mohit Minerals (2022) the Court held GST Council recommendations non-binding, describing Indian federalism as "a dialogue between cooperative and uncooperative federalism." Structural imbalance in the Seventh Schedule, and Article 356 itself, survive untouched. Durable reform needs the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commission proposals on the Governor and a transparent, formula-based fiscal transfer.

Value additions used: Arts 249 / 250 / 253 / 254 / 356 / 293 / 279A / 263 / 131 · Bommai (1994) · Mohit Minerals (2022) + its quotable line · Kerala's pending Art 131 suit · cess-and-surcharge issue · Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions.
Budget check: 200 / 200 words. Cut: the "institutional fora do resolve real friction" preamble and the separate mitigate-not-resolve sentence, which the final line already carries. Bought: the Sarkaria Commission alongside Punchhi — two named commissions for four words, and the pair is what examiners expect on Centre–State reform.
What would cost you marks: treating cooperative federalism as an unqualified good — the question explicitly asks "to what extent"; covering only fiscal drawbacks and omitting the legislative and administrative limbs.
M82013 · GS-II10 marks · 150 words

Many State Governments further bifurcate geographical administrative areas like Districts and Talukas for better governance. In light of the above, can it also be justified that more number of smaller States would bring in effective governance at State level? Discuss.

Directive: "Can it be justified… Discuss." A both-sides question with an analogy embedded in it. Crucially: the analogy between districts and States is imperfect, and saying so is a high-value insight — administrative sub-division is not the same as creating a political unit.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 3 makes it constitutionally easy
  2. Case for smaller States
  3. Case against
  4. Why the analogy is imperfect
  5. Verdict: criteria, not size

Model answer

Article 3 lets Parliament create new States by simple majority, the State legislature's views being merely consultative — so this is a question of policy, not constitutional difficulty.

The case for

Shorter administrative distances and better last-mile delivery; homogeneous units easing planning; closer accountability; redress of regional neglect. Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand (2000) saw improved growth after separation.

The case against

Fixed costs of new capitals, secretariats and High Courts; non-viable units dependent on Union transfers; disputes over water, capital and cadre, as Andhra Pradesh–Telangana (2014) showed; and encouragement of sub-regional agitations. The States Reorganisation Commission (Fazl Ali, 1955) warned against language or size as a sole criterion, insisting on administrative viability and national unity.

Why the analogy is imperfect

Bifurcating a district creates no new political tier; creating a State creates a legislature, executive, judiciary and a fiscal claim on the Union — so efficiency arguments do not transfer automatically. Smaller States are therefore justifiable case by case, best decided by a permanent, criteria-based Second States Reorganisation Commission.

Value additions used: Article 3 mechanics (simple majority, consultative State view) · the 2000 trio · Telangana 2014 disputes · Fazl Ali SRC 1955 criteria · administrative-vs-political distinction · second SRC as the way forward.
Budget check: 167 / 150 words. Cut: the standalone verdict sentence — it is now folded into the closing line of the last block. Bought: nothing new; this answer was already well-cited. The whole trade was length: 205 → budget, which is what actually costs marks in a 10-marker.
What would cost you marks: accepting the analogy uncritically — spotting its limit is the differentiating insight; giving no concrete State examples; concluding with a flat yes or no rather than a criteria-based position.
M9Practice · Week 1 test15 marks · 250 words

What is the controversy behind the Citizenship Amendment Act? Can the policy gain a wider ground of reconciliation with the NPR/NRC project?

Directive: two-part — describe the controversy, then a forward-looking judgement. Part 2 ("can it gain wider reconciliation") invites a reform-oriented answer. This is a politically sensitive topic: the answer must be constitutionally framed and even-handed, arguing through Article 14 rather than taking a political side.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: what CAA does, precisely
  2. The case for it
  3. The constitutional objections
  4. The NPR/NRC interaction
  5. Way forward toward reconciliation

Model answer

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 exempts Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian migrants from Pakistan, Afghanistan and Bangladesh who entered on or before 31 December 2014 from being treated as "illegal migrants", fast-tracking naturalisation through the new Section 6B of the Citizenship Act, 1955. Rules were notified in March 2024; challenges remain pending before the Supreme Court.

The case for it

A humanitarian route for religious minorities in three neighbouring states with a constitutionally established majority faith; it alters no existing citizen's status.

The constitutional objections

First, it is the first Indian citizenship law to use religion as an express criterion, raising an Article 14 question: reasonable classification requires an intelligible differentia with a rational nexus to the object. If the object is protecting the persecuted, why exclude Ahmadiyyas and Shias of Pakistan, Rohingyas of Myanmar, or Sri Lankan Tamils? Second, secularism is part of the basic structure (S.R. Bommai, 1994). Third, the North-East objects on demographic and Assam Accord grounds — the Accord's cut-off being 25 March 1971, upheld in In re Section 6A (2024).

The NPR/NRC interaction

Fear arises from the combination: a nationwide NRC would place the burden of proving citizenship on individuals, while CAA offers a religiously selective safety net for those excluded. That asymmetry, more than either measure alone, drove the protests.

Toward reconciliation

Recast the criterion from a religion list to demonstrated persecution, applied neutrally; decouple CAA from any nationwide NRC by explicit statement; guarantee Sixth Schedule and Inner Line Permit safeguards; and await the Court's ruling on Article 14. The objective — protecting the persecuted — is legitimate; the classification is what must be repaired.

Value additions used: CAA cut-off + Section 6B + March 2024 Rules · Art 14 classification test · Bommai (1994) on secularism · Assam Accord 25 March 1971 · In re Section 6A (2024) · Sixth Schedule / ILP · persecution-based redraft.
Budget check: 270 / 250 words. Cut: "persecution-based citizenship routes exist worldwide" (a claim without a citation), and the reconciliation block's four full sentences, now compressed into one imperative chain. Bought: Section 6B — the actual provision inserted, which almost no candidate names, at a cost of eight words.
What would cost you marks: writing politically rather than constitutionally; omitting the Article 14 classification test, which is the controversy; ignoring the second half of the question about reconciliation.
M10Practice15 marks · 250 words

"The Indian amending process is neither as rigid as the American nor as flexible as the British — and that middle path has been both its strength and its vulnerability." Discuss with reference to Ambedkar's defence of Article 368 and the 42nd–44th Amendment cycle.

Directive: "Discuss" with two named anchors. The tail names what must appear — CA debate material and the 42nd/44th cycle. Both must be present. The quoted claim has a two-sided shape ("strength and vulnerability") which dictates the body's two blocks.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: the three-tier design
  2. Ambedkar's defence — the strength
  3. The 42nd Amendment — the vulnerability
  4. The correction — 44th + judicial brake
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Article 368 creates a three-tier amending process — simple majority for some provisions, special majority for most, and special majority plus ratification by half the States for federal provisions. It deliberately avoids both American rigidity (three-fourths of States) and British flexibility (an ordinary law).

The strength: Ambedkar's defence

Answering critics in the Assembly, Ambedkar argued the founders must not bind posterity — future generations should be able to correct their errors without revolution. The record vindicates this: over a hundred amendments have absorbed profound change, including the 73rd and 74th creating local self-government, the 101st enabling GST, and the 106th reserving one-third of seats for women.

The vulnerability: the 42nd Amendment

The same accessibility was turned against the Constitution. The 42nd Amendment (1976), enacted during the Emergency, declared Parliament's amending power unlimited, barred judicial review of amendments, subordinated Fundamental Rights to all Directive Principles, and extended the Lok Sabha's term. Flexibility designed for correction became an instrument of concentration.

The correction, from two directions

Textually, the 44th Amendment (1978) restored the term, replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion", and protected Articles 20 and 21 from suspension. Doctrinally, the judiciary supplied the brake the text omitted: Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda Bharati (1973), establishing the basic structure → Minerva Mills (1980), which struck down the 42nd's clauses, holding limited amending power and judicial review to be themselves basic features.

The middle path is therefore vindicated — but only because the judiciary supplied a limit the text omitted. Its safety rests on the basic structure doctrine, not on Article 368's own words.

Value additions used: three-tier design · Ambedkar's posterity argument · 73rd / 74th / 101st / 106th as evidence of useful flexibility · 42nd Amendment specifics · 44th Amendment repairs · the arc Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980) · Arts 20 and 21.
Budget check: 254 / 250 words. Cut: the Canada/Australia comparison and the "repair came from two directions" set-up sentence, now carried by the sub-heading itself. Bought: Golaknath (1967) at the head of the doctrinal arc — the case that made Kesavananda necessary, and the standard marker of a candidate who knows the sequence rather than the destination.
What would cost you marks: covering only the 42nd Amendment and omitting Ambedkar's defence, or vice versa — the tail demands both; failing to state the conclusion's real insight, that the safeguard is judicial rather than textual.
M112017 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

Critically examine the Supreme Court's judgement on the 'National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014' with reference to the appointment of judges of higher judiciary in India.

Directive: "Critically examine." Deconstruct the judgment and deliver a balanced verdict. "Critically" is compulsory here — an answer that only praises or only attacks the verdict is capped. You must argue both the majority's reasoning and the dissent's.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: name it the Fourth Judges Case + the core tension
  2. The doctrinal lineage in one line
  3. Why the Court struck it down
  4. The case against the judgment
  5. Balanced verdict + way forward

Model answer — revised after examiner-style review; see the note below

The NJAC judgment — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015), the Fourth Judges Case — tested the constitutional balance between judicial independence and democratic accountability in appointments to the higher judiciary under Articles 124 and 217.

The doctrinal lineage

Article 124 requires only "consultation" with the CJI. S.P. Gupta (First Judges Case, 1981) read this as executive primacy; the Second (1993) and Third (1998) Judges Cases converted "consultation" into "concurrence", creating the collegium. The 99th Amendment and NJAC Act, 2014 — passed near-unanimously in Parliament and ratified by over half the State legislatures — replaced it with a six-member commission including the Law Minister and two eminent persons.

Why the Court struck it down (4:1)

The majority held that judicial primacy in appointments is part of the basic structure, because an independent judiciary is indispensable to judicial review and constitutional supremacy — reinforced by Article 50's directive to separate the judiciary from the executive. Fatally, the veto rule let any two members block a recommendation, enabling non-judicial members to override the judges.

The case against the judgment

Justice Chelameswar's dissent held the collegium opaque and unaccountable, and nowhere in the text. Critics add that primacy is not exclusivity; that a plural commission was recommended by the 121st Law Commission Report (1987) and the Venkatachaliah Commission (NCRWC, 2002); and that invalidating a duly ratified amendment strains separation of powers and democratic legitimacy.

Verdict

The judgment was right in principle but incomplete in effect — it protected independence but restored an unreformed collegium, leaving the Memorandum of Procedure unfinalised a decade later. The way forward lies in reform within judicial primacy: a settled MoP, published criteria, a permanent secretariat and recorded reasons — securing accountability without surrendering independence.

Value additions used: Fourth Judges Case label · Arts 124, 217, 50 · full lineage S.P. Gupta (1981) → Second (1993) → Third (1998) → Fourth (2015) · consultation→concurrence shift · ratification by half the States · basic structure ground · veto rule · Chelameswar dissent · 121st Law Commission (1987) · Venkatachaliah/NCRWC (2002) · MoP.

Most of that was right in substance but impossible in arithmetic: this answer is capped at 250 words and 7½ minutes. So nothing was simply "added" — content was traded. Cut: the full NJAC composition list, the "every major democracy involves the executive" line, and the long restatement of the collegium's opacity. Bought with that space: the Fourth Judges Case label, the four-case lineage, Articles 217 and 50, the consultation→concurrence shift, the ratification fact, and the two reform commissions. Same length, denser marks. The transferable lesson: value addition is substitution under a fixed budget, never accumulation — any advice to "add more" must be paid for by cutting something weaker, or it costs you the argument itself.
What would cost you marks: describing the judgment without critiquing it — "critically" is not decorative; omitting the dissent, which is where the balance lives; failing to note the reform promise that was never delivered.
M122017 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

Examine the scope of Fundamental Rights in the light of the latest judgement of the Supreme Court on the Right to Privacy.

Directive: "Examine." Break the subject into parts and inspect each — here, break "scope" into its dimensions and show how Puttaswamy altered each. Not a debate; an analytical dissection ending in an assessment.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Puttaswamy's holding
  2. How it widened scope — four dimensions
  3. The limits it simultaneously imposed
  4. Consequences since
  5. Assessment

Model answer

In Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), a nine-judge bench unanimously held the right to privacy to be a fundamental right intrinsic to Article 21 and to the freedoms of Part III. It closed a long arc: A.K. Gopalan (1950) read Article 21 narrowly; M.P. Sharma (1954) and Kharak Singh (1962) denied a privacy right; Maneka Gandhi (1978) made Article 21 substantive. Both earlier rulings were overruled.

"Judicial review has narrowed the Governor's discretion more than any constitutional amendment has." Examine with reference to S.R. Bommai (1994) and Nabam Rebia (2016).

Directive: "Examine" a comparative claim. The comparison is the point: judiciary versus amendment. So the answer must show what courts did AND what amendments did not do — otherwise the comparison is never actually made.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 163's textual discretion
  2. What judicial review narrowed
  3. What amendments did not do
  4. The limits of judicial narrowing
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Article 163 requires the Governor to act on ministerial advice "except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions in his discretion" — deliberately open-ended language that the text has never since narrowed.

What judicial review narrowed

S.R. Bommai (1994), nine judges, transformed Article 356: majority must be tested on the floor of the House, not by the Governor's subjective assessment; a proclamation is justiciable and may be struck down for mala fides or irrelevant grounds; and a dissolved Assembly can be revived. Rameshwar Prasad (2006) applied this, holding the Bihar dissolution on the Governor's report unconstitutional. Nabam Rebia (2016) narrowed a different power — summoning, prorogation and advancing a session are ordinarily exercised on ministerial advice, and intra-party dissidence is no ground for unilateral action. The 2025 assent litigation kept indefinite inaction on Bills judicially examinable, even after the Constitution Bench declined to fix timelines.

What amendments did not do

Article 163 stands unamended. Article 156 still holds the Governor in office "during the pleasure of the President" — removable at will, without cause or process. The Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions recommended fixed tenure, removal only for proven misconduct, and consultation with the Chief Minister before appointment. None has been enacted.

The limits of judicial narrowing

Courts act only after the event, and remedies are often hollow — in Rameshwar Prasad the dissolution was declared mala fide, yet the Assembly was not revived, elections having been notified.

The claim therefore holds: every meaningful restraint on the Governor has come from the bench, not from Parliament. But judicial narrowing is remedial and episodic; a structural fix still requires the constitutional amendment that has never come.

M25Practice10 marks · 150 words

Distinguish between collective and individual ministerial responsibility in the Indian parliamentary system, and explain why the distinction matters for Cabinet decision-making discipline.

Directive: "Distinguish… and explain why it matters." Two demands. The distinction is straightforward; the marks are in the second half — the consequence for how Cabinet actually functions. Most answers stop after the distinction.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: both in Art 75
  2. Collective — to whom, effect
  3. Individual — mechanism
  4. Why it matters — the discipline
  5. Close: no legal responsibility

Model answer

Both principles sit in Article 75 but do different work.

Collective

Under Article 75(3) the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha alone — not to the Rajya Sabha, and not to the President. A successful no-confidence motion brings down the entire Council, including Ministers of State and Deputy Ministers.

Individual

Under Article 75(2) Ministers hold office during the President's pleasure, exercised on the Prime Minister's advice, so one Minister may be dropped for personal or departmental failure without the government falling. The limit: the Lok Sabha cannot remove an individual Minister; it can act only against the Council as a whole.

Why it matters

Together they produce Cabinet discipline. Because all sink together, dissent must be voiced inside Cabinet and abandoned outside it — a Minister who cannot accept a collective decision is expected to resign. Because the Prime Minister can have any individual removed, that expectation is enforceable. Collective responsibility supplies the norm; individual responsibility supplies the sanction. Unlike Britain, India has no legal ministerial responsibility: no countersignature is required, and Article 74(2) bars courts from examining the advice tendered.

M262020 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

"Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker"! Do you think this practice should be adopted to impart objectivity to the office of the Speaker of Lok Sabha? What could be its implications for the robust functioning of parliamentary business in India?

Directive: "Do you think… What could be its implications." An opinion question with a mandatory consequence-analysis. You must take a position — but a qualified, reasoned one. The implications half must cover both positive and negative consequences.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: what the British convention is
  2. Why reform is sought — the problem
  3. Implications in favour
  4. Implications against
  5. Qualified position + phased reform

Model answer

The phrase describes the British convention under which the Speaker resigns party membership on election, is returned unopposed at subsequent elections, and remains politically neutral for life. India adopted the office but not the convention.

The problem it addresses

The Indian Speaker remains a party politician who must seek re-election on a party ticket, yet exercises powers demanding strict neutrality: certifying a Money Bill under Article 110(3) — a certificate whose reviewability is pending before a seven-judge bench after Rojer Mathew (2019) — deciding disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, and regulating debate and discipline. Kihoto Hollohan (1992) made defection decisions judicially reviewable; Keisham Meghachandra (2020) criticised prolonged inaction and urged Parliament to consider transferring defection cases to an independent tribunal.

Implications in favour

Greater objectivity in defection and Money Bill decisions; institutional credibility; insulation from party pressure; and stronger opposition confidence in the fairness of proceedings, which would itself reduce disruption.

Implications against

A constituency effectively loses competitive representation; an unopposed return requires cross-party consensus India's fragmented polity may not sustain; there is no career path after demitting office, deterring able candidates; and coalition parties may resist surrendering a strategically important post.

Position

The objective is right; wholesale adoption is impractical at present. Sequence the reform: first, remove defection adjudication from the Speaker to an independent tribunal, as Keisham Meghachandra suggests, since that is the sharpest conflict of interest; second, institutionalise conventions — the Deputy Speaker going to the Opposition, the Speaker abstaining from party campaigning; and only then consider the full British convention. Objectivity is better secured by removing conflicted functions than by relying on a convention the political system may not honour.

M27Practice15 marks · 250 words

The Lok Sabha's seat allocation among States has remained frozen at 1971 Census levels for five decades. Discuss the constitutional rationale for this freeze and the federal tension it has created.

Directive: "Discuss" two named things — rationale and tension. Both halves are compulsory. This is a rare Polity question with a demographic-federal dimension, so the value-addition comes from population data and the 2026 timeline.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 81/82 + the freeze
  2. The rationale
  3. The federal tension
  4. The 2026 cliff-edge
  5. Way forward

Model answer

Article 81 requires Lok Sabha seats to be allocated so that the ratio of seats to population is, so far as practicable, the same for all States; Article 82 requires readjustment after every census. The 42nd Amendment (1976) froze allocation at 1971 Census figures until 2000; the 84th Amendment (2001) extended the freeze to the first census taken after 2026.

The constitutional rationale

The freeze was a deliberate incentive for population stabilisation. Without it, States that successfully reduced fertility would lose parliamentary seats to States with higher growth — penalising the very policy the national family-planning programme pursued. It severed the link between demographic performance and political representation. The same 1971 baseline is also used for computing vote values in the Presidential election.

The federal tension

Two opposing grievances now coexist. Southern and smaller States — Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Punjab — argue that having reached replacement-level fertility they will be punished for success if seats are reallocated on current population. Northern States — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan — argue the reverse: their citizens are under-represented, each MP standing for far more people, which offends the one-person-one-vote principle Article 81 itself asserts. The freeze also interacts with the 106th Amendment (2023): women's reservation takes effect only after the first delimitation following the next census.

Way forward

Extend the freeze again; enlarge the House so that no State loses seats in absolute terms while relative weights adjust; strengthen the Rajya Sabha as a genuine chamber of State equality; or compensate through fiscal devolution rather than seats. The question is ultimately about the terms of federal union, and any settlement will need broad political consensus rather than a bare majority.

M28Practice15 marks · 250 words

"Classifying a Bill as a Money Bill when it contains substantial non-financial provisions undermines the Rajya Sabha's constitutional role." Examine, with reference to Article 110 and recent legislative practice.

Directive: "Examine… with reference to." Dissect the mechanism, then assess. The tail requires both the constitutional test (Art 110) and actual practice (Aadhaar and after). An answer with only the law, or only the controversy, is half an answer.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 110's exclusivity test
  2. Why misclassification matters — RS's reduced role
  3. Recent practice
  4. The judicial position — unsettled
  5. Verdict + reform

Model answer

Article 110(1) defines a Money Bill exhaustively: a Bill qualifies only if it contains only provisions on taxation, Union borrowing, the Consolidated or Contingency Fund, appropriation, charged expenditure, or the receipt and audit of public money. That word "only" is the whole test — a single substantive non-financial provision should disqualify a Bill.

Why misclassification matters

The consequences are structural. A Money Bill originates only in the Lok Sabha; the Rajya Sabha must return it within 14 days and may only recommend, which the Lok Sabha is free to reject; if it does nothing, the Bill is deemed passed. The Rajya Sabha falls from a co-equal chamber to an advisory one. Since Article 110(3) makes the Speaker's certificate final, misclassification removes a constitutional check by purely procedural means.

Recent practice

The Aadhaar Act, 2016 passed as a Money Bill although it created a statutory authority, an offences regime and a data framework. Finance Act amendments restructuring tribunals and altering political-funding law — including the electoral bonds amendments struck down in 2024 — took the same route.

The judicial position

Puttaswamy (Aadhaar, 2018) examined the certification on merits and upheld it 4:1, Chandrachud J. dissenting that it was a "fraud on the Constitution". Rojer Mathew (2019), five judges, doubted that reasoning and referred reviewability to a seven-judge bench, still pending. Reviewability is therefore open, not settled.

Verdict

Well founded: bicameralism is weakened not by amendment but by classification. The remedy lies in the pending reference holding certification justiciable on the Article 110 test, with the Speaker required to record reasons — preserving procedural finality without immunising the classification.

M29Practice15 marks · 250 words

The absence of full codification of parliamentary privileges has both protected legislative independence and created scope for arbitrary use. Discuss.

Directive: "Discuss" a two-sided claim. The quotation itself hands you the structure — protection on one side, arbitrariness on the other. Follow it exactly, then resolve. The 2024 Sita Soren ruling is the freshest possible evidence and should feature.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 105 + the four sources
  2. How non-codification protects independence
  3. How it enables arbitrariness
  4. The judicial boundary
  5. Verdict: middle path

Model answer

Article 105 (and Article 194 for the States) guarantees freedom of speech in Parliament and such other privileges as Parliament may define by law — but no code has ever been enacted. Privileges therefore rest on four sources: the Constitution, a few statutes, each House's Rules of Procedure, and convention with judicial precedent.

How non-codification protects independence

It keeps privileges flexible, evolving with practice rather than frozen in statute. It avoids continuous judicial interpretation of the legislature's internal affairs, consistent with Articles 122 and 212, which bar courts from questioning proceedings for procedural irregularity. And it lets the House protect itself against executive or external interference without fresh legislation.

How it enables arbitrariness

No citizen or journalist can know in advance what constitutes a breach — offending the rule-of-law principle that punishable conduct must be knowable. The House acts as complainant, judge and executioner in its own cause, and the power has been used against journalists and critics with no defined procedure or appeal, in direct tension with Article 19(1)(a).

The judicial boundary

Sita Soren (2024), seven judges, unanimously overruled P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998): a legislator who accepts a bribe for a vote or speech enjoys no privilege immunity — the offence completes on acceptance, and bribery can never be "necessary" to legislative functioning. Raja Ram Pal (2007) had already held expulsion of members judicially reviewable.

Verdict

Both halves of the claim are true, so the answer is partial codification: codify the citizen-facing edge — what outsiders may be punished for, with notice, hearing and appeal — leaving the House's internal discipline flexible. That preserves independence where it is needed and removes arbitrariness where it harms.
