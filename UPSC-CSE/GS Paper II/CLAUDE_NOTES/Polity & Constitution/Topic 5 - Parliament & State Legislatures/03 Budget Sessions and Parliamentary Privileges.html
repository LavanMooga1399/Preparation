Budget, Sessions & Parliamentary Privileges

Senior-mentor notes closing out the Parliament block — how the calendar year's sessions actually work, the Budget's own multi-stage journey through the House, and the one fact this test has already shown a real weakness for: parliamentary privileges are barely codified at all.

Article 85 — 6-month gap rule Cut Motions Guillotine Article 105 — Privileges Not Codified
6 monthsMax gap allowed between two sessions
◆ The theme tying this note together: things Parliament runs on habit, not hard rules

Sessions are called at the President's discretion (on Cabinet advice) with only one hard rule — a maximum 6-month gap. Cut motions and the Guillotine are procedural tools born from the Lok Sabha's own Rules of Procedure, not the Constitution's text. And privileges — the topic this note ends on — are the clearest example of all: mostly uncodified, resting on a patchwork of Article 105, House rules, conventions, and judicial precedent rather than one clean list. Treat "is this written in the Constitution, or is this a convention/rule" as the running question for this whole note.

Week 3, Day 6 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 23.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Parliament doesn't sit all year round — it meets in blocks of time called "sessions" (traditionally three: Budget, Monsoon, Winter), and between sessions it is "prorogued" — a formal pause, distinct from a daily "adjournment" (a short break within a sitting) and from "dissolution" (the Lok Sabha's term ending entirely, requiring fresh elections). Only the Lok Sabha can be dissolved; the Rajya Sabha, being permanent, is never dissolved, only prorogued.

The Budget session is Parliament's biggest annual event because that's when the government asks Parliament's permission to spend money for the coming year — and Parliament's tools for pushing back (cut motions, the "Guillotine" that force-closes debate on unfinished spending demands) are really just different ways of saying "we haven't fully agreed to this yet." The final section, on privileges, explains something that surprises most students: MPs' special protections (like immunity for what they say in the House) are barely written down as one clean list anywhere — which is itself the single most tested fact about them.

01

Sessions, adjournment, prorogation & dissolution — Articles 85, 108

TermMeaning
SessionThe period between the first sitting of a House and its prorogation — conventionally three per year: Budget (Feb–May), Monsoon (Jul–Sep), Winter (Nov–Dec). Not fixed by the Constitution — purely convention.
Summoning rule (Art 85)The gap between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next cannot exceed 6 months — the only hard constitutional rule governing session frequency, meaning Parliament must meet at least twice a year at minimum.
AdjournmentSuspends a sitting for a specified time — hours, days, or weeks — the House meets again at the appointed time without any fresh summons needed.
Adjournment sine dieTerminates a sitting without any date fixed for the next one — the power to do this rests with the presiding officer (Speaker/Chairman).
ProrogationEnds a session (not just a sitting) — done by the President, usually after (but sometimes before) an adjournment sine die. All pending notices lapse, though pending Bills do not lapse on prorogation.
DissolutionEnds the Lok Sabha's very life — happens automatically at the end of its 5-year term or earlier if the President dissolves it. Only the Lok Sabha can be dissolved; the Rajya Sabha never is (Note 01).
Lame-duck sessionThe last session of an outgoing Lok Sabha, held after a new House has already been elected — the members who lost their seats ("lame ducks") still sit until the new House is constituted. Occasionally tested as a definitional one-liner.
!
The principle that ties the whole motions family together — worth more than the individual facts. Confidence flows from the House that can withdraw it. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha alone (Art 75(3)), so every device that expresses want of confidence belongs to the Lok Sabha and none of them exists in the Rajya Sabha — not the no-confidence motion, not the censure motion, and not the adjournment motion, which carries an element of censure. The Rajya Sabha may discuss and criticise; it cannot censure. The Week 5 paper asked precisely this and the marks went to whoever held the principle rather than three separate facts.
⚠

Trap — what survives dissolution: when the Lok Sabha is dissolved, all pending Bills in the Lok Sabha lapse (except a Bill pending in the Rajya Sabha but not yet passed by the Lok Sabha, which does not lapse), but Bills pending in, or already passed by, the Rajya Sabha and not yet passed by the Lok Sabha do not lapse. Joint-sitting-pending Bills, once the President has notified an intention to summon one, also do not lapse. This "what lapses on dissolution" list is a classic multi-statement trap.

✚

Quorum: to constitute a valid sitting of either House, at least 1/10th of the total membership of that House must be present. If quorum is not met, the presiding officer either adjourns the House or suspends the sitting until quorum is restored.

Question Hour & Zero Hour — the three kinds of question

TypeDistinguishing features
Starred questionMarked with an asterisk; requires an oral answer on the floor; supplementary questions can follow. Notice period: 10 days.
Unstarred questionRequires a written answer laid on the table; no supplementaries permitted. Notice period: 10 days.
Short notice questionOn a matter of urgent public importance, asked with less than 10 days' notice; answered orally.
Question to a private memberAddressed to a private member (not a Minister) when the subject relates to a Bill or matter for which that member is responsible — the forgotten fourth category.
✚

Question Hour is governed by the rules; Zero Hour is an Indian innovation found nowhere in the Rules of Procedure, dating from the 1960s, for raising matters without prior notice. The order differs by House, and this is the sharp edge of the topic: in the Lok Sabha, Question Hour runs first (11 am–12 noon) and Zero Hour follows from 12 noon. In the Rajya Sabha, the Chairman shifted Question Hour to 12 noon in 2014 to protect it from disruption — so the Rajya Sabha's day now begins with Zero Hour at 11 am. A bare statement that "Zero Hour follows Question Hour" is therefore true only of the Lok Sabha.

Motions and resolutions — the full family, and the differences that get asked

A motion is a proposal put to the House for its decision; a resolution is a specific kind of motion that expresses the House's opinion or will. Every resolution is a motion; not every motion is a resolution.

DeviceWhat it is, and the fact that gets tested
Three classes of motionSubstantive — a self-contained independent proposal (impeachment of the President, removal of a judge). Substitute — moved in substitution of an original motion, offering an alternative. Subsidiary — has no meaning by itself and depends on the original motion; subdivided into ancillary, superseding and amendment motions.
Closure motionTo cut short debate and put the matter to vote. Four kinds: simple closure (matter sufficiently discussed), closure by compartments (clauses grouped in advance and each group voted together), kangaroo closure (only important clauses taken up, the rest skipped), and guillotine closure (undiscussed clauses or demands put to vote when the allotted time expires).
Privilege motionMoved by a member against a Minister alleged to have withheld or distorted facts. Its purpose is censure of the Minister, not removal of the government.
Calling Attention motionA member, with prior permission, calls a Minister's attention to a matter of urgent public importance and seeks an authoritative statement. It is an Indian innovation not found in British practice, and unlike a question it need not be preceded by a notice period of the usual kind.
Adjournment motionInterrupts normal business to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance; needs 50 members' support. Because it carries an element of censure, the Rajya Sabha cannot use it. Distinct from the routine adjournment of a sitting.
No-Confidence motionLok Sabha only; needs 50 members' support for admission; states no reasons; if passed, the entire Council of Ministers falls. It can be moved against the government only, never against an individual Minister.
Censure motionThe contrast that carries the marks. It must state the reasons; it may be moved against an individual Minister, a group of Ministers, or the whole Council; it can be moved in the Lok Sabha only; and if passed, the Council need not resign — it must merely explain itself. A no-confidence motion is about survival; a censure motion is about reprimand.
Motion of ThanksMoved on the President's address under Article 87. It must be passed; failure is treated as a defeat of the government, which makes it one of the few floor tests hidden inside routine business.
No-Day-Yet-Named motionA motion admitted by the Speaker for which no date of discussion has yet been fixed.
Point of OrderRaised on a procedural irregularity only — a breach of the rules or of a constitutional provision. It is not a device to raise the merits of a policy, it needs no notice, it suspends proceedings, and no debate is allowed on it. Statement questions routinely define it as a device to question policy; that is wrong.
Half-an-Hour DiscussionOn a matter of sufficient public importance that has already been the subject of a question, where the answer needs elucidation on a point of fact. Three sittings a week are allotted; no formal motion, no voting.
Short Duration DiscussionAlso called a two-hour discussion. On an urgent matter of public importance, raised with the support of two members; two sittings a week; again no formal motion and no voting.
Special Mention / Rule 377A matter not raisable under other devices may be mentioned — called a Special Mention in the Rajya Sabha, and a Mention Under Rule 377 in the Lok Sabha.
!
The three discriminators worth memorising. (1) No-confidence vs censure: no reasons vs reasons stated; against the government only vs against any Minister; resignation compelled vs not compelled. (2) Adjournment motion vs Calling Attention: both need urgency, but only the adjournment motion carries censure — which is why the Rajya Sabha is barred from one and not the other. (3) Half-an-Hour vs Short Duration: the first follows an earlier question; the second does not. Neither involves voting.

The hidden confidence votes — defeats that bring a government down without a no-confidence motion

This is the highest-value exception in the whole Parliament chapter, and it is invisible unless someone points it out: a government can fall without anyone ever moving a motion of no confidence. Certain defeats are treated by convention as an implied withdrawal of the Lok Sabha's confidence, and the Council of Ministers must resign.

What happens on the floorConsequence for the government
A no-confidence motion is passedMust resign. The explicit route — Lok Sabha only, 50 members to admit, no reasons stated.
A confidence motion moved by the government is defeatedMust resign. The mirror image, used to prove a majority the government claims it has.
The Budget is rejected — the Annual Financial Statement, Demands for Grants, the Appropriation Bill or the Finance BillMust resign. A government that cannot carry its own money through the House has, by definition, lost its majority. No motion of any kind is needed.
Any cut motion is passedMust resign. Passing a policy, economy or token cut against the government's wishes operates as a no-confidence vote — which is why cut motions are almost never carried.
The Motion of Thanks on the President's Address is defeatedMust resign. The motion must be passed; its defeat is treated as a defeat of the government.
A government Bill is rejected in the Lok SabhaMust resign where the government has staked its position on the Bill. An ordinary legislative defeat does not automatically topple a government, but a defeat on a measure central to its programme does.
A censure motion is passedNeed NOT resign — the exception inside the exception. The Council must instead seek the confidence of the Lok Sabha as soon as possible. Censure is reprimand, not removal.
An adjournment motion is passedNeed not resign. It carries an element of censure — which is why the Rajya Sabha cannot use it — but it does not compel resignation.
!
Why this is a favourite question, and how it is framed. The stem never says "no-confidence". It says something like "When the annual budget fails to be passed by the Lok Sabha after it is put to vote, which of the following takes place?" — and offers plausible administrative answers: the budget is modified and presented again, it is referred to the Rajya Sabha, the Finance Minister is asked to resign. All three sound like what a government would do. The correct answer is the constitutional one: the Prime Minister submits the resignation of the entire Council of Ministers. The test is not whether you know the Budget process; it is whether you recognise a money defeat as a confidence defeat.

The single rule to carry: money and confidence are the same thing. Anything the Lok Sabha refuses the government on money — the Budget, a Demand for Grant, an Appropriation or Finance Bill, or a cut motion carried against it — is a loss of confidence. Everything else is criticism.

Parliamentary devices & motions — the family a Week 2 question tested

DeviceWhat it is, precisely
No-Confidence MotionLok Sabha only; needs 50 members' support for admission; states no reasons; if passed, the entire Council of Ministers falls. Can be moved against the government only, never against an individual Minister.
Censure MotionMust state the reasons; can target an individual Minister, a group, or the whole Council; if passed, the government need not resign (it signals displeasure, not removal) — the classic contrast with no-confidence.
Adjournment MotionInterrupts normal business to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance; needs 50 members' support; involves an element of censure, so the Rajya Sabha cannot use it. Distinct from the routine adjournment of a sitting.
Calling AttentionA member calls a Minister's attention to an urgent public matter and seeks an authoritative statement — an Indian innovation, no vote, no censure element.
Zero HourThe informal slot right after Question Hour (from 12 noon) for raising matters without prior notice — another Indian procedural innovation, found in no rulebook.
President's Special Address (Article 87)Under Article 87, the President addresses both Houses together at the first session after each general election and the first session of each year — outlining government policy; the address is followed by a Motion of Thanks, whose defeat is treated as a government defeat.
⚠

Trap pairing: no-confidence (no reasons, government-wide, fatal if passed) vs censure (reasons required, can be individual, non-fatal) vs adjournment motion (urgent specific matter, censure element, Lok Sabha only). Multi-statement questions swap exactly these attributes between the three.

The rest of the device toolkit — closure, discussions and points of order

DeviceWhat it does
Closure Motion — four typesA motion to cut short debate and put the matter to vote. Simple closure (a member moves that a matter having been sufficiently discussed be put to vote); closure by compartments (clauses are grouped in advance and each group voted together); kangaroo closure (only important clauses are taken up for debate — the rest are "hopped over" and passed); guillotine (undiscussed clauses/demands are voted along with the discussed ones when time expires).
Privilege MotionMoved by a member against a Minister for a breach of privilege — e.g., withholding facts or giving distorted/incorrect information to the House. Its purpose is to censure the Minister concerned.
Point of OrderRaised when proceedings depart from the normal rules; it is addressed to the presiding officer, whose decision is final. It carries no debate and is an extraordinary device — it suspends the proceedings before the House. Note: it relates to interpretation or enforcement of the Rules, not to the substance of a matter.
Half-an-Hour DiscussionFor a matter of sufficient public importance that has already been the subject of a question and needs elucidation on a point of fact. Conventionally held on three sittings a week; no formal motion, no voting.
Short Duration DiscussionAlso called a "two-hour discussion" — on an urgent matter of public importance, held twice a week; again no formal motion and no voting.
Special MentionA matter that is not a point of order or raisable under other devices may be mentioned with the presiding officer's prior permission. In the Lok Sabha the equivalent device is called "Notice (Mention) Under Rule 377."
Resolutions — three typesPrivate Member's Resolution (moved by a private member, discussed on alternate Fridays); Government Resolution (moved by a Minister); Statutory Resolution (moved under a provision of the Constitution or an Act — e.g., approving an Emergency proclamation). All resolutions are substantive motions and must be voted upon — unlike many motions, every resolution ends in a decision of the House.
No-Day-Yet-Named MotionA motion admitted by the Speaker for which no date has yet been fixed for discussion — it sits in the queue until time is allotted.
⚠

The highest-value distinctions in this table: (1) Kangaroo closure skips unimportant clauses; the guillotine force-votes everything undiscussed when time runs out — different mechanisms, routinely swapped. (2) Half-an-Hour and Short Duration discussions involve no voting; every resolution must be voted on. (3) A Point of Order concerns the rules, not the merits — and it alone suspends business immediately.

Budgetary process — the Annual Financial Statement, Article 112

The Budget (formally the "Annual Financial Statement") is a statement of the Union government's estimated receipts and expenditure for the coming financial year — presented, discussed, and voted on in a fixed multi-stage sequence.

StageDetail
Voting on Demands for GrantsHappens only in the Lok Sabha — each Ministry's spending request ("Demand for Grant") is voted on separately; the Rajya Sabha only discusses the Budget generally and has no vote here, mirroring its limited Money Bill power (Note 02).
Appropriation BillPassed after voting on grants, to legally authorise withdrawal of the voted (and "charged") sums from the Consolidated Fund of India — without this, no money can legally leave the Fund even after grants are voted.
Finance BillGives effect to the government's taxation proposals for the year — must be passed within 75 days of introduction, per the Rules of Procedure.
Charged expenditureCertain expenditures (President's/Governors' emoluments, Supreme Court/High Court judges' salaries, CAG's salary, debt charges) are "charged" on the Consolidated Fund — not voted on at all, only discussed, since they are meant to stay outside day-to-day political bargaining.

Parliamentary Committees — the financial trio and the standing system

Explicitly listed in the Week 3 schedule under the budgetary process, and heavily numeric — the three financial committees' composition figures are the single most testable cluster here.

CommitteeStrength & compositionFunction and the fact that gets tested
Public Accounts Committee (PAC)22 members — 15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha; 1-year term. Ministers cannot be members.Examines the CAG's audit reports — scrutinises spending after it happens ("post-mortem" of expenditure), checking money was spent lawfully on the purpose voted. By convention since 1967 the Chairman is from the Opposition — the most-asked PAC fact.
Estimates Committee30 members — all from the Lok Sabha only; no Rajya Sabha representation at all; 1-year term. Ministers cannot be members.Examines the Budget estimates and suggests economies — scrutiny before/around spending. Nicknamed the "continuous economy committee." The trap: its Lok-Sabha-only composition, versus PAC and COPU which are joint.
Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU)22 members — 15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha (raised from an original 15).Examines the reports and accounts of public sector undertakings, and the CAG's reports on them.
Departmental Standing Committees24 committees, each of 31 members — 21 Lok Sabha + 10 Rajya Sabha; established 1993.Each shadows a cluster of ministries; examines their Demands for Grants before voting (the scrutiny stage in Fig 1) and Bills referred to them. This is where most detailed Budget scrutiny actually happens.
⚠

Three traps in one cluster: (1) the Estimates Committee has no Rajya Sabha members — PAC and COPU both do; (2) a Minister can never be a member of PAC, Estimates or COPU (and if elected, ceases to be one on becoming a Minister); (3) PAC works after expenditure via CAG reports, Estimates works on proposed estimates — swap those two functions and the statement is false. Also note the standing criticism: committee recommendations are advisory, not binding on the government.

Standing · Select · Joint · Ad hoc — the taxonomy questions are built on

Four words that get used loosely in ordinary speech and precisely in exams. The classification runs on two independent axes, and confusing them is the commonest error here.

TypeWhat defines it
Standing committeeDefined by permanence. Constituted every year or periodically, works continuously, is reconstituted rather than dissolved. The financial trio (PAC, Estimates, Public Undertakings), the 24 Departmentally Related Standing Committees, and the housekeeping committees (Business Advisory, Privileges, Ethics, Rules, Government Assurances, Subordinate Legislation, Petitions) are all standing.
Ad hoc committeeDefined by impermanence. Appointed for a specific purpose and ceases to exist once it reports. Two sub-kinds: Inquiry Committees and Advisory Committees — and Select and Joint Committees on Bills fall inside the advisory sub-kind.
Select committeeDefined by membership drawn from one House only. A Bill referred to a Select Committee of the Lok Sabha contains only Lok Sabha members. It is an ad hoc body: it examines the Bill clause by clause, may take evidence, reports back, and then dissolves.
Joint committeeDefined by membership drawn from both Houses. Also ad hoc when constituted on a particular Bill — a Joint Parliamentary Committee — with the convention of roughly twice as many Lok Sabha members as Rajya Sabha members. The 129th Constitutional Amendment Bill on simultaneous elections went to a JPC in 2024.
!
The two axes, kept apart. Permanent vs temporary is one axis: standing versus ad hoc. One House vs both is the other: select versus joint. They are not alternatives to each other — a Select Committee is both ad hoc and single-House. A statement that "a Select Committee is a Standing Committee" is false; so is one claiming a Joint Committee must be permanent. And note the vocabulary trap: a Joint Parliamentary Committee on a scam is an inquiry committee, while a Joint Committee on a Bill is an advisory one — same phrase, different function.
✚
Where a Bill can be sent, and by whom. After the first reading, a Bill may be (1) taken up for consideration straight away, (2) referred to a Select Committee of the House, (3) referred to a Joint Committee of both Houses, or (4) circulated to elicit public opinion. Separately, the presiding officer may refer it to the relevant Departmentally Related Standing Committee — which is the ordinary route today, while Select and Joint Committees are reserved for contentious Bills. All committee recommendations are advisory, not binding on the House.

The wider committee system — standing vs ad hoc

!
Committee numbers, in one line each — because these are pure recall and pure marks. PAC 22 (15 LS + 7 RS) · Estimates 30 (Lok Sabha only) · Public Undertakings 22 (15 LS + 7 RS) · Departmentally Related Standing Committees 24 committees of 31 (21 LS + 10 RS) · Official Language 30 (20 LS + 10 RS). The Official Language Committee is the only one with a 20:10 split — every other mixed committee runs roughly 15:7 or 21:10, so "20 members, 10 and 10" is a manufactured wrong answer that looks plausible.

Parliamentary committees divide into Standing (permanent, reconstituted periodically) and Ad hoc (created for a specific purpose, dissolved on reporting). The financial trio above are Standing Financial Committees; these are the rest that get named in questions.

CommitteeWhat it does
Committee on Government AssurancesChecks whether the assurances, promises and undertakings given by Ministers on the floor of the House have actually been fulfilled — and how far.
Committee on Subordinate LegislationExamines whether the executive is properly exercising the power to make rules, regulations and by-laws delegated by Parliament or conferred by the Constitution — i.e., policing delegated legislation. (Asked directly in UPSC Prelims 2015.)
Committee of Parliament on Official LanguageThe odd one out in this list, and the reason it gets asked: it is the only parliamentary committee created by the Constitution itself — Article 344(4) — and was constituted in 1957. It reviews the progress in the use of Hindi for the Union's official purposes and reports, with recommendations, directly to the President. 30 members: 20 from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha, elected by proportional representation through the single transferable vote. Its Chairman is elected by the committee's own members — by convention the Union Home Minister, which is why it is not chaired by the Speaker.
Committee of PrivilegesSemi-judicial in character; examines breaches of privilege of the House or its members and recommends appropriate action.
Ethics CommitteeEnforces the code of conduct of members; examines cases of misconduct and recommends action.
Business Advisory CommitteeRegulates the programme and time-table of the House — it allots time for the discussion of government legislative and other business.
Committee on PetitionsConsiders petitions from citizens on Bills and matters of general public importance; also examines representations.
Rules CommitteeConsiders matters of procedure and conduct of business, and recommends amendments to the Rules.
Ad hoc committeesCreated for a specific purpose and cease to exist on submitting their report — the two most cited kinds are Select/Joint Committees on a Bill and Committees of Inquiry (e.g., a Joint Parliamentary Committee constituted to probe a specific matter).
✚

Constitutional anchor: parliamentary committees derive from Articles 105 (privileges) and 118 (each House's power to make rules regulating its procedure). Chairmen of these committees are appointed by the Speaker from among the committee's own members — including the Public Accounts Committee. What is special about the PAC is not who appoints its chairman but whom the Speaker appoints: by convention since 1967, a member of the Opposition.

Cut motions, the Guillotine & special grants

ToolWhat it does
Policy Cut MotionMP proposes reducing a Demand for Grant's amount to Re. 1, as a way to record disapproval of the policy underlying the expenditure.
Economy Cut MotionProposes reducing the Demand by a specific amount, to express that the expenditure figure itself should be lower — an economy objection, not a policy one.
Token Cut MotionProposes reducing the Demand by Rs. 100, purely to ventilate a specific grievance falling within the government's responsibility, without objecting to the policy or the amount as such.
The GuillotineAt the end of the time allotted for the Budget discussion, the Speaker puts all outstanding Demands for Grants to vote together, whether or not they have actually been discussed — the mechanism that forces the Budget process to a hard close within a fixed calendar.
Vote on AccountGrants an advance to cover government expenditure for a short period (usually 1–2 months) while the full Budget is still being discussed and voted — keeps government spending legal in the interim.
Vote of Credit / Exceptional GrantVote of Credit: for unforeseen, large-scale needs (e.g., a sudden war) that can't be detailed in advance. Exceptional Grant: for a purpose entirely outside the current year's normal Budget, forming no part of ordinary services.

The other grants — the full six, since questions list them together

GrantWhen it is used
Supplementary GrantThe amount authorised for a service this year proves insufficient and more is needed during the same year.
Additional GrantA new service not contemplated in the Budget of that year requires spending.
Excess GrantMoney has already been spent in excess of the amount granted — voted after the financial year ends, and it must first be examined by the Public Accounts Committee. This PAC linkage is the most-tested fact of the six.
Vote on AccountAdvance to keep spending legal while the Budget is still under consideration.
Vote of CreditUnexpected demand of magnitude/indefinite character that cannot be stated with Budget-style detail.

The three funds — a permanent Prelims favourite

FundArticleKey features
Consolidated Fund of India266(1)All revenues received, loans raised, and repayments received by the Union. No money can be withdrawn without Parliament's authorisation (an Appropriation Act). "Charged" expenditure is met from here without a vote.
Public Account of India266(2)Money where government acts as a banker/trustee — provident funds, small savings, remittances. Withdrawals do NOT require Parliamentary appropriation; they are made by executive action. This is the distinction most often tested.
Contingency Fund of India267Held by the President, an imprest (standing advance) for meeting unforeseen expenditure pending Parliament's authorisation. Operated by the Finance Secretary on behalf of the President. The corpus was raised to ₹30,000 crore in 2021.
⚠

Trap: "all three funds require Parliamentary authorisation before withdrawal" is false — only the Consolidated Fund does. The Public Account operates by executive action, and the Contingency Fund is at the President's disposal for unforeseen needs (with subsequent regularisation).

04

Parliamentary privileges — Article 105 (Union), Article 194 (State)

⚠

The single most important fact in this section: parliamentary privileges in India are largely NOT codified into one clean law. They rest on four different sources at once — (1) specific constitutional provisions (Articles 105, 194); (2) laws made by Parliament (very few actually exist); (3) the Rules of Procedure of each House; and (4) constitutional conventions and judicial interpretation built up case by case. Any MCQ statement claiming "privileges are fully codified in a single Act" is false — this remains, deliberately, an open-ended, evolving body of protections rather than a fixed list.

Individual privileges — belonging to each MP personally

🔴 Individual privileges

●Freedom of speech in Parliament (Art 105(1)) — no MP can be held liable in any court for anything said, or any vote given, in Parliament or its committees.
●Freedom from arrest in civil cases during the session, and 40 days before and after it — criminal-case arrest is not protected at all, a frequent trap.
●Exemption from jury service while Parliament is in session.
●Right to receive prompt information of a fellow member's arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment, or release.

Collective privileges — belonging to the House as a whole

PrivilegeWhat it means
Right to publish debates/proceedingsThe House can publish its own reports without needing prior government permission — but this does not extend to a private individual publishing an unauthorised version of proceedings.
Right to exclude strangersEach House can hold a secret sitting, excluding non-members entirely.
Right to make rulesEach House regulates its own procedure and conduct of business, and no court can question the validity of proceedings on grounds of an alleged procedural irregularity (Art 122/212).
Right to punish for breach of privilege / contemptA House can reprimand, admonish, or even imprison a member or outsider for breach of its privileges — a power exercised sparingly but real.
⚠

Trap: freedom of speech under Article 105(1) is not unlimited — it is subject to the Constitution's other provisions and to the House's own Rules of Procedure; it does not protect a member from action for, say, contempt of court committed through a parliamentary speech, or from the House's own internal disciplinary action.

✚

Recent-judgment layer — Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024), the most important privileges ruling in a generation. A seven-judge bench unanimously overruled P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998) — the JMM-bribery-era precedent that had shielded legislators who took bribes and then voted as paid. The Court held that Articles 105(2)/194(2) immunity attaches to what is necessary for legislative functioning, and bribery is never necessary for a vote or speech: the offence is complete the moment the bribe is accepted, regardless of whether the member ultimately votes as promised, votes the other way, or never votes at all. Concept behind the holding: privilege belongs to the House's collective functioning, not to the member as personal armour — a member claiming individual immunity must show the act falls within what the House needs to function freely, and corruption fails that test by definition. Any statement claiming a legislator's vote-linked bribery is protected by parliamentary privilege is now flatly wrong.

📖 Pros / cons / way forward — should privileges be codified?

The classic Mains debate this note's "largely uncodified" fact sets up. For codification: certainty for citizens and the press (today nobody can list the full set of acts constituting "breach of privilege"); prevents arbitrary or self-serving use of the contempt power against critics; aligns with the rule-of-law principle that punishable conduct must be knowable in advance. Against: a fixed statutory list would freeze what must evolve with parliamentary practice; codification would invite constant judicial interpretation, entangling courts in the legislature's internal affairs and diluting separation of powers; the House's flexible self-protection is itself part of legislative independence. Way forward (liftable): a middle path — codify the citizen-facing edges (what outsiders can be punished for, with procedural safeguards) while leaving the House's internal discipline flexible; cite Sita Soren (2024) as evidence that courts already police the outer boundary where privilege collides with criminal law.

Practice · Direct (Week 3 test — you answered "the budget is modified and presented again")

When the annual budget fails to be passed by the Lok Sabha after it is put to vote, which of the following takes place?

  • (a) The budget is modified and presented again
  • (b) The budget is referred to the Rajya Sabha for suggestions
  • (c) The Union Finance Minister is asked to resign
  • (d) The Prime Minister submits the resignation of the Council of Ministers
Answer: (d)
  • Rejection of the Budget is an implied vote of no confidence. A government that cannot carry its own money through the Lok Sabha has lost its majority, and the entire Council of Ministers must resign — no motion of any kind is required.
  • (a), (b) and (c) all describe things a government might administratively do, which is exactly why they are offered. (b) is doubly wrong: the Rajya Sabha has no vote on Demands for Grants at all.
  • (c) is the sharpest distractor — it isolates the Finance Minister. But responsibility here is collective under Article 75(3), not individual.
  • The transferable rule: money defeat = confidence defeat. The same applies to a cut motion carried against the government and to defeat of the Motion of Thanks.
Practice · How many (Week 4)

Consider the following events in the Lok Sabha:

  1. A cut motion is passed against the government.
  2. The Motion of Thanks on the President's Address is defeated.
  3. A censure motion against the Council of Ministers is passed.
  4. An adjournment motion is passed.

After how many of the above is the Council of Ministers required to resign?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (b) — only two
  • 1 and 2 compel resignation. A carried cut motion operates as a no-confidence vote; the Motion of Thanks must be passed, and its defeat is a defeat of the government.
  • 3 does not. A passed censure motion requires the Council to seek the confidence of the House as soon as possible — it does not itself remove the government.
  • 4 does not. An adjournment motion carries an element of censure, which is why the Rajya Sabha cannot use it, but it compels nothing.
  • Sort them by the rule: money and confidence topple; criticism does not.
Practice · Statements (Week 4, Day 1)

Consider the following statements regarding parliamentary committees:

  1. A Select Committee on a Bill consists of members drawn from one House only.
  2. A Select Committee is a Standing Committee of that House.
  3. A Joint Committee on a Bill ceases to exist once it presents its report.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (b) — only two
  • Statements 1 and 3 are correct. "Select" means single-House; a Joint or Select Committee on a Bill dissolves on reporting.
  • Statement 2 is wrong. A Select Committee is ad hoc, not standing. The two axes are independent: permanent-versus-temporary is one, single-House-versus-both is the other.
  • Hold the rule: Select = one House and temporary. Joint = both Houses. Standing = permanent.
Practice · Statements (Week 4, Day 1)

With reference to a censure motion, consider the following statements:

  1. It must state the reasons for its adoption.
  2. It can be moved against an individual Minister.
  3. If passed, the Council of Ministers must resign.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 2 and 3 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (a) — 1 and 2 only
  • Statements 1 and 2 are correct — a censure motion states reasons and may target an individual Minister, a group, or the whole Council.
  • Statement 3 is wrong. A passed censure motion does not compel resignation; the Council must explain itself. Only a no-confidence motion brings the government down.
  • The contrast in one line: no-confidence is about survival, censure is about reprimand.
Practice · Negative (Week 4, Day 1)

Which one of the following statements about a Point of Order is NOT correct?

  • (a) It relates to the interpretation or enforcement of the Rules of Procedure
  • (b) It may be used to raise the merits of a policy under discussion
  • (c) It requires no prior notice and suspends the proceedings
  • (d) No debate is allowed on a Point of Order
Answer: (b) is NOT correct
  • A Point of Order is strictly procedural — it raises a breach of the Rules or of a constitutional provision, never the merits of a policy.
  • (a), (c) and (d) are all accurate; it is an extraordinary device because it interrupts business without notice.
  • To raise substance rather than procedure, the correct devices are a Short Duration Discussion or a Calling Attention motion.
Practice · Statements (Week 3 test — you skipped this after 13 seconds)

With reference to the Committee of Parliament on Official Language, consider the following statements:

  1. It consists of 20 members, 10 from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha.
  2. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha is the Chairman of the Committee.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (d) — neither
  • Statement 1 is wrong on both numbers. The Committee has 30 members — 20 from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha, elected by proportional representation through the single transferable vote.
  • Statement 2 is wrong. The Chairman is elected by the Committee's own members; by convention the Union Home Minister holds the chair.
  • Worth knowing for its own sake: this is the only parliamentary committee created by the Constitution itself, under Article 344(4), constituted in 1957, and it reports directly to the President.

Practice · Negative ⚠

With reference to the Question Hour, which one of the following statements is NOT correct?

  • (a) A starred question requires an oral answer and permits supplementary questions.
  • (b) An unstarred question requires a written answer and permits no supplementaries.
  • (c) A short notice question is asked with less than ten days' notice on a matter of urgent public importance.
  • (d) In the Lok Sabha, Zero Hour precedes Question Hour in the daily order of business.
Answer: (d)
  • In the Lok Sabha Question Hour is the first hour (11 am–12 noon) and Zero Hour follows from 12 noon — so the statement inverts the order.
  • Note the qualifier "in the Lok Sabha". Since 2014 the Rajya Sabha takes Zero Hour at 11 am and Question Hour at 12 noon, so without naming the House the statement would be arguable rather than wrong.

M29Practice15 marks · 250 words

The absence of full codification of parliamentary privileges has both protected legislative independence and created scope for arbitrary use. Discuss.

Directive: "Discuss" a two-sided claim. The quotation itself hands you the structure — protection on one side, arbitrariness on the other. Follow it exactly, then resolve. The 2024 Sita Soren ruling is the freshest possible evidence and should feature.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 105 + the four sources
  2. How non-codification protects independence
  3. How it enables arbitrariness
  4. The judicial boundary
  5. Verdict: middle path

Model answer

Article 105 (and Article 194 for the States) guarantees freedom of speech in Parliament and such other privileges as Parliament may define by law — but no code has ever been enacted. Privileges therefore rest on four sources: the Constitution, a few statutes, each House's Rules of Procedure, and convention with judicial precedent.

How non-codification protects independence

It keeps privileges flexible, evolving with practice rather than frozen in statute. It avoids continuous judicial interpretation of the legislature's internal affairs, consistent with Articles 122 and 212, which bar courts from questioning proceedings for procedural irregularity. And it lets the House protect itself against executive or external interference without fresh legislation.

How it enables arbitrariness

No citizen or journalist can know in advance what constitutes a breach — offending the rule-of-law principle that punishable conduct must be knowable. The House acts as complainant, judge and executioner in its own cause, and the power has been used against journalists and critics with no defined procedure or appeal, in direct tension with Article 19(1)(a).

The judicial boundary

Sita Soren (2024), seven judges, unanimously overruled P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998): a legislator who accepts a bribe for a vote or speech enjoys no privilege immunity — the offence completes on acceptance, and bribery can never be "necessary" to legislative functioning. Raja Ram Pal (2007) had already held expulsion of members judicially reviewable.

Verdict

Both halves of the claim are true, so the answer is partial codification: codify the citizen-facing edge — what outsiders may be punished for, with notice, hearing and appeal — leaving the House's internal discipline flexible. That preserves independence where it is needed and removes arbitrariness where it harms.

Value additions used: Arts 105, 194, 122, 212, 19(1)(a) · the four sources of privilege · complainant-judge-executioner critique · Sita Soren (2024, 7 judges) overruling P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998) · Raja Ram Pal (2007) · the partial-codification proposal.

Union of India" from the case name. Bought: nothing new — length only, 324 → budget over two passes. The 1998 → 2024 overruling stays: a question about codification is really a question about who draws the boundary, which is precisely what Sita Soren answers.
What would cost you marks: arguing only one side; missing Sita Soren (2024), the single most current and relevant authority; proposing full codification without acknowledging what would be lost.
★ 90-second recall

Last-mile revision — Budget, Sessions & Privileges

  • Art 85: max 6-month gap between sessions — the only hard constitutional rule on session timing.
  • Adjournment (short pause) → adjournment sine die (no date fixed) → prorogation (ends session, by President) → dissolution (ends Lok Sabha's life, LS only, never RS).
  • Bills pending in RS (not yet passed by LS) survive dissolution; Bills pending in LS itself lapse.
  • Quorum: 1/10th of total House membership.
  • Budget stages: Presentation → General Discussion → Standing Committee scrutiny → Voting on Demands for Grants (LS only) → Appropriation Bill → Finance Bill (75-day rule).
  • Charged expenditure: discussed, never voted (President/Governor emoluments, judges' salaries, CAG salary, debt charges).
  • Cut motions: Policy (cut to Re. 1) / Economy (specific amount) / Token (Rs. 100, grievance only).
  • Guillotine: force-votes all undiscussed Demands when time runs out.
  • Vote on Account: short-term advance while full Budget is pending.
  • Parliamentary privileges: NOT fully codified — rest on Art 105/194 + rare statutes + House Rules + convention/case law.
  • Freedom of speech (Art 105(1)): not absolute — subject to other constitutional provisions and House rules.
  • Freedom from arrest: civil cases only, session + 40 days before/after — never protects against criminal arrest.
  • Sita Soren (2024, 7 judges): overruled P.V. Narasimha Rao — NO privilege immunity for bribery linked to a vote/speech; offence complete on accepting the bribe.
