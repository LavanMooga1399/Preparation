Powers, Functions & Legislative Process

Senior-mentor notes on what Parliament actually does with the structure Note 01 described — the six power categories, the precise stage-by-stage journey of a Bill, and the single most commonly confused pairing in this whole topic: Money Bill vs. Financial Bill.

Across both Week 1 and Week 2, this coaching test's single most reliable trap shape has been "X can do this but not that, and here's the specific exception" — and the Money Bill vs. Financial Bill vs. Ordinary Bill distinction is exactly that shape, three times over. This note is built around getting that one distinction airtight, since it alone likely accounts for more marks than any other single fact in Topic 5.

Week 3, Day 5 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 23.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Parliament's core job is to turn a proposal into a law, and that journey is called a Bill's passage. Most Bills ("ordinary Bills") can start in either House and need both Houses' agreement, roughly like two people needing to both sign a contract. But money is different — since the Lok Sabha alone is directly elected, the Constitution gives it near-total control over anything involving taxation or government spending (a "Money Bill"), and reduces the Rajya Sabha's role there to advice only, not a real veto.

The exam-relevant confusion is that some Bills involve money and other important provisions — these are "Financial Bills," and unlike a pure Money Bill, they still need both Houses' real agreement, just with one extra procedural step (a Presidential recommendation) added on top of the ordinary process. Getting this three-way distinction exactly right is most of what this note is for.

Powers of Parliament — the six-category checklist

LegislativeMakes laws on Union List and Concurrent List subjects; can legislate on a State List subject in specific circumstances (national interest via Rajya Sabha resolution, National Emergency, State's request, or to implement international treaties — the same Article 249/250/252/253 set from Topic 2, Note 02).
FinancialControls the public purse — no tax can be levied, no expenditure incurred, without Parliament's authorisation. Full detail in Note 03 (Budget process).

JudicialCan impeach the President (Art 61) and the Vice-President's removal resolution (Art 67); can remove Supreme Court/High Court judges via an address to the President; decides on breach of its own privileges.
ConstituentAmends the Constitution under Article 368 — the full detail lives in Topic 1, Note 05.

OtherServes as the highest deliberative forum on national issues and public policy; acts as an organ of information — Laxmikanth notes it is a more effective information-extracting instrument than the press, because ministers are bound to answer.

Is Parliament sovereign? — the six limitations (a standing Mains question)

The British Parliament is legally sovereign; the Indian Parliament is not. An answer that simply asserts this scores little — the marks are in naming the specific constraints.

LimitationHow it binds Parliament
Written Constitution with a rigid amendment procedureParliament's own powers are defined and limited by a document it did not create and cannot casually rewrite — the special-majority and State-ratification routes of Article 368 constrain it.
Federal division of powersParliament ordinarily legislates only on the Union and Concurrent Lists; State List subjects are outside it save in the Article 249/250/252/253 situations.
Fundamental Rights (Part III)Article 13 voids any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights — a direct, textual limit on legislative competence.
Judicial reviewThe Supreme Court and High Courts can strike down a law as unconstitutional; judicial review is itself part of the basic structure (Minerva Mills).
Basic structure doctrineEven a validly-passed constitutional amendment falls if it damages the basic structure — the deepest limit, and one Parliament cannot legislate away (Topic 1, Note 06).
Presidential assent & the second chamberA Bill needs the President's assent; and outside Money Bills, the Rajya Sabha's concurrence is real — for a Constitutional Amendment Bill it is absolute, with no joint-sitting override.
✚

Model line for Mains: "The Indian Parliament is supreme within its sphere but not sovereign over the Constitution — it is a creature of the Constitution, not its creator, which is precisely the argument Palkhivala pressed in Kesavananda Bharati and the Court accepted."

Types of Bills

TypeCan originate inRajya Sabha's real power over it
Ordinary BillEither HouseFull — can reject, amend, or delay; disagreement can lead to a joint sitting (Note 01, Section 04).
Money Bill (Art 110)Lok Sabha onlyVery limited — must return it within 14 days with or without recommendations; Lok Sabha may accept or reject every recommendation; if RS doesn't act within 14 days, the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses anyway.
Financial Bill, Category I (Art 117(1))Lok Sabha only (contains Money Bill matters plus other provisions)Full ordinary-Bill powers after introduction — RS can reject or amend it like any ordinary Bill, unlike a true Money Bill.
Financial Bill, Category II (Art 117(3))Either HouseFull ordinary-Bill powers — the only special rule is that it needs the President's prior recommendation before being passed, not before introduction.
Constitutional Amendment Bill (Art 368)Either HouseFull, equal power with Lok Sabha — no joint sitting possible if the Houses disagree (Note 01's trap).
⚠

Trap — who decides if a Bill is a Money Bill: the Speaker of the Lok Sabha alone certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill when it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha, and Article 110(3) declares this certification final. This is precisely the mechanism at the centre of recent controversies over Bills (e.g., the Aadhaar Act, 2016) being passed as Money Bills to bypass Rajya Sabha scrutiny.

✚

Recent-judgment layer — "final" is no longer the whole story. The textbook position ("not questionable in any court") has been overtaken in stages: in the Aadhaar judgment (K.S. Puttaswamy, 2018) the Supreme Court actually examined the Money Bill certification on merits (and upheld it 4:1, Chandrachud J. dissenting powerfully that it was a "fraud on the Constitution"); then in Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019) a five-judge bench doubted the Aadhaar majority's reading of Article 110 and referred the certification-review question to a larger seven-judge bench — where it remains pending. Exam-precise formulation: the Speaker's certificate is final for parliamentary procedure, but whether it is judicially reviewable is an open constitutional question before a seven-judge bench, not a settled "no." A statement asserting flatly that certification "cannot be questioned in any court" is the outdated-textbook trap.

Rajya Sabha vs Lok Sabha — where the two are equal, and where they are not

This is the most reliably recurring comparison in the whole Parliament syllabus, and it is almost always asked as a how-many question: "in how many of the above matters do both Houses have equal status?" Learn it as two closed lists, because a how-many question gives no partial credit for knowing most of it.

Equal status and powersRajya Sabha is weaker

Introduction and passage of Constitutional Amendment Bills — and note there is no joint sitting for these, so the Rajya Sabha holds an absolute veto hereFinancial Bills Category I (Art 117(1)) — introduction only in the Lok Sabha.
Introduction and passage of Financial Bills involving expenditure from the Consolidated Fund (Category II)Demands for Grants — voted only in the Lok Sabha; the Rajya Sabha may discuss the Budget but casts no vote.
Election and impeachment of the PresidentA no-confidence motion lies only in the Lok Sabha — the Council of Ministers is responsible to that House alone.
Election and removal of the Vice-President — though the removal resolution can originate only in the Rajya SabhaDiscontinuance of a National Emergency — a resolution disapproving its continuance can be passed only by the Lok Sabha.
Recommending removal of the CJI and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, the CEC and the CAGIn a joint sitting, the numerically larger Lok Sabha ordinarily prevails.
Approval of ordinances issued by the President 
Approval of a proclamation of all three emergencies — National (352), President's Rule (356) and Financial (360)

!
Three items candidates routinely misfile. (1) Emergency approval is equal — all three proclamations need both Houses. What is unequal is discontinuance of a National Emergency, which only the Lok Sabha can resolve. (2) Constitutional Amendment Bills are equal, and because Article 368 permits no joint sitting, the Rajya Sabha's veto there is absolute — stronger, not weaker, than its position on ordinary Bills. (3) Enlarging the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and the UPSC is equal, despite sounding like a Union-heavy function. Money Bills are the only really lopsided category, and everything unequal traces back to either money or confidence.
📖 Pros / cons / way forward — simultaneous elections ("One Nation, One Election")

The live legislative-process debate most likely to surface in any Parliament-themed Mains paper. The 129th Constitutional Amendment Bill (introduced December 2024, referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee) proposes synchronising Lok Sabha and Assembly elections, following the Kovind Committee's recommendation. For: massive cost and administrative savings; ends the near-permanent Model Code of Conduct freeze on governance; reduces voter fatigue. Against: a State government falling mid-cycle would get only a truncated term, weakening federal autonomy; national issues would swamp State-level accountability in a single campaign; the constitutional machinery for handling mid-term collapse is untested. Way forward (liftable): phased synchronisation with consensus-built safeguards for truncated terms, so efficiency gains do not come at the price of the federal principle — and note the JPC process itself as evidence that major constitutional change is being routed through deliberation rather than speed.

03

An ordinary Bill — stage by stage

✚

The three readings, named precisely (the schedule lists "Stages of a Bill" as its own topic). First Reading — introduction only: the member asks leave, the Bill's title and objectives are read, and it is published in the Gazette. No discussion of the Bill's substance takes place at this stage at all — the most common trap. Second Reading — the substantive stage, itself in three sub-stages: general discussion (principles debated, no clause detail), the committee stage (usually a Departmental Standing Committee examines it clause by clause), then the consideration/clause-by-clause stage in the House where amendments are voted. Third Reading — debate confined to accepting or rejecting the Bill as a whole; no amendments are permitted, only verbal/formal corrections. The Bill is then transmitted to the other House, where all three readings repeat.

✚

Private Member's Bill vs. Government Bill: a Bill introduced by any MP who is not a Minister is a Private Member's Bill; one introduced by a Minister is a Government Bill. Private Member's Bills need 1 month's advance notice (vs. 7 days for Government Bills) and are taken up only on specific days (traditionally Fridays) — reflecting how little parliamentary time is actually reserved for them. Very few Private Member's Bills have ever become law — a fact worth citing if a Mains answer needs evidence of Parliament's executive-dominated legislative agenda.

04

Money Bill vs. Financial Bill — the exact test, Article 110

A Bill is a Money Bill only if it contains exclusively provisions dealing with the specific matters listed in Article 110(1) — the moment it contains anything else, it becomes a Financial Bill instead, even if money matters dominate it.

🔴 Article 110(1) — the exhaustive list (verbatim-close)

●Imposition, abolition, remission, alteration, or regulation of any tax.
●Regulation of borrowing of money by the Union government.
●Custody of the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund of India — payments in or withdrawals from either.
●Appropriation of money out of the Consolidated Fund of India.
●Declaring any expenditure to be "charged" on the Consolidated Fund, or increasing such expenditure.
●Receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund, or the audit of Union/State accounts.
⚠

Trap: a Bill that only incidentally touches money — for example, one that imposes a fine or fee as part of a wider regulatory scheme — is not automatically a Money Bill just because a payment is involved. The test is whether the Bill deals exclusively with the Article 110(1) list; any additional non-financial provision (even a small one) reclassifies it as a Financial Bill.

PYQ analysis & practice questions

Trend verdict: the Money Bill definition is the most heavily-tested item in this note, and UPSC's favourite way of asking it is the negative form — "which is NOT correct" — with one option subtly swapping the Consolidated Fund for the Contingency Fund. (A) Genuine UPSC PYQs

Practice · How many (Week 3 test)

Consider the following matters:

  1. Election and impeachment of the President
  2. Approval of a proclamation of all three types of emergency
  3. Enlargement of the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and the UPSC
  4. Introduction of Money Bills
  5. Introduction and passage of Constitutional Amendment Bills

In how many of the above do the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha have equal status and powers?

  • (a) Only two
  • (b) Only three
  • (c) Only four
  • (d) All five
Answer: (c) — only four
  • Equal: 1, 2, 3 and 5. Emergency approval needs both Houses; enlarging the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and the UPSC is an equal power; and Constitutional Amendment Bills are equal — with no joint sitting available, giving the Rajya Sabha an absolute veto there.
  • Unequal: only 4. A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha.
  • The discriminator to hold: what the Rajya Sabha loses is tied to money and to confidence — Money Bills, Demands for Grants, no-confidence motions, and discontinuing a National Emergency. Everything else is equal.
2017Prelims

Regarding the Money Bill, which of the following statements is not correct?

  • (a) A bill shall be deemed to be a Money Bill if it contains only provisions relating to imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax.
  • (b) A Money Bill has provisions for the custody of the Consolidated Fund of India or the Contingency Fund of India.
  • (c) A Money Bill is concerned with the appropriation of moneys out of the Contingency Fund of India.
  • (d) A Money Bill deals with the regulation of borrowing of money or giving of any guarantee by the Government of India.
Answer: (c)
  • Article 110(1)(d) covers appropriation of moneys out of the Consolidated Fund — not the Contingency Fund.
  • One word changed, one mark lost. Verdict: direct hit on Section 04's Article 110 list.
  • Drill this exact shape: on a "not correct" Money Bill question, check every option against the six-item list before choosing.
2015Prelims

Consider the following statements regarding a No-Confidence Motion in India:

  1. There is no mention of a No-Confidence Motion in the Constitution of India.
  2. A Motion of No-Confidence can be introduced in the Lok Sabha only.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (c)
  • Both correct. The Constitution mentions only collective responsibility (Article 75(3));
  • The no-confidence motion itself is a creature of the Lok Sabha's Rules of Procedure (Rule 198), not the constitutional text.
  • And it lies only in the Lok Sabha, because confidence flows from the directly-elected House alone.
2013Prelims

The Parliament can make any law for the whole or any part of India for implementing international treaties

  • (a) with the consent of all the States
  • (b) with the consent of the majority of States
  • (c) with the consent of the States concerned
  • (d) without the consent of any State
Answer: (d)
  • Article 253 lets Parliament legislate even on State List subjects to implement any treaty, agreement or convention — no State consent required at all.
  • Contrast this deliberately with Article 252, which does need two or more States to request it.
  • Treaty power (253) vs States' request (252) is a standing pairs trap.

Predicted MCQ

If the Rajya Sabha does not return a Money Bill to the Lok Sabha within the constitutionally prescribed period, consider the following statements:

  1. The Bill lapses.
  2. The Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form the Lok Sabha originally passed it.
  3. A joint sitting of both Houses must be called.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 2 and 3 only
Answer: (b)

The 14-day window (Section 02) exists precisely so that Rajya Sabha inaction cannot block a Money Bill; no joint-sitting mechanism applies to Money Bills at all.

Predicted MCQ

With reference to the certification of a Bill as a Money Bill, consider the following statements:

  1. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill.
  2. Article 110(3) declares the Speaker's certificate on this to be final.
  3. Whether such certification is amenable to judicial review is currently pending before a larger Bench of the Supreme Court.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  • (b) 2 and 3 only

M28Practice15 marks · 250 words

"Classifying a Bill as a Money Bill when it contains substantial non-financial provisions undermines the Rajya Sabha's constitutional role." Examine, with reference to Article 110 and recent legislative practice.

Directive: "Examine… with reference to." Dissect the mechanism, then assess. The tail requires both the constitutional test (Art 110) and actual practice (Aadhaar and after). An answer with only the law, or only the controversy, is half an answer.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 110's exclusivity test
  2. Why misclassification matters — RS's reduced role
  3. Recent practice
  4. The judicial position — unsettled
  5. Verdict + reform

Model answer

Article 110(1) defines a Money Bill exhaustively: a Bill qualifies only if it contains only provisions on taxation, Union borrowing, the Consolidated or Contingency Fund, appropriation, charged expenditure, or the receipt and audit of public money. That word "only" is the whole test — a single substantive non-financial provision should disqualify a Bill.

Why misclassification matters

The consequences are structural. A Money Bill originates only in the Lok Sabha; the Rajya Sabha must return it within 14 days and may only recommend, which the Lok Sabha is free to reject; if it does nothing, the Bill is deemed passed. The Rajya Sabha falls from a co-equal chamber to an advisory one. Since Article 110(3) makes the Speaker's certificate final, misclassification removes a constitutional check by purely procedural means.

Recent practice

The Aadhaar Act, 2016 passed as a Money Bill although it created a statutory authority, an offences regime and a data framework. Finance Act amendments restructuring tribunals and altering political-funding law — including the electoral bonds amendments struck down in 2024 — took the same route.

The judicial position

Puttaswamy (Aadhaar, 2018) examined the certification on merits and upheld it 4:1, Chandrachud J. dissenting that it was a "fraud on the Constitution". Rojer Mathew (2019), five judges, doubted that reasoning and referred reviewability to a seven-judge bench, still pending. Reviewability is therefore open, not settled.

Verdict

Well founded: bicameralism is weakened not by amendment but by classification. The remedy lies in the pending reference holding certification justiciable on the Article 110 test, with the Speaker required to record reasons — preserving procedural finality without immunising the classification.

★ 90-second recall

Last-mile revision — Powers, Functions & Legislative Process

  • Six power categories: Legislative, Financial, Executive, Judicial, Constituent, Electoral.
  • Money Bill: Lok Sabha only; RS gets 14 days, advisory power only; deemed passed if RS sits on it.
  • Financial Bill I: LS only, but full RS power after introduction. Financial Bill II: either House, only needs prior Presidential recommendation.
  • Money Bill test (Art 110(1)): must deal exclusively with tax, borrowing, Consolidated/Contingency Fund, appropriation, charged expenditure, or accounts/audit.
  • Speaker certifies Money Bill status — "final" (Art 110(3)); but judicial reviewability is OPEN, pending before a 7-judge bench (Rojer Mathew, 2019; Aadhaar 4:1 examined it on merits).
  • Ordinary Bill: 3 readings in each House, then Presidential assent (Art 111) — absolute/suspensive/pocket veto (Topic 6, Note 00).
  • Private Member's Bill: 1 month's notice, rarely enacted. Government Bill: 7 days' notice.
  • Constitutional Amendment Bills: equal RS power, no joint sitting if disagreement (Note 01's trap).
