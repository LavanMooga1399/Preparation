Parliamentary System & Presidential Government

Senior-mentor notes on the form of government India chose — how a Parliamentary system differs structurally from a Presidential one, why the Constituent Assembly picked the former, and where the Indian model quietly departs from the British original it borrowed from.

◆ Why this note exists

Laxmikanth's Indian Polity devotes a full chapter (Chapter 7, "Parliamentary System") to the form of government question before it ever gets to Parliament's own structure and functioning — because you cannot study how Parliament works until you know why India built a Parliament-centred executive at all. This note plays the same role for Topic 5 that Note 00 played for Topic 1: it is added as Note 00, not because it is one of Topic 5's official syllabus elements (structure, functioning, conduct of business, powers & privileges), but because it is the conceptual scaffolding those elements sit on.

This is also Week 2, Day 3 of the study plan — sequenced right after Topic 1 closes out with Fundamental Duties, and right before the Federal System notes (Topic 2) that follow it.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Every democracy has to answer one basic design question: once you elect a legislature to make laws, who actually runs the government day to day, and how are they kept accountable? There are two classic answers. In a Parliamentary system (Britain, India), the government is not separately elected at all — it is simply drawn from whichever party or coalition controls the legislature, and it survives only for as long as it keeps that legislature's confidence. Lose a vote of confidence, and the government falls immediately, possibly triggering fresh elections. In a Presidential system (the USA), voters separately elect a President for a fixed term, and that President runs the government independently of the legislature — Congress cannot vote the President out just for losing political support, only impeach for a specific serious wrongdoing.

India deliberately chose the first model, and the reason is worth holding before any of the technical detail below: the framers wanted a government that answers to elected representatives continuously, every week, in Parliament — not just once every four or five years at an election. That single choice is why India ended up with a President who is largely ceremonial (Section 02) even though the country is also, confusingly, a "Republic" — having an elected President is not the same thing as running a Presidential system, and that exact confusion is one of Prelims' favourite traps here (Section 03).

Two Constituent Assembly touchpoints worth anchoring early: Article 74 (President aided and advised by a Council of Ministers headed by the PM) and Article 75(3) (Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha) are the two clauses that constitutionally lock in the parliamentary form — everything else in this note explains why the Assembly wrote those two clauses the way it did.

02

The Parliamentary System — definition & features

A Parliamentary system (also called the Westminster or Cabinet system) is one in which the executive is drawn from, and remains accountable to, the legislature — the government survives only as long as it commands the confidence of the House. India runs this system at both the Centre (Article 74–75) and in every State (Article 163–164), making it, along with the UK, one of the most consistently parliamentary large democracies in the world.

FeatureHow it works in India
Nominal vs real executivePresident is the constitutional (de jure) head of state; the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers are the real (de facto) executive who actually govern — Article 74(1).
Majority party ruleThe party or coalition with a majority in the Lok Sabha forms the government; its leader is appointed Prime Minister by the President.
Collective responsibilityArticle 75(3) — the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha; a no-confidence motion against one minister brings down the whole ministry.
Membership overlapMinisters must be members of Parliament (or become one within six months of appointment) — the executive sits inside the legislature, not apart from it.
LeadershipThe Prime Minister leads both the government and (usually) the majority party — the link that makes fusion of powers work in practice.
DissolutionThe Lok Sabha can be dissolved before its full term on the PM's advice, and mid-term elections can follow a lost confidence vote.
03

The Presidential System — the comparison case

India did not adopt this model, but Prelims tests it as the contrast case almost every year it asks about Parliamentary features — so knowing the Presidential system precisely is often the fastest way to eliminate a wrong option about India's own system. The USA is the textbook example.

FeaturePresidential (USA model)
Head of state & governmentCombined in one office — the President is both, with no separate ceremonial head.
Source of authorityPresident is directly (in the US, quasi-directly via an Electoral College) elected by the people, not by the legislature.
Separation of powersStrict — the President and Cabinet secretaries are not members of Congress and cannot vote in it.
ResponsibilityPresident is not responsible to the legislature for policy; cannot be removed by a no-confidence vote, only by impeachment for a defined constitutional wrong.
TenureFixed term (four years in the US) regardless of how unpopular the President's policies become.
CabinetAppointed by the President as advisors, individually responsible to him — not a collective, Parliament-facing body.
⚠️

Common trap: "India has a President, so India is quasi-Presidential" — false. Having an office titled "President" says nothing about the system; it is how that President is chosen, and whether the executive is accountable to the legislature, that decides Parliamentary vs Presidential. India's President is indirectly elected and not the real executive — both are Parliamentary-system markers, not Presidential ones.

04

Merits & demerits of the Parliamentary System

This is the classic Mains "critically evaluate" prompt — 2020's GS2 question (Section 07) is built almost entirely on this merit/demerit tension, so the two columns below are worth being able to reproduce as an answer skeleton, not just recognise.

MeritsDemerits
Harmony between legislature and executive — no gridlock of the kind a separately-elected President and Congress can produce.Government instability — a coalition can collapse mid-term on a lost confidence vote, unlike a Presidential system's fixed term.
Responsible government — ministers answer daily to Parliament (Question Hour, motions, committees), not just once every few years to voters.No separation of powers in the strict sense — the same people who make the law (majority MPs) also run the executive that enforces it.
Alternative government is ready-made — the Leader of the Opposition and shadow scrutiny mean a fallen government can be replaced without a fresh election.Rule by amateurs, historically argued — ministers are chosen from winning politicians, not necessarily domain experts, unlike a US Cabinet appointee.
More representative — a Council of Ministers can accommodate multiple parties, regions and interests inside one Cabinet, useful for a diverse country.Dictatorship of the Cabinet — a Prime Minister with a strong single-party majority can dominate both the executive and the legislature simultaneously.
!
Confidence is not only lost by a no-confidence motion. The Council must also resign if the Budget is rejected, if any cut motion is carried against the government, if the Motion of Thanks is defeated, or if a confidence motion it moves is lost. A censure motion is the exception — the Council need not resign but must seek the confidence of the House as soon as possible. The working rule: money defeat = confidence defeat; criticism is not. Full treatment in Topic 5, Note 03.
05

Why the Constituent Assembly chose the Parliamentary System

The Assembly debated this explicitly and picked Parliamentary over Presidential for four connected reasons — all four are individually testable as Prelims "which of the following is a reason" statements.

●
Familiarity: India had already run diarchy and provincial-autonomy structures under the 1919 and 1935 Acts that resembled Cabinet government — a known system was less risky to launch a new republic on.
●
Preference for responsible government: continuous accountability to Parliament was judged more democratic than accountability once every fixed term.
●
Avoiding legislative–executive conflict: a Presidential system's separately-elected branches can deadlock each other; a newly independent, diverse country could not risk that.
●
Representing India's diversity: a multi-member Council of Ministers offers many more seats at the executive table than a single President ever could — useful for a linguistically and religiously plural country.
◆

Value-addition: Dr. Ambedkar defended the choice directly in the Constituent Assembly, arguing that a Presidential system trades daily accountability for occasional accountability, and that "the daily assessment of responsibility... is more effective than the periodic assessment" a Presidential term provides. This line is a ready-made Mains quote for any answer defending the Parliamentary choice.

✚

The doctrinal anchor — Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974): when an answer needs a judicial source (not just the Assembly's intent) for the proposition that India's is a parliamentary executive, cite this seven-judge decision: the President and Governors are constitutional heads who act on the aid and advice of their Ministers save in exceptional situations — the case that converted this note's entire framework from convention into declared law. It pairs naturally with the 42nd/44th Amendment arc in Topic 6, Note 00.

📖 The recurring counter-debate — should India switch to a Presidential system?

This resurfaces in public argument every few years, so hold both sides. The case for switching: a directly-elected executive with a fixed term would end instability from coalition collapses and defections; separation from the legislature could mean ministers chosen for expertise rather than electoral arithmetic; clearer accountability of one visible executive. The case against (and the Assembly's own position): daily answerability to Parliament beats a once-in-five-years verdict; a single national executive concentrates dangerous power in a diverse federation; and the deadlock risk between separately elected branches is exactly what a plural, developing polity cannot afford. The clinching structural point: S.R. Bommai and later cases treat the parliamentary system as part of the basic structure — meaning the switch is very likely not available by amendment at all, only by a new constituent exercise. That last line converts a general-opinion answer into a constitutional-law answer.

06

Distinction between the Indian and British models

India borrowed the Parliamentary system from Britain (Note 02 of Topic 1 already flagged this borrowing), but did not copy it wholesale — five differences are worth holding precisely, since Prelims likes to test "which of these is true only of Britain, not India."

Point of differenceBritainIndia
Form of stateConstitutional monarchy — the Crown is hereditary head of state.Republic — the President is elected, not hereditary (Note 02, Topic 1).
Parliamentary sovereigntyParliament is legally supreme; no court can strike down an Act of Parliament.Constitution is supreme, not Parliament — Parliament's laws are subject to judicial review and the Basic Structure limit (Note 06, Topic 1).
Source of PM's membershipBy convention, the PM sits in the House of Commons, not the Lords.The PM can be a member of either House — Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha — with no constitutional bar.
Shadow cabinetAn institutionalised, salaried Shadow Cabinet formally shadows each government department.No formal, salaried Shadow Cabinet system — opposition scrutiny is less institutionally structured.
Legal status of the CabinetThe Cabinet is a product of convention, not a written law.The Council of Ministers has express constitutional recognition — Articles 74 and 75 name it directly.
07

PYQ analysis

Trend verdict: Prelims tests this note almost entirely as a "define the system correctly" statement-MCQ — confirmed genuine in 2013, 2015, 2017 and 2020, always anchored on Article 75(3)'s collective-responsibility clause or a direct definitional statement. Mains tests the merit/demerit tension directly, confirmed genuine in 2020.

2020Prelims

A Parliamentary System of Government is one in which

  • (a) all political parties in the Parliament are represented in the Government
  • (b) the Government is responsible to the Parliament and can be removed by it
  • (c) the Government is elected by the people and can be removed by them
  • (d) the Government is chosen by the Parliament but cannot be removed by it before completion of a fixed term
Answer: (b)
  • Verdict: direct hit — word-for-word the definition in Section 02.
  • Note how the distractors are built: (a) describes a national/coalition government, (c) describes direct democracy, and (d) describes a fixed-tenure executive, i.e. the Presidential system.
  • The removability test is what defines the parliamentary form.
2017Prelims

The Parliamentary system of government is also known as the "Cabinet system" of government because

  • (a) the Cabinet is appointed directly by the Head of State
  • (b) real executive authority is vested in the Cabinet, which is collectively responsible to the legislature
  • (c) the Cabinet cannot be removed by the legislature during its term
  • (d) the Cabinet consists of members drawn from outside the legislature
Answer: (b)
  • Verdict: direct hit — tests the same collective-responsibility definition from a different angle.
  • Options (c) and (d) both describe features of the Presidential system, which is exactly the contrast Section 03 draws.
2013 & 2015Prelims

Under the Constitution of India, the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to

  • (a) the President of India
  • (b) the Prime Minister
  • (c) the House of the People (Lok Sabha)
  • (d) both Houses of Parliament
Answer: (c)
  • Article 75(3), verbatim. Verdict: direct hit, and the same clause is a genuine Prelims repeater across at least four years (2013, 2015, 2017, 2020).
  • Option (d) is the standing trap: responsibility runs to the directly-elected House only, never to the Rajya Sabha, which is why a no-confidence motion lies only in the Lok Sabha.

M20Practice15 marks · 250 words

Critically evaluate the Parliamentary system of government as adopted by India. Discuss its merits and demerits.

Directive: "Critically evaluate" + explicit merits/demerits. Evaluate = reach a decision from weighed pros and cons. The structure is dictated: merits block, demerits block, verdict. The verdict must actually decide, not trail off.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: why chosen — Ambedkar
  2. Merits
  3. Demerits
  4. Why the alternative is unavailable
  5. Verdict + reform

Model answer

India adopted a parliamentary executive — Articles 74, 75 and 163 — with the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Ambedkar defended the choice in the Assembly as privileging "daily assessment of responsibility" over the periodic assessment a fixed-term presidency allows.

Merits

Continuous accountability — question hour, debates, motions, and ultimately a no-confidence motion under Article 75(3). Harmony between legislature and executive, avoiding the deadlock separately-elected branches produce. Representative flexibility: a multi-member Council accommodates India's linguistic, regional and religious plurality far better than a single President. Responsive government, removable the moment it loses its majority. Shamsher Singh (1974) confirmed the President and Governors as constitutional heads acting on aid and advice.

Demerits

Instability in the coalition era — repeated mid-term collapses between 1989 and 1999. No real separation of powers: a disciplined majority makes Parliament an instrument of the executive, worsened by the Tenth Schedule's whip regime, whose disqualification decisions Kihoto Hollohan (1992) made judicially reviewable while leaving them with a partisan Speaker. Government by amateurs — ministers chosen for electoral arithmetic, not domain expertise. Executive dominance of legislation: declining sitting days, thin committee scrutiny, and contested use of the Money Bill route.

Why the alternative is unavailable

A switch to a presidential system is very likely foreclosed: the parliamentary system has been treated as part of the basic structure, placing it beyond Article 368's reach.

Verdict

The choice remains sound but under-performing; the defect lies in the practice, not the model. Repair means stronger committee scrutiny, guaranteed sitting days, an independent tribunal for anti-defection cases, and a Speaker insulated from party pressure.

◆ One-page recall sheet

Last-mile revision — Parliamentary System & Presidential Government

  • Parliamentary = executive drawn from and accountable to the legislature; Presidential = executive separately elected, fixed term.
  • Article 74: President aided/advised by Council of Ministers headed by PM. Article 75(3): collective responsibility to Lok Sabha.
  • India: dual executive (President nominal, PM+CoM real) — same at States via Articles 163–164.
  • USA is the textbook Presidential case: fixed term, strict separation of powers, impeachment not no-confidence.
  • Merit: harmony of legislature+executive, responsible daily accountability. Demerit: instability, no strict separation of powers.
  • India chose Parliamentary for: familiarity, responsible-government preference, avoiding legislative-executive conflict, representing diversity.
  • Ambedkar: daily assessment of responsibility beats periodic assessment.
  • Indian vs British: Republic not monarchy; Constitution supreme not Parliament; PM can sit in either House; no formal Shadow Cabinet; Council of Ministers has express constitutional status (Britain's is conventional).
  • Genuine Prelims repeater on Art 75(3) collective responsibility: 2013, 2015, 2017, 2020.
