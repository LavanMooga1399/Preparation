State Legislature

Articles 168–212 — Vidhan Sabha and Vidhan Parishad. Structurally the Parliament you already know, with one genuinely distinct feature: an upper house that can be created and abolished at will, and whose powers are weaker than the Rajya Sabha's in every respect.

Articles 168–212 Art 169 — create/abolish a Council Art 171 — the 6 categories No joint sitting Art 200 assent
6States with a Legislative Council — the rest are unicameral
◆ What is genuinely new here

You have already learnt Parliament in three notes. Do not re-learn it. Three things in this chapter have no Union equivalent and account for most of the questions: Article 169, which lets Parliament create or abolish a Legislative Council on a State's own resolution; Article 171, whose six-category composition of a Council looks nothing like the Rajya Sabha's; and the fact that a Legislative Council can delay a Bill but never block it, because there is no joint sitting at State level.

Week 4, Day 6 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 33.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Every State has a Legislative Assembly — the Vidhan Sabha — directly elected, and the house that actually decides who governs. Only some States also have a Legislative Council, the Vidhan Parishad, an indirectly elected second chamber meant to add reflection without adding a veto.

The framers could not agree on whether States needed a second chamber, so they did something unusual: rather than settle it, they made it reversible. Article 169 lets Parliament create a Council for a State, or abolish an existing one, if that State's own Assembly passes a resolution by a special majority. The result is that the number of bicameral States has moved up and down over the decades — Andhra Pradesh abolished its Council in 1985 and revived it in 2007, which is the standard illustration.

Because a Council can be abolished by the very Assembly it is supposed to check, it was never given real power. It can hold up an ordinary Bill for a few months and that is all. Keep that asymmetry in mind and the rest of the chapter follows from it.

01

Structure — Articles 168 to 172

ArticleWhat it provides
Article 168For every State there shall be a legislature consisting of the Governor and one or two Houses. Exactly as at the Union, the head of the executive is part of the legislature — a favourite one-line trap.
Article 169Parliament may by law create or abolish a Legislative Council, if the Legislative Assembly of that State first passes a resolution by a special majority. Such a law is not deemed an amendment under Article 368 and passes by simple majority in Parliament.
Article 170Composition of the Assembly: not more than 500 and not fewer than 60 members, directly elected from territorial constituencies. Goa, Sikkim, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh have lower minimums fixed by separate provision.
Article 171Composition of the Council: not more than one-third of the Assembly's strength, and never fewer than 40. The six-category breakdown is in Section 03.
Article 172Duration: the Assembly runs five years unless dissolved sooner, extendable by one year at a time during a National Emergency and never beyond six months after it ends. The Council is a permanent body — never dissolved, with one-third retiring every two years.
!
Numbers that get swapped. Assembly: max 500, min 60. Council: max one-third of the Assembly, min 40. Candidates routinely apply the one-third rule to the wrong house or quote 40 as the Assembly minimum. And note the mirror to Parliament: the Assembly is the Lok Sabha analogue (dissolvable, five-year term), the Council the Rajya Sabha analogue (permanent, one-third retiring every two years).
02

Creating and abolishing a Council — Article 169

🌱 The mechanism, step by step

Step 1. The State's Legislative Assembly passes a resolution — by a special majority, meaning a majority of the total membership of the Assembly and two-thirds of members present and voting.

Step 2. Parliament then passes a law creating or abolishing the Council. That law needs only a simple majority.

Step 3. Crucially, Parliament is not bound to act on the resolution — it may simply let it lie. Several State resolutions have never been taken up.

The point candidates miss: Article 169 expressly says such a law shall not be deemed an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368, even though it changes the composition of a State legislature. It is one of the standard examples of the Constitution being amendable by simple majority.

Next → the six States that currently have one, and the one that abolished and revived
✚
The six bicameral States: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh. The instructive history is Andhra Pradesh — Council abolished in 1985, revived in 2007, and a fresh abolition resolution passed by its Assembly in 2020 on which Parliament has not acted. That single State demonstrates every feature of Article 169: reversibility, the Assembly's initiative, and Parliament's discretion not to act. Jammu & Kashmir's Council ceased to exist in 2019 on reorganisation, which is a different route entirely — reorganisation, not Article 169.

Composition & presiding officers

Article 171 — the six categories of a Legislative Council

This is the most mechanical set of fractions in the State chapter, and it is asked directly.

ShareElected or nominated by
1/3Elected by members of local bodies — municipalities, district boards and other local authorities.
1/3Elected by members of the Legislative Assembly, from among persons who are not Assembly members.
1/12Elected by graduates of three years' standing residing in the State.
1/12Elected by teachers of at least three years' standing in institutions not lower than secondary school.
1/6Nominated by the Governor from persons with special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, co-operative movement and social service.
!
Two facts that decide questions here. (1) The Governor's nomination list has five fields — it includes co-operative movement, which the President's Rajya Sabha list under Article 80(3) does not. That single-word gap is a ready-made pairing question. (2) Only five-sixths of a Council is elected and the manner of election of that portion may be altered by Parliament — so the fractions are not entrenched. Add them up as a check: 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/12 + 1/12 + 1/6 = 1.

Presiding officers, and how they differ from Parliament's

04

Legislative procedure — and why the Council is weak

Bill typeWhat the Legislative Council can do
Ordinary BillWhere a Bill originates in the Assembly and the Council rejects it, delays it beyond three months, or amends it unacceptably, the Assembly may pass it again. The Council then has only one month, after which the Bill is deemed passed. Maximum delay: four months. Where a Bill originates in the Council and the Assembly rejects it, the Bill simply dies.
Money BillCan originate only in the Assembly. The Council must return it within 14 days and may only recommend; the Assembly may reject every recommendation. Inaction means deemed passage. Identical to the Rajya Sabha's position.
Constitutional Amendment BillWhere State ratification is required under Article 368, the ratifying resolution is passed by the Legislative Assembly. The Council has no role.
ConfidenceThe Council cannot remove the government. Collective responsibility under Article 164(2) runs to the Assembly alone.
!
The single sharpest contrast in this note. When the two Houses of Parliament disagree on an ordinary Bill, the President may summon a joint sitting under Article 108 and the matter is decided by combined vote. There is no joint sitting at State level. None. The Assembly simply outlasts the Council after four months. This is why the Rajya Sabha is a genuine second chamber and a Legislative Council is a revising chamber with a delaying power — and it is the reason a Council can be abolished under Article 169 without anyone claiming the federal structure has been damaged.
✚
Assent — Article 200, already familiar. Once both Houses (or the Assembly alone in a unicameral State) pass a Bill, it goes to the Governor, who may assent, withhold assent, return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration, or reserve it for the President. If returned and passed again, the Governor must assent. Reserving a Bill is a discretionary function, and the timeline question was litigated through 2025 — the full treatment sits in Topic 6, Note 01.

Privileges, procedure and the Governor's address

✚
Article 194 gives State legislatures the same privileges as Article 105 gives Parliament — freedom of speech in the House, freedom from arrest in civil cases during the session and 40 days either side, and immunity for publication under authority. Article 212 mirrors Article 122: courts cannot question proceedings for procedural irregularity. And Article 176 mirrors Article 87 — the Governor addresses the first session after each general election and the first session of each year. The privileges chapter, including Sita Soren (2024), applies to State legislators through Article 194 in exactly the same terms.

  • All three are instruments of legislative control, and the same devices operate in a State legislature through its own Rules of Procedure.

Practice · Statements

With reference to Article 169, consider the following statements:

  1. Parliament may create or abolish a Legislative Council only if the Legislative Assembly of that State passes a resolution by a special majority.
  2. Such a law of Parliament is deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368.
  3. Parliament is bound to act on a resolution passed by the State Assembly.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (a) — only one
  • Statement 1 is correct — the Assembly's special-majority resolution is a precondition.
  • Statement 2 is wrong. Article 169 expressly says such a law is not deemed an amendment for Article 368 purposes; it passes by simple majority. This is a standard example of amendment by simple majority.
  • Statement 3 is wrong. Parliament may act; it is not obliged to. Andhra Pradesh's 2020 abolition resolution is still pending.
Practice · Negative

Which one of the following statements about a State Legislative Council is NOT correct?

  • (a) Its strength cannot exceed one-third of the Legislative Assembly's strength
  • (b) It is a permanent body with one-third of members retiring every two years
  • (c) Its Chairman is elected by the Council from among its own members
  • (d) Where the two Houses disagree on an ordinary Bill, the Governor may summon a joint sitting
Answer: (d) is NOT correct
  • There is no joint sitting at State level. Article 108's joint-sitting device exists only for Parliament. A State Assembly simply outlasts the Council after a maximum delay of four months.
  • (a), (b) and (c) are all accurate — and (c) is the key contrast with the Rajya Sabha, whose Chairman is the Vice-President and not a member.
  • Circle the NOT first, then test each option normally and flip at the end.
Practice · How many

Consider the following States:

  1. Karnataka
  2. Telangana
  3. Odisha
  4. Bihar

How many of the above currently have a Legislative Council?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c) — only three
  • The six bicameral States are Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh. Of those listed, Karnataka, Telangana and Bihar qualify.
  • Odisha does not have one — its Assembly passed a resolution seeking a Council, but Parliament has not enacted the law. Passing a resolution is not the same as having a Council.
  • Learn the six as a closed list. How-many questions give no credit for knowing most of it.
Practice · Pairs

Consider the following pairs of category and share in a Legislative Council:

  1. Elected by members of local bodies — one-third
  2. Elected by graduates of three years' standing — one-twelfth
  3. Nominated by the Governor — one-sixth

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (c) — all three
  • All correct. The full set under Article 171: local bodies 1/3, MLAs 1/3, graduates 1/12, teachers 1/12, Governor's nominees 1/6.
  • Check by addition — the five shares must total 1. If a question offers fractions that do not sum to one, at least one pair is wrong.
  • The Governor's nominee list has five fields including co-operative movement; the President's Rajya Sabha list under Art 80(3) has only four.
Practice · Statements

Consider the following statements regarding an ordinary Bill in a bicameral State legislature:

  1. A Bill originating in the Legislative Council and rejected by the Assembly comes to an end.
  2. The Legislative Council can delay a Bill passed by the Assembly for a maximum of four months.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (c) — both
  • Statement 1 is correct. A Council-originated Bill rejected by the Assembly simply dies — there is no mechanism to revive it.
  • Statement 2 is correct. Three months on first passage plus one month on second passage: a maximum delay of four months, after which the Bill is deemed passed.
  • The asymmetry is the whole point: the Council delays, the Assembly decides.
Practice · Assertion–Reason

Statement-I: A State Legislative Council is a far weaker chamber than the Rajya Sabha.

Statement-II: A Legislative Council can be abolished by ordinary law on a resolution of the very Assembly it is meant to review.

Which one of the following is correct?

  • (a) Both are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (b) Both are correct but Statement-II is not the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
  • (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct
Answer: (a)
  • Both correct, and the second explains the first. A chamber that the Assembly can vote out of existence under Article 169 was never going to be given a real veto.
  • The Rajya Sabha, by contrast, cannot be abolished at all, holds an absolute veto on Constitutional Amendment Bills, and has exclusive powers under Articles 249 and 312.
  • When an Assertion–Reason pair offers a structural cause for a structural fact, it is usually (a) — but confirm the causation rather than assuming it.

Mains Questions

Written to the MAINS_ANSWER_CRAFT.md standard.

M32Practice · Week 415 marks · 250 words

"A State Legislative Council is a chamber of revision without the power to revise." Critically examine, and assess whether Article 169 should be used more readily.

Directive: "Critically examine" + a second tail. Two tasks: test the claim, then answer the reform question. An answer that does only the first is capped.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 169's reversible design
  2. The case that it cannot revise
  3. The case for retention
  4. Should abolition be readier?
  5. Verdict

Model answer

The framers could not settle whether States needed a second chamber, so they made the question reversible: under Article 169 Parliament may create or abolish a Legislative Council on a special-majority resolution of the State's own Assembly, by a law that is expressly not an amendment under Article 368. Six States have one today.

Why it cannot truly revise

Its power is delay, not decision. An ordinary Bill can be held for three months and, on second passage, a further one month — after which it is deemed passed. There is no joint sitting at State level, so disagreement never goes to a combined vote as under Article 108. A Money Bill may be held 14 days. Ratification under Article 368 is done by the Assembly alone, and the government answers only to the Assembly under Article 164(2). A chamber the Assembly can abolish was never going to be given a veto.

The case for retention

It admits expertise the Assembly lacks — Article 171 reserves a sixth to gubernatorial nominees in literature, science, art, co-operative movement and social service, and a sixth to graduates and teachers. It gives representation to local bodies, offers a route back for able people who lose an election, and imposes a pause on hurried legislation.

Should abolition be readier?

The honest criticisms are cost and patronage — Councils have become accommodation for defeated candidates. But the answer is reform of Article 171's electoral colleges, not abolition by convenience: a chamber abolished whenever it inconveniences a majority is not a check at all. Andhra Pradesh's abolition in 1985, revival in 2007 and fresh resolution in 2020 illustrate the instability.

The claim therefore stands as description and fails as prescription: the Council cannot revise, but the cure is to make it worth revising with.

Last-mile revision — State Legislature

  • Art 168 — legislature = Governor + one or two Houses. The Governor is part of it.
  • Art 169 — Parliament creates/abolishes a Council on the Assembly's special majority resolution. NOT an Art 368 amendment; Parliament passes it by simple majority and is not bound to act.
  • Art 170 — Assembly: max 500, min 60. Art 171 — Council: max 1/3 of the Assembly, min 40.
  • Art 172 — Assembly 5 years, extendable 1 year at a time in a National Emergency. Council permanent, 1/3 retire every 2 years.
  • Art 171 shares: local bodies 1/3 · MLAs 1/3 · graduates 1/12 · teachers 1/12 · Governor's nominees 1/6. They sum to 1.
  • Governor's nominee fields = five, including co-operative movement. President's Rajya Sabha list (Art 80(3)) = four, without it.
  • Six bicameral States: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh. AP abolished 1985, revived 2007, resolution again 2020.
  • No joint sitting at State level. Council delays an ordinary Bill 3 months + 1 month = 4 months max; a Money Bill 14 days. A Council-originated Bill rejected by the Assembly dies.
  • Age: Assembly 25, Council 30. Council's Chairman is a member of the House — unlike the Rajya Sabha's.
  • Speaker/Chairman removal: effective majority, 14 days' notice.
  • Art 194 privileges (=105) · Art 212 no judicial questioning of procedure (=122) · Art 176 Governor's address (=87) · Art 200 assent.
  • Art 368 ratification by States is done by the Assembly alone; the Council has no role.
