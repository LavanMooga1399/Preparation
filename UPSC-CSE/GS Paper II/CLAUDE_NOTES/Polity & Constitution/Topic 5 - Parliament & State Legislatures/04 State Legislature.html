State Legislature

Articles 168–212 — Vidhan Sabha and Vidhan Parishad. Structurally the Parliament you already know, with one genuinely distinct feature: an upper house that can be created and abolished at will, and whose powers are weaker than the Rajya Sabha's in every respect.

Articles 168–212 Art 169 — create/abolish a Council Art 171 — the 6 categories No joint sitting Art 200 assent
6States with a Legislative Council — the rest are unicameral
◆ What is genuinely new here

You have already learnt Parliament in three notes. Do not re-learn it. Three things in this chapter have no Union equivalent and account for most of the questions: Article 169, which lets Parliament create or abolish a Legislative Council on a State's own resolution; Article 171, whose six-category composition of a Council looks nothing like the Rajya Sabha's; and the fact that a Legislative Council can delay a Bill but never block it, because there is no joint sitting at State level.

Week 4, Day 6 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 33.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Every State has a Legislative Assembly — the Vidhan Sabha — directly elected, and the house that actually decides who governs. Only some States also have a Legislative Council, the Vidhan Parishad, an indirectly elected second chamber meant to add reflection without adding a veto.

The framers could not agree on whether States needed a second chamber, so they did something unusual: rather than settle it, they made it reversible. Article 169 lets Parliament create a Council for a State, or abolish an existing one, if that State's own Assembly passes a resolution by a special majority. The result is that the number of bicameral States has moved up and down over the decades — Andhra Pradesh abolished its Council in 1985 and revived it in 2007, which is the standard illustration.

Because a Council can be abolished by the very Assembly it is supposed to check, it was never given real power. It can hold up an ordinary Bill for a few months and that is all. Keep that asymmetry in mind and the rest of the chapter follows from it.

01

Structure — Articles 168 to 172

ArticleWhat it provides
Article 168For every State there shall be a legislature consisting of the Governor and one or two Houses. Exactly as at the Union, the head of the executive is part of the legislature — a favourite one-line trap.
Article 169Parliament may by law create or abolish a Legislative Council, if the Legislative Assembly of that State first passes a resolution by a special majority. Such a law is not deemed an amendment under Article 368 and passes by simple majority in Parliament.
Article 170Composition of the Assembly: not more than 500 and not fewer than 60 members, directly elected from territorial constituencies. Goa, Sikkim, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh have lower minimums fixed by separate provision.
Article 171Composition of the Council: not more than one-third of the Assembly's strength, and never fewer than 40. The six-category breakdown is in Section 03.
Article 172Duration: the Assembly runs five years unless dissolved sooner, extendable by one year at a time during a National Emergency and never beyond six months after it ends. The Council is a permanent body — never dissolved, with one-third retiring every two years.
!
Numbers that get swapped. Assembly: max 500, min 60. Council: max one-third of the Assembly, min 40. Candidates routinely apply the one-third rule to the wrong house or quote 40 as the Assembly minimum. And note the mirror to Parliament: the Assembly is the Lok Sabha analogue (dissolvable, five-year term), the Council the Rajya Sabha analogue (permanent, one-third retiring every two years).
02

Creating and abolishing a Council — Article 169

🌱 The mechanism, step by step

Step 1. The State's Legislative Assembly passes a resolution — by a special majority, meaning a majority of the total membership of the Assembly and two-thirds of members present and voting.

Step 2. Parliament then passes a law creating or abolishing the Council. That law needs only a simple majority.

Step 3. Crucially, Parliament is not bound to act on the resolution — it may simply let it lie. Several State resolutions have never been taken up.

The point candidates miss: Article 169 expressly says such a law shall not be deemed an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368, even though it changes the composition of a State legislature. It is one of the standard examples of the Constitution being amendable by simple majority.

Next → the six States that currently have one, and the one that abolished and revived
✚
The six bicameral States: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh. The instructive history is Andhra Pradesh — Council abolished in 1985, revived in 2007, and a fresh abolition resolution passed by its Assembly in 2020 on which Parliament has not acted. That single State demonstrates every feature of Article 169: reversibility, the Assembly's initiative, and Parliament's discretion not to act. Jammu & Kashmir's Council ceased to exist in 2019 on reorganisation, which is a different route entirely — reorganisation, not Article 169.

Composition & presiding officers

Article 171 — the six categories of a Legislative Council

This is the most mechanical set of fractions in the State chapter, and it is asked directly.

ShareElected or nominated by
1/3Elected by members of local bodies — municipalities, district boards and other local authorities.
1/3Elected by members of the Legislative Assembly, from among persons who are not Assembly members.
1/12Elected by graduates of three years' standing residing in the State.
1/12Elected by teachers of at least three years' standing in institutions not lower than secondary school.
1/6Nominated by the Governor from persons with special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, co-operative movement and social service.
!
Two facts that decide questions here. (1) The Governor's nomination list has five fields — it includes co-operative movement, which the President's Rajya Sabha list under Article 80(3) does not. That single-word gap is a ready-made pairing question. (2) Only five-sixths of a Council is elected and the manner of election of that portion may be altered by Parliament — so the fractions are not entrenched. Add them up as a check: 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/12 + 1/12 + 1/6 = 1.

04

Legislative procedure — and why the Council is weak

Bill typeWhat the Legislative Council can do
Ordinary BillWhere a Bill originates in the Assembly and the Council rejects it, delays it beyond three months, or amends it unacceptably, the Assembly may pass it again. The Council then has only one month, after which the Bill is deemed passed. Maximum delay: four months. Where a Bill originates in the Council and the Assembly rejects it, the Bill simply dies.
Money BillCan originate only in the Assembly. The Council must return it within 14 days and may only recommend; the Assembly may reject every recommendation. Inaction means deemed passage. Identical to the Rajya Sabha's position.
Constitutional Amendment BillWhere State ratification is required under Article 368, the ratifying resolution is passed by the Legislative Assembly. The Council has no role.
ConfidenceThe Council cannot remove the government. Collective responsibility under Article 164(2) runs to the Assembly alone.
!
The single sharpest contrast in this note. When the two Houses of Parliament disagree on an ordinary Bill, the President may summon a joint sitting under Article 108 and the matter is decided by combined vote. There is no joint sitting at State level. None. The Assembly simply outlasts the Council after four months. This is why the Rajya Sabha is a genuine second chamber and a Legislative Council is a revising chamber with a delaying power — and it is the reason a Council can be abolished under Article 169 without anyone claiming the federal structure has been damaged.
✚
Assent — Article 200, already familiar. Once both Houses (or the Assembly alone in a unicameral State) pass a Bill, it goes to the Governor, who may assent, withhold assent, return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration, or reserve it for the President. If returned and passed again, the Governor cannot withhold assent — but after the November 2025 advisory opinion he is not bound to assent either: on the second round he may assent or reserve the Bill for the President. Reserving a Bill is a discretionary function, and the timeline question was litigated through 2025 — the full treatment sits in Topic 6, Note 01.

Privileges, procedure and the Governor's address

✚
Article 194 gives State legislatures the same privileges as Article 105 gives Parliament — freedom of speech in the House, freedom from arrest in civil cases during the session and 40 days either side, and immunity for publication under authority. Article 212 mirrors Article 122: courts cannot question proceedings for procedural irregularity. And Article 176 mirrors Article 87 — the Governor addresses the first session after each general election and the first session of each year. The privileges chapter, including Sita Soren (2024), applies to State legislators through Article 194 in exactly the same terms.

Membership, sessions, voting & committees

◆ Added for Week 5, Days 1–2

The Week 5 plan returns to State Legislature for two full days and lists a wider surface than Week 4 did — qualifications and disqualifications, presiding officers by Article number, introduction of Bills, committee stages, voting procedures and the Governor's role in the legislative process. This section covers that surface. Nothing here duplicates Sections 01–04; read it as the machinery chapter that sits underneath them.

Qualifications and disqualifications

ArticleProvisionDetail worth carrying
173Qualifications for membershipCitizen of India; makes and subscribes the prescribed oath or affirmation before a person authorised by the Election Commission; not less than 25 for the Assembly and not less than 30 for the Council; plus such other qualifications as Parliament — not the State legislature — may prescribe.
191DisqualificationsHolding an office of profit under the Union or a State (other than an office the State legislature has by law exempted); unsound mind so declared by a court; undischarged insolvent; not a citizen or has voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship; disqualified under any law made by Parliament — plus disqualification on defection under the Tenth Schedule, in Art 191(2).
192Who decidesThe question is decided by the Governor, whose decision is final — but he shall obtain the opinion of the Election Commission and shall act according to that opinion. So the Governor is the deciding authority in form and the Commission is in substance. Defection cases are the exception: they are decided by the Speaker or Chairman under the Tenth Schedule, not by the Governor.
190Vacation of seatsA member vacates on becoming a member of both Houses, or of the legislature of two States, or on disqualification, or on resignation to the Speaker or Chairman. The House may declare the seat vacant if a member is absent for sixty days without permission.
193PenaltyA person sitting or voting while disqualified, or before taking the oath, is liable to a penalty of ₹500 per day, recoverable as a debt due to the State.
!
Two mirrors, one broken. The State provisions mirror the Union almost exactly — 173↔84, 190↔101, 191↔102, 192↔103, 193↔104. The break is in who decides: at the Union level the President decides a disqualification question on the Election Commission's opinion (Art 103); at State level the Governor does (Art 192). In both cases the deciding authority is bound by the Commission's opinion, and in both cases defection is carved out and goes to the presiding officer. A statement putting the Governor in charge of a defection case is false.

Presiding officers by Article — and the pro-tem question

ArticleProvision
178Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Legislative Assembly — chosen by the Assembly from among its members.
179Vacation, resignation and removal. A Speaker or Deputy Speaker may be removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Assembly, after not less than fourteen days' notice. The Speaker resigns to the Deputy Speaker, and the Deputy Speaker to the Speaker.
180Power of the Deputy Speaker, or another person, to perform the Speaker's duties.
181The Speaker or Deputy Speaker shall not preside while a resolution for their own removal is under consideration — but may speak in and take part in the proceedings, and may vote in the first instance, though not exercise a casting vote.
182–184The mirror provisions for the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Legislative Council.
185The Chairman or Deputy Chairman likewise does not preside while their own removal is considered.
186Salaries and allowances of the presiding officers — charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State.
187Secretariat of the State legislature — separate staff for the House, so it is not dependent on the State executive.
188Oath or affirmation by members, made before the Governor or a person appointed by him.
◆
There is no "pro-tem Speaker" in the Constitution — at either level. The office is a matter of convention and rules of procedure. At State level the Governor appoints a pro-tem Speaker, usually the senior-most member, to administer the oath to newly elected members and preside over the election of the Speaker. Compare the Union: the President appoints the pro-tem Speaker of the Lok Sabha. In both cases the appointment is by the constitutional head, not by the House. A statement locating the pro-tem Speaker in a particular Article is false — there is none.
!
Article 181 is a small provision with a big trap. While a removal resolution against the Speaker is under consideration, the Speaker shall not preside — but is entitled to speak and to vote in the first instance. What is lost is only the casting vote. Prelims 2024 asked exactly this at the Union level (Art 96, the identical provision for the Lok Sabha) and the answer was that only the first of three statements was correct. Learn the three separately: cannot preside — true; cannot speak — false; cannot vote in the first instance — false.
◆
Reserved seats — Article 332, added after the Week 5 paper. Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in every State Legislative Assembly, in proportion to their share of the State's population — the same proportionality rule as Article 330 for the Lok Sabha. Reservation was originally to run for ten years from the commencement of the Constitution and has been extended by successive amendments; the 104th Amendment, 2020 extended it to 25 January 2030 and simultaneously discontinued the nomination of Anglo-Indians. Two facts a setter would use: the reservation is population-proportional, not a fixed number, and the Anglo-Indian nomination is gone from both the Lok Sabha and the State Assemblies.

Sessions, quorum and voting

ArticleProvisionNumber to remember
174Sessions, prorogation and dissolutionThe Governor summons the House. Six months shall not intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. He may prorogue the Houses and dissolve the Assembly — never the Council, which is a permanent body.
175Governor's right to address and send messagesHe may address either House at any time, and send messages.
176Special addressThe Governor shall address the Assembly — both Houses assembled together where there is a Council — at the commencement of the first session after each general election and at the commencement of the first session of each year. Note "shall": this one is obligatory, whereas Art 175 is discretionary.
189Voting, vacancies and quorumQuestions are determined by a majority of members present and voting, other than the Speaker or Chairman, who has no vote in the first instance but a casting vote in the event of a tie. Proceedings are valid notwithstanding a vacancy or the participation of a person not entitled to sit. Quorum is ten members or one-tenth of the total membership, whichever is greater.
!
Quorum is a numbers-with-a-twin item. At State level (Art 189) the quorum is ten members or one-tenth of the total, whichever is greater — the floor of ten has no Union equivalent. At the Union level (Art 100) it is simply one-tenth of the total membership. Compare the same shape as the ministry-size rule you already know: the State version carries a floor, the Union version does not.
◆
How a vote is actually taken. The presiding officer first puts the question and takes a voice vote — "ayes" and "noes". If a member challenges it, a division is held, recorded by automatic vote recorder, by slips, or by members going into lobbies. A secret ballot is not used for ordinary legislative business at State level. The Speaker's casting vote is exercised only on a tie, and by convention in favour of the status quo — that is, to keep the existing position rather than to change it.

Bills — introduction and the committee stage

StageWhat happens
Introduction — first readingThe Bill is introduced, sometimes after leave of the House, and published in the Gazette. No debate on the merits at this stage. A Money Bill under Art 199 can be introduced only in the Assembly, and only on the Governor's recommendation.
Second reading — general discussionThe House may take one of four courses: consider the Bill at once, refer it to a Select Committee of that House, refer it to a Joint Committee of both Houses, or circulate it for eliciting public opinion.
Committee stageThe committee examines the Bill clause by clause, may take evidence, and reports back with amendments. It cannot alter the Bill's underlying principle, which the House approved at the second reading.
Consideration stageThe House considers the Bill as reported, clause by clause, and votes on amendments.
Third readingDebate is confined to accepting or rejecting the Bill as a whole; only formal or verbal amendments are allowed. On passage it is transmitted to the Council, where the same process is repeated.
AssentArticle 200 — the Governor's four-way choice, covered in Section 04. Bills reserved go to the President under Article 201.
!
Article 201 is the one most often skipped, and it contains an asymmetry. Where a Bill is reserved for the President, he may assent or withhold assent. If it is not a Money Bill he may instead direct the Governor to return it to the House for reconsideration within six months. And here is the asymmetry: even if the legislature passes it again, the President is not bound to assent. Contrast the Governor's own position under Art 200, where withholding is closed off on the second round. There is also no time limit on the President's decision under Art 201 — a point the November 2025 opinion confirmed by refusing to read one in.

The State budget — and the Council's exact disability

Stage / itemDetail
Annual Financial StatementArticle 202 — the Governor causes it to be laid before both Houses. So the Council does see the budget.
General discussionHeld in both Houses. The Legislative Council may discuss the budget fully.
Demands for grants — Art 203Voted on by the Legislative Assembly alone. The Council has no vote. The Assembly may assent, refuse, or reduce a demand — and no demand can be made except on the Governor's recommendation.
Cut motionsPolicy cut (reduce to ₹1) · economy cut (reduce by a specified amount) · token cut (reduce by ₹100, to air a specific grievance). Moved in the Assembly only.
Charged expenditure — Art 202(3)Not submitted to the vote of the legislature, though it may be discussed. Includes the Governor's emoluments, the Speaker's and Chairman's salaries, debt charges, High Court judges' salaries and pensions, and sums needed to satisfy a court decree.
Appropriation Bill — Art 204No money is withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund of the State except under an appropriation made by law. No amendment may be proposed that would vary the amount or alter the destination of a grant, or vary the amount of charged expenditure.
Finance BillGives effect to the taxation proposals. A Money Bill under Art 199, so it follows the 14-day route through the Council.
Vote on account — Art 206Advance grant pending the passage of the Appropriation Bill. Art 206 also provides for votes of credit and exceptional grants.
!
The Council's disability, stated precisely — a favourite mixing question. A Legislative Council may discuss the budget. It cannot vote on the demands for grants, cannot move cut motions on them, and cannot reject or reduce a grant. That is exactly the Rajya Sabha's position, and the examiner builds the question by mixing "discuss" with "vote". Hold the pair: discussion yes, voting no.

Second edge in the same area: charged expenditure is non-votable but fully discussable. A statement saying it "cannot be discussed" is false.

Committees of a State legislature

CommitteeWhat to know
Public Accounts CommitteeExamines the Accountant General's audit report on the State's accounts — the State analogue of the PAC examining the CAG's report. Members from the Assembly, and in bicameral States from the Council as associate members who cannot vote.
Estimates CommitteeExamines the State budget estimates and suggests economies. Drawn entirely from the Assembly — as at the Union level, where the Estimates Committee has no Rajya Sabha members.
Committee on Public UndertakingsExamines the reports and accounts of State public undertakings.
Select CommitteeAd hoc, constituted for a single Bill, members from one House, and it ceases to exist on presenting its report.
Joint CommitteeAd hoc, but drawn from both Houses — available only in a bicameral State.
Business Advisory, Rules, Privileges, Subordinate Legislation, Petitions, Government AssurancesStanding committees, permanent and reconstituted periodically, mirroring the Lok Sabha's structure.
◆
The two axes to keep separate. A committee is standing or ad hoc on one axis (does it exist permanently?) and select or joint on another (does it draw from one House or both?). These are independent — a Select Committee is ad hoc and single-House; a Joint Committee is ad hoc and both-House; the Business Advisory Committee is standing and single-House. Questions are built by crossing the axes wrongly, for instance by calling a Select Committee a standing committee.

Anti-defection at State level, and the ordinance power

!
Tenth Schedule — the State facts that differ from what you learnt for Parliament. Disqualification on defection is decided by the Speaker of the Assembly or the Chairman of the Council, and that decision is subject to judicial review (Kihoto Hollohan, 1992). The 91st Amendment, 2003 deleted the exemption for a split of one-third; only a merger of two-thirds now survives as a defence. A defector is also barred from being a Minister for the remaining term — Art 164(1B). And note the Nabam Rebia (2016) holding: a Speaker who is himself facing a removal notice under Art 179(c) cannot proceed to decide disqualification petitions.
◆
Article 213 — the Governor's ordinance power, the State mirror of Article 123. Available when the legislature is not in session and the Governor is satisfied that immediate action is necessary. An ordinance has the same force as an Act, must be laid before the House on reassembly, and ceases to operate six weeks from reassembly — so the maximum life is about six months and six weeks. Three situations require the President's prior instructions: where a Bill containing the same provisions would have required the President's previous sanction for introduction; where the Governor would have deemed it necessary to reserve such a Bill for the President; and where an Act containing the same provisions would have been invalid without the President's assent. An ordinance cannot amend the Constitution — the same limit as Art 123.

Prelims Questions

2013UPSC Prelims · Statements

The Parliament of India exercises control over the functions of the Council of Ministers through

  1. Adjournment motion
  2. Question hour
  3. Supplementary questions

Select the correct answer using the codes given below:

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 and 3 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (d) — all three
  • All three are instruments of legislative control, and the same devices operate in a State legislature through its own Rules of Procedure.
  • Another genuine "all correct" answer from a real paper — worth registering, given the instinct to reject option (d).
Practice · Statements

With reference to Article 169, consider the following statements:

  1. Parliament may create or abolish a Legislative Council only if the Legislative Assembly of that State passes a resolution by a special majority.
  2. Such a law of Parliament is deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368.
  3. Parliament is bound to act on a resolution passed by the State Assembly.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (a) — only one
  • Statement 1 is correct — the Assembly's special-majority resolution is a precondition.
  • Statement 2 is wrong. Article 169 expressly says such a law is not deemed an amendment for Article 368 purposes; it passes by simple majority. This is a standard example of amendment by simple majority.
  • Statement 3 is wrong. Parliament may act; it is not obliged to. Andhra Pradesh's 2020 abolition resolution is still pending.
Practice · Negative

Which one of the following statements about a State Legislative Council is NOT correct?

  • (a) Its strength cannot exceed one-third of the Legislative Assembly's strength
  • (b) It is a permanent body with one-third of members retiring every two years
  • (c) Its Chairman is elected by the Council from among its own members
  • (d) Where the two Houses disagree on an ordinary Bill, the Governor may summon a joint sitting
Answer: (d) is NOT correct
  • There is no joint sitting at State level. Article 108's joint-sitting device exists only for Parliament. A State Assembly simply outlasts the Council after a maximum delay of four months.
  • (a), (b) and (c) are all accurate — and (c) is the key contrast with the Rajya Sabha, whose Chairman is the Vice-President and not a member.
  • Circle the NOT first, then test each option normally and flip at the end.
Practice · How many

Consider the following States:

  1. Karnataka
  2. Telangana
  3. Odisha
  4. Bihar

How many of the above currently have a Legislative Council?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c) — only three
  • The six bicameral States are Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh. Of those listed, Karnataka, Telangana and Bihar qualify.
  • Odisha does not have one — its Assembly passed a resolution seeking a Council, but Parliament has not enacted the law. Passing a resolution is not the same as having a Council.
  • Learn the six as a closed list. How-many questions give no credit for knowing most of it.
Practice · Pairs

Consider the following pairs of category and share in a Legislative Council:

  1. Elected by members of local bodies — one-third
  2. Elected by graduates of three years' standing — one-twelfth
  3. Nominated by the Governor — one-sixth

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (c) — all three
  • All correct. The full set under Article 171: local bodies 1/3, MLAs 1/3, graduates 1/12, teachers 1/12, Governor's nominees 1/6.
  • Check by addition — the five shares must total 1. If a question offers fractions that do not sum to one, at least one pair is wrong.
  • The Governor's nominee list has five fields including co-operative movement; the President's Rajya Sabha list under Art 80(3) has only four.
Practice · Statements

Consider the following statements regarding an ordinary Bill in a bicameral State legislature:

  1. A Bill originating in the Legislative Council and rejected by the Assembly comes to an end.
  2. The Legislative Council can delay a Bill passed by the Assembly for a maximum of four months.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (c) — both
  • Statement 1 is correct. A Council-originated Bill rejected by the Assembly simply dies — there is no mechanism to revive it.
  • Statement 2 is correct. Three months on first passage plus one month on second passage: a maximum delay of four months, after which the Bill is deemed passed.
  • The asymmetry is the whole point: the Council delays, the Assembly decides.
Practice · Assertion–Reason

Statement-I: A State Legislative Council is a far weaker chamber than the Rajya Sabha.

Statement-II: A Legislative Council can be abolished by ordinary law on a resolution of the very Assembly it is meant to review.

Which one of the following is correct?

  • (a) Both are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (b) Both are correct but Statement-II is not the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
  • (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct
Answer: (a)
  • Both correct, and the second explains the first. A chamber that the Assembly can vote out of existence under Article 169 was never going to be given a real veto.
  • The Rajya Sabha, by contrast, cannot be abolished at all, holds an absolute veto on Constitutional Amendment Bills, and has exclusive powers under Articles 249 and 312.
  • When an Assertion–Reason pair offers a structural cause for a structural fact, it is usually (a) — but confirm the causation rather than assuming it.

(C) Trap statements — True or False

◆ Why this bucket exists, and how to work it

Twenty-one of the fifty questions in the Week 4 paper were “how many of the above” — 42 per cent — and that format scored 52 per cent. It cannot be solved by elimination: every statement has to be judged on its own. So the skill the paper actually rewards is not answering questions, it is marking a single statement true or false.

That is all this bucket is. 20 statements from this note, one at a time. Decide before you click. If you find yourself reasoning about how many ought to be true, stop — that instinct is what the format punishes.

Roughly half are false, and most of the false ones are wrong by one word: an authority, a number, a fraction, or a qualifier. That is how this setter builds them.

Trap 1 · True or False

The State Legislature consists of the Legislative Assembly and, where it exists, the Legislative Council.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Incomplete, and therefore false. Article 168 makes the State Legislature the Governor plus one or two Houses. Dropping the Governor is the standard trap.
Trap 2 · True or False

A Legislative Council is created or abolished by Parliament by law, but only after the Legislative Assembly passes a resolution.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 169. Parliament acts, but only on the Assembly's initiative.
Trap 3 · True or False

The Assembly resolution under Article 169 requires a simple majority.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It requires a special majority — a majority of the total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting.
Trap 4 · True or False

A law made under Article 169 is deemed to be a constitutional amendment under Article 368.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Expressly not. Article 169(3) says such a law shall not be deemed an amendment for the purposes of Article 368.
Trap 5 · True or False

One-sixth of the members of a Legislative Council are nominated by the Governor.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • And from five fields — literature, science, art, co-operative movement and social service. The President's Rajya Sabha list under Art 80(3) has only four.
Trap 6 · True or False

The fields from which the Governor nominates to the Council are the same as those from which the President nominates to the Rajya Sabha.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Art 171(5) has five and includes the co-operative movement; Art 80(3) has four and does not.
Trap 7 · True or False

The Chairman of a Legislative Council is elected by the Council from among its own members.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • This is the contrast with the Rajya Sabha, whose Chairman is the Vice-President and not a member of the House.
Trap 8 · True or False

The quorum of a House of a State legislature is one-tenth of its total membership.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Article 189 — ten members or one-tenth of the total, whichever is greater. The floor of ten has no Union equivalent; Art 100 is one-tenth flat.
Trap 9 · True or False

A resolution to remove the Speaker of a Legislative Assembly requires fourteen days' notice.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 179, and the resolution must be passed by a majority of all the then members — an effective majority.
Trap 10 · True or False

While a resolution for his removal is under consideration, the Speaker may not speak in the House.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Article 181. He may speak and take part, and may vote in the first instance. He loses only the chair and the casting vote.
Trap 11 · True or False

The Speaker has no vote in the first instance but exercises a casting vote in the event of a tie.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 189. The casting vote is by convention exercised in favour of the status quo.
Trap 12 · True or False

A question of disqualification of a member of a State legislature is decided by the Governor, who must act according to the opinion of the Election Commission.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 192. The Governor decides in form; the Commission decides in substance.
Trap 13 · True or False

A question of disqualification on the ground of defection is also decided by the Governor.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • That is the carve-out. Defection goes to the Speaker or Chairman under the Tenth Schedule, and the decision is subject to judicial review after Kihoto Hollohan (1992).
Trap 14 · True or False

The decision of the Speaker that a Bill is a Money Bill is final.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 199(3), in a State with a Legislative Council. At the Union level the finality of such certification was referred to a larger bench in Rojer Mathew (2019).
Trap 15 · True or False

A Legislative Council may discuss the budget but cannot vote on the demands for grants.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Exactly the Rajya Sabha's position. It also cannot move cut motions or reject a grant.
Trap 16 · True or False

Charged expenditure can neither be voted upon nor discussed by the State legislature.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It is non-votable but fully discussable. The examiner builds the question by joining the two.
Trap 17 · True or False

The Governor must address the Legislative Assembly at the commencement of the first session of each year.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 176 uses shall. Article 175, by contrast, says he may address either House at any time.
Trap 18 · True or False

An ordinance promulgated by the Governor ceases to operate six weeks from the date of its promulgation.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Six weeks from the reassembly of the legislature, not from promulgation — Article 213. Maximum life is therefore about six months and six weeks.
Trap 19 · True or False

The Estimates Committee of a State legislature includes members of the Legislative Council.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It is drawn entirely from the Assembly, as at the Union level. Council members may be associated with the Public Accounts Committee, but as associates who cannot vote.
Trap 20 · True or False

A Select Committee of a State legislature is a standing committee reconstituted every year.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • A Select Committee is ad hoc, constituted for a single Bill, drawn from one House, and it ceases to exist on presenting its report.

M32Practice · Week 415 marks · 250 words

"A State Legislative Council is a chamber of revision without the power to revise." Critically examine, and assess whether Article 169 should be used more readily.

Directive: "Critically examine" + a second tail. Two tasks: test the claim, then answer the reform question. An answer that does only the first is capped.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 169's reversible design
  2. The case that it cannot revise
  3. The case for retention
  4. Should abolition be readier?
  5. Verdict

Model answer

The framers could not settle whether States needed a second chamber, so they made the question reversible: under Article 169 Parliament may create or abolish a Legislative Council on a special-majority resolution of the State's own Assembly, by a law that is expressly not an amendment under Article 368. Six States have one today.

Why it cannot truly revise

Its power is delay, not decision. An ordinary Bill can be held for three months and, on second passage, a further one month — after which it is deemed passed. There is no joint sitting at State level, so disagreement never goes to a combined vote as under Article 108. A Money Bill may be held 14 days. Ratification under Article 368 is done by the Assembly alone, and the government answers only to the Assembly under Article 164(2). A chamber the Assembly can abolish was never going to be given a veto.

The case for retention

It admits expertise the Assembly lacks — Article 171 reserves a sixth to gubernatorial nominees in literature, science, art, co-operative movement and social service, and a sixth to graduates and teachers. It gives representation to local bodies, offers a route back for able people who lose an election, and imposes a pause on hurried legislation.

Should abolition be readier?

The honest criticisms are cost and patronage — Councils have become accommodation for defeated candidates. But the answer is reform of Article 171's electoral colleges, not abolition by convenience: a chamber abolished whenever it inconveniences a majority is not a check at all. Andhra Pradesh's abolition in 1985, revival in 2007 and fresh resolution in 2020 illustrate the instability.

The claim therefore stands as description and fails as prescription: the Council cannot revise, but the cure is to make it worth revising with.

Value additions used: Art 169 with the "not an amendment" point · the 3+1 month and 14-day mechanics · absence of a joint sitting contrasted with Art 108 · Art 164(2) · Art 171's six categories · the Andhra Pradesh 1985/2007/2020 sequence · the reform-not-abolition position.

What would cost you marks: describing the Council without answering the Article 169 question in the tail; and asserting the Council is weak without the two mechanics — four months and no joint sitting — that prove it.

Last-mile revision — State Legislature

  • Art 168 — legislature = Governor + one or two Houses. The Governor is part of it.
  • Art 169 — Parliament creates/abolishes a Council on the Assembly's special majority resolution. NOT an Art 368 amendment; Parliament passes it by simple majority and is not bound to act.
  • Art 170 — Assembly: max 500, min 60. Art 171 — Council: max 1/3 of the Assembly, min 40.
  • Art 172 — Assembly 5 years, extendable 1 year at a time in a National Emergency. Council permanent, 1/3 retire every 2 years.
  • Art 171 shares: local bodies 1/3 · MLAs 1/3 · graduates 1/12 · teachers 1/12 · Governor's nominees 1/6. They sum to 1.
  • Governor's nominee fields = five, including co-operative movement. President's Rajya Sabha list (Art 80(3)) = four, without it.
  • Six bicameral States: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh. AP abolished 1985, revived 2007, resolution again 2020.
  • No joint sitting at State level. Council delays an ordinary Bill 3 months + 1 month = 4 months max; a Money Bill 14 days. A Council-originated Bill rejected by the Assembly dies.
  • Age: Assembly 25, Council 30. Council's Chairman is a member of the House — unlike the Rajya Sabha's.
  • Speaker/Chairman removal: effective majority, 14 days' notice.
  • Art 194 privileges (=105) · Art 212 no judicial questioning of procedure (=122) · Art 176 Governor's address (=87) · Art 200 assent.
  • Art 368 ratification by States is done by the Assembly alone; the Council has no role.
