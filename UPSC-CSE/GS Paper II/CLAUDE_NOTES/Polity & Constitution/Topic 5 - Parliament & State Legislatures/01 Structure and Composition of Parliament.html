Topic 5's official syllabus line — "Parliament and State legislatures: structure, functioning, conduct of business..." — starts with structure for a reason: nearly every Prelims question on this ground is really just asking "do you know the exact number, the exact term, the exact qualifying age." 🌱 Start here — plain English first

Parliament has two Houses, and the easiest way to hold them apart is to remember what each one represents. The Lok Sabha ("House of the People") represents citizens directly — you vote for your own MP, every seat is up for election together, and the whole House can be dissolved and re-elected. The Rajya Sabha ("Council of States") represents the States as political units, not citizens directly — State MLAs vote to send representatives to it, it is never dissolved as a whole, and a rolling one-third of its members retire every two years instead.

That single distinction — direct-and-dissolvable vs indirect-and-permanent — explains almost every structural difference in this note: why Rajya Sabha has a fixed retirement cycle and Lok Sabha doesn't, why Money Bills only start in Lok Sabha (the directly-elected House controls the purse), and why the Vice-President, not an elected MP, chairs the Rajya Sabha.

Bicameral structure — Article 79

Parliament consists of the President plus two Houses — a fact worth holding precisely, since the President is constitutionally part of Parliament even though not a member of either House, which is why Presidential assent is needed to turn any Bill into law.

02

Rajya Sabha composition — Article 80

✚

Election method: State representatives are elected by the elected members of the State's Legislative Assembly (MLAs) — not directly by citizens — through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote. Seats are allocated to States roughly by population, so larger States get more Rajya Sabha seats — unlike the US Senate's equal-representation model, a frequently tested comparative point.

⚠

Two features unique to the Rajya Sabha ballot — both heavily tested. (1) Open ballot. Since 2003, voting in a Rajya Sabha election is by open ballot: an MLA must show the marked ballot to the party's authorised agent. Secrecy is deliberately removed, precisely so that cross-voting can be detected and the Tenth Schedule can operate. (2) No NOTA. In Shailesh Manubhai Parmar v. Election Commission of India (2018) the Supreme Court held that NOTA has no place in Rajya Sabha elections. Its reasoning: NOTA was created in PUCL v. Union of India (2013) for direct elections under universal adult suffrage, whereas Article 80(4) prescribes indirect election by proportional representation. Applying NOTA to an indirect election would defeat the value of the elector's vote, undercut the open-ballot discipline and open the door to defection and corruption. The trap version pairs this with a false reason — that NOTA "reduces voter turnout". Turnout is not the Court's ground; the mismatch with indirect representation is.

✚

The 12 nominated members are chosen by the President from persons with special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service — four fields, under Article 80(3). Do not merge this with the Governor's list for a State Legislative Council: Article 171(5) carries five fields, adding co-operative movement. That one-word gap between the two lists is a ready-made pairing question.

⚠

Trap: the "domicile" requirement was relaxed. Originally, a Rajya Sabha candidate had to be an elector in the specific State they wished to represent. This was changed — a candidate now only needs to be an elector anywhere in India to contest a Rajya Sabha seat from any State, a change upheld by the Supreme Court in Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India (2006) against a challenge that it violated federal principles.

03

Lok Sabha composition — Articles 81, 331

✚

Election method: members are directly elected by voters in territorial constituencies, using the first-past-the-post system. Seats are allotted to States so that the ratio of seats to population is, as far as practicable, the same across all States — the mechanism behind why larger States get proportionally more seats.

⚠

Trap — the delimitation freeze: Lok Sabha seat allocation among States has been frozen based on the 1971 Census since the 42nd Amendment (1976), and this freeze has been repeatedly extended — most recently intended to last until after the first census following 2026. The freeze exists to avoid penalising States that successfully controlled population growth by giving them fewer seats relative to high-growth States. An MCQ testing "current" seat numbers by population is almost always testing whether you know this freeze exists, not asking you to calculate live population ratios.

✚

Recent-amendment layer — the 106th Amendment, 2023 (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam): reserves one-third of all seats in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies, and the Delhi Assembly for women (including within the existing SC/ST reserved seats). Two heavily testable implementation riders: it takes effect only after the first delimitation following the first Census conducted after commencement — so it is law on the books but not yet operative in any sitting House — and the reservation is set to run for 15 years from commencement, extendable by Parliament. Note also what it does not cover: the Rajya Sabha and State Legislative Councils have no women's reservation under it — a clean negative-question trap.

📖 Constituent Assembly ammunition — why have a Rajya Sabha at all?

"Is the Rajya Sabha a true federal chamber or an obstruction?" is a perennial Mains framing, and the Assembly's own debate supplies the answer's spine. Gopalaswami Ayyangar's defence, moving the provision: a second chamber exists to "hold dignified debates" and to delay legislation which might be the outcome of passions of the moment" — the cooling-chamber logic. The concrete version: if a sudden wave of public anger pressures the directly-elected Lok Sabha into a hasty or draconian law, the indirectly-elected, never-dissolved Rajya Sabha exists to slow that Bill down until the sentiment cools. Add the federal argument (States as political units get a voice in Union law-making, though seats are population-weighted rather than equal like the US Senate) and the continuity argument (a permanent body keeps Parliament alive even when the Lok Sabha stands dissolved). For balance, cite the standing criticism — a house of party-ticket politicians duplicating the Lok Sabha's composition rarely behaves as a States' chamber in practice — and close on the revisory record (Money Bill limits aside, it has repeatedly forced genuine redrafting). Framers' argument + live criticism + evidence = a complete answer.

Disqualifications — the three separate sources (Laxmikanth's full list)

A recurring Prelims theme, because disqualification grounds come from three different places and questions mix them deliberately.

SourceGrounds
Constitution (Art 102)Holds an office of profit under the Union or a State (other than exempted offices); is of unsound mind and so declared by a court; is an undischarged insolvent; is not a citizen of India, or has voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship / acknowledged allegiance to a foreign State; is disqualified under any law made by Parliament.
Representation of the People Act, 1951Convicted for certain election offences or corrupt practices; convicted of any offence and sentenced to imprisonment of 2 years or more (note: preventive detention does not count); failed to lodge election expenses in time; has an interest in government contracts/works/services; is a director or managing agent of a government corporation with more than 25% government stake; dismissed from government service for corruption or disloyalty; convicted for promoting enmity between groups or for the offence of bribery; punished for preaching/practising social crimes like untouchability, dowry or sati.
Tenth Schedule (anti-defection)Voluntarily giving up party membership; voting or abstaining contrary to the party whip without prior permission and without condonation within 15 days; an independent member joining a party after election; a nominated member joining a party after 6 months of taking a seat. Exception: a merger is protected if at least two-thirds of that party's legislators agree (the 91st Amendment deleted the old one-third "split" exemption).
⚠

Who decides? For Article 102 grounds, the President decides, but must obtain the Election Commission's opinion and act according to it — the EC's opinion is binding here, which is the single most-tested nuance. Note the direction of travel: the Election Commission cannot disqualify a Member of Parliament of its own motion — it only advises the President, who then acts. A statement giving the EC a suo motu power of disqualification is wrong. For Tenth Schedule grounds, the presiding officer (Speaker/Chairman) decides; Kihoto Hollohan (1992) made that decision subject to judicial review, and Keisham Meghachandra (2020) proposed shifting it to an independent tribunal (Topic 6, Note 02).

⚠

Where a candidate may contest — the rule and its three exceptions. Under Section 4 of the RPA, 1951, a candidate for a general Lok Sabha seat needs only to be an elector in any parliamentary constituency in India — not in the constituency, or even the State, being contested. But three cases require the candidate to be an elector locally: seats reserved for Scheduled Tribes in the autonomous districts of Assam, the Lakshadweep seat, and the Sikkim seat. So the flat statement "a registered voter can contest from any constituency in India" is incorrect — the exceptions are the whole question. Contrast the Rajya Sabha, where the domicile requirement was dropped entirely (upheld in Kuldip Nayar, 2006).

✚

The disqualification clock, in full. Under Section 8(3), RPA 1951, conviction for any offence with a sentence of two years or more disqualifies a person from the date of conviction — and the disqualification continues for a further six years from the date of release. Candidates routinely remember the two years and forget the six; the six-year tail is what makes the disqualification bite. (Section 8(1) and 8(2) list specific offences that disqualify regardless of sentence length.)

✚

Reservation of seats: the Constitution reserves Lok Sabha seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes on a population basis — this is reservation of seats in specified constituencies, not a reservation of candidates by party. Note two things examiners exploit: the reservation was originally for 10 years but has been extended repeatedly (most recently by the 104th Amendment, 2019, which simultaneously ended Anglo-Indian nomination while extending SC/ST reservation to 2030); and in a reserved constituency every voter votes — only the candidate must belong to the reserved category.

Speaker, Deputy Speaker & the Anti-Defection connection

✚

The Speaker is elected by the Lok Sabha from among its own members, usually holds office for the House's full term, but must vacate the office if they cease to be a member of the House. The Speaker has a casting vote only in case of a tie — not an ordinary vote in the first round like other members. The Speaker also decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110 — a decision Article 110(3) declares final, though whether courts can review it is now an open question pending before a seven-judge bench (see Note 02's Rojer Mathew box).

⚠

Trap: the Speaker's resignation letter goes to the Deputy Speaker, not the President — mirroring the general pattern of resignations going "one step down" in the chain rather than to the very top.

✚

Removal and the rest of the presiding machinery — the schedule lists all four presiding officers, so hold the full set. Removal of the Speaker or Deputy Speaker: by a resolution of the Lok Sabha passed by an effective majority, after 14 days' advance notice. Critically, while such a resolution is under consideration the Speaker cannot preside over the sitting (though they may speak and vote in the first instance — the one occasion the Speaker votes normally rather than only to break a tie). Pro-tem Speaker: the outgoing Speaker vacates office immediately before the first meeting of a newly elected Lok Sabha, so the President appoints a sitting member as Speaker Pro Tem — by convention the senior-most member. Three facts get tested and all three are about who does what: the Pro-tem Speaker is appointed by the President (not elected by the House, not nominated by the outgoing Speaker, not chosen by the majority party); the President personally administers the oath to the Pro-tem Speaker; and the Pro-tem Speaker then administers the oath to the new members and presides over the election of the regular Speaker, after which the office ends. Panel of Chairpersons (Lok Sabha) / Panel of Vice-Chairpersons (Rajya Sabha): members nominated by the Speaker/Chairman who preside when both the Speaker and Deputy Speaker (or Chairman and Deputy Chairman) are absent. Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha is elected by that House from among its own members and is removable by an effective majority with 14 days' notice — and takes over presiding duties whenever the Vice-President is acting as President.

Rajya Sabha's position — equal, unequal, and exclusive (Laxmikanth's three-column framing)

The cleanest way to answer any "is the Rajya Sabha a weak House?" question is to split its powers into three buckets rather than argue in generalities.

⚠

Two traps here: (1) the Rajya Sabha can discuss the Annual Financial Statement — it simply cannot vote on Demands for Grants; a statement saying it "cannot discuss the Budget" is false. (2) The removal resolution for the Vice-President must originate in the Rajya Sabha, but the removal of the President can be initiated in either House — students routinely swap these.

Rajya Sabha's exclusive powers — the two the Lok Sabha cannot touch

✚

The standard "why do we need a Rajya Sabha" answer needs these two, since they belong to it alone: Article 249 — by a resolution supported by two-thirds of members present and voting, the Rajya Sabha can authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest, for up to one year at a time (renewable). Article 312 — by the same two-thirds resolution, it can authorise Parliament to create a new All India Service (like the IAS/IPS/IFS) binding on all States. Both exist because these decisions intrude into State autonomy, so the Constitution routes them through the House that represents States. Trap: the Lok Sabha cannot initiate either, and neither resolution needs Lok Sabha concurrence to be passed — the Rajya Sabha's role here is genuinely exclusive, not merely concurrent.

Joint Sitting — Article 108

If the two Houses disagree on an ordinary Bill (or one House sits on it for more than 6 months without acting), the President may summon a joint sitting of both Houses to resolve the deadlock — presided over by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha (or, in their absence, the Deputy Speaker; if neither is present, the Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha).

⚠

Trap: a joint sitting cannot be called for a Money Bill or a Constitutional Amendment Bill — both are excluded by design. Since Lok Sabha almost always has more members than Rajya Sabha, a joint sitting structurally favours whichever position the Lok Sabha holds — only three joint sittings have ever been held in India's history (the Dowry Prohibition Bill 1961, the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill 1978, and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill (POTA) 2002).

(A) Genuine UPSC PYQs

Practice · Assertion–Reason (Week 3 test)

Statement-I: Applying the None of the Above (NOTA) option to Rajya Sabha elections contradicts the essence of indirect electoral representation.

Statement-II: The use of NOTA reduces voter turnout in Rajya Sabha elections, thereby diluting the democratic process.

Which one of the following is correct?

  • (a) Both are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (b) Both are correct but Statement-II is not the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
  • (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct
Answer: (c)
  • Statement-I is correct. In Shailesh Manubhai Parmar (2018) the Supreme Court struck down NOTA in Rajya Sabha elections precisely because NOTA was devised in PUCL (2013) for direct elections, while Article 80(4) prescribes indirect election by proportional representation.
  • Statement-II is incorrect. Turnout was never the Court's ground. Its concerns were devaluing the elector's vote, defeating the open ballot, and opening the door to defection and corruption.
  • Assertion–Reason questions are often decided by a plausible but unused reason. Ask what the Court actually said, not what sounds sensible.
Practice · Statements (Week 3 test — you answered "both correct")

Consider the following statements:

  1. A registered voter in India can contest an election to the Lok Sabha from any constituency in India.
  2. Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, a person convicted of any offence and sentenced to imprisonment of two years or more is disqualified from contesting an election.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (b) — 2 only
  • Statement 1 is wrong because of three exceptions. The general rule under Section 4, RPA 1951 is indeed "elector in any parliamentary constituency in India" — but seats in the autonomous districts of Assam, Lakshadweep and Sikkim require the candidate to be an elector locally. The word any is what makes the statement false.
  • Statement 2 is correct under Section 8(3), and remember the tail: disqualification runs from the date of conviction and continues for a further six years after release.
  • The lesson is general: when a statement contains "any", "all" or "every", hunt for the exception before accepting it.
Practice · Direct (Week 3 test — you answered "nominated by the outgoing Speaker")

The Speaker Pro Tem of the Lok Sabha is:

  • (a) Elected by the members of the Lok Sabha
  • (b) Nominated by the outgoing Speaker
  • (c) Nominated by the party in the majority
  • (d) Appointed by the President
Answer: (d) — appointed by the President
  • The outgoing Speaker vacates office immediately before the first meeting of the new Lok Sabha, so there is no Speaker to nominate anyone — which is exactly why (b) is built to look reasonable.
  • The President appoints a sitting member, by convention the senior-most, and personally administers the oath to them.
  • The Pro Tem Speaker then swears in the new members and presides over the election of the regular Speaker, and the office ends there.

Consider the following statements:

  1. In the election for Lok Sabha or State Assembly, the winning candidate shall get at least 50 percent of the votes polled, to be declared elected.
  2. According to the provisions laid down in the Constitution of India, in Lok Sabha, the Speaker's post goes to the majority party and the Deputy Speaker's post goes to the opposition.

Deep drill — disqualifications, vacation of seats & the RS's position

Practice · Statements

With reference to the disqualification of a Member of Parliament, consider the following statements:

  1. On questions of disqualification under Article 102, the President decides after obtaining the opinion of the Election Commission.
  2. The President is bound to act according to that opinion.
  3. Disqualification on the ground of defection is decided by the President.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 1 and 2 only
  • (c) 2 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (b)

Defection cases are decided by the presiding officer of the House, not the President — and that decision is judicially reviewable after Kihoto Hollohan (1992).

Practice · How many ⚠

Consider the following grounds of disqualification for membership of Parliament:

  1. Holding an office of profit under the Union or a State
  2. Being convicted and sentenced to imprisonment for two years or more
  3. Voluntarily acquiring the citizenship of a foreign State
  4. Being detained under a preventive detention law

How many of the above are valid grounds of disqualification?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c)

Grounds 1, 2 and 3. Preventive detention is not a conviction and does not disqualify; the two-year rule under the RPA applies to a sentence following conviction.

Practice · Pairs

  1. Elected to both Houses of Parliament — must vacate one within 10 days, failing which the Rajya Sabha seat falls vacant
  2. Elected to Parliament and a State Legislature — must resign the State seat within 14 days
  3. Absent from all meetings without permission — the House may declare the seat vacant after 30 days

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (b)

Pairs 1 and 2. The absence threshold is 60 days, not 30 (excluding prorogation and adjournments longer than four consecutive days).

Practice · Negative ⚠

With reference to the powers of the Rajya Sabha, which one of the following statements is NOT correct?

  • (a) It cannot vote on the Demands for Grants.
  • (b) It cannot discuss the Annual Financial Statement.
  • (c) It alone can authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest.
  • (d) It alone can authorise the creation of a new All India Service.
Answer: (b)
  • The Rajya Sabha can discuss the Budget — it simply cannot vote on Demands for Grants.
  • Discussion and voting are different powers, and this is the single most reliable Rajya Sabha trap.
Practice · Assertion–Reason ⚠

Statement-I: A resolution for the removal of the Vice-President can be moved only in the Rajya Sabha.
Statement-II: Proceedings for the impeachment of the President may be initiated in either House of Parliament.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

  • (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct but Statement-II is not the correct explanation of Statement-I
  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
  • (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct
Answer: (b)

Both statements are true, but they are independent rules about two different offices — Statement-II does not explain Statement-I. This is the classic "both true, no causal link" A-R trap, and the reason the explanation step must be judged separately from the truth step.

M262020 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

"Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker"! Do you think this practice should be adopted to impart objectivity to the office of the Speaker of Lok Sabha? What could be its implications for the robust functioning of parliamentary business in India?

Directive: "Do you think… What could be its implications." An opinion question with a mandatory consequence-analysis. You must take a position — but a qualified, reasoned one. The implications half must cover both positive and negative consequences.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: what the British convention is
  2. Why reform is sought — the problem
  3. Implications in favour
  4. Implications against
  5. Qualified position + phased reform

Model answer

The phrase describes the British convention under which the Speaker resigns party membership on election, is returned unopposed at subsequent elections, and remains politically neutral for life. India adopted the office but not the convention.

The problem it addresses

The Indian Speaker remains a party politician who must seek re-election on a party ticket, yet exercises powers demanding strict neutrality: certifying a Money Bill under Article 110(3) — a certificate whose reviewability is pending before a seven-judge bench after Rojer Mathew (2019) — deciding disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, and regulating debate and discipline. Kihoto Hollohan (1992) made defection decisions judicially reviewable; Keisham Meghachandra (2020) criticised prolonged inaction and urged Parliament to consider transferring defection cases to an independent tribunal.

Implications in favour

Greater objectivity in defection and Money Bill decisions; institutional credibility; insulation from party pressure; and stronger opposition confidence in the fairness of proceedings, which would itself reduce disruption.

Implications against

A constituency effectively loses competitive representation; an unopposed return requires cross-party consensus India's fragmented polity may not sustain; there is no career path after demitting office, deterring able candidates; and coalition parties may resist surrendering a strategically important post.

Position

The objective is right; wholesale adoption is impractical at present. Sequence the reform: first, remove defection adjudication from the Speaker to an independent tribunal, as Keisham Meghachandra suggests, since that is the sharpest conflict of interest; second, institutionalise conventions — the Deputy Speaker going to the Opposition, the Speaker abstaining from party campaigning; and only then consider the full British convention. Objectivity is better secured by removing conflicted functions than by relying on a convention the political system may not honour.

Value additions used: the British convention's three elements · Art 110(3) + the pending Rojer Mathew (2019) reference · Tenth Schedule · Kihoto Hollohan (1992) · Keisham Meghachandra (2020) tribunal proposal · Deputy Speaker convention · a phased reform sequence.

M27Practice15 marks · 250 words

The Lok Sabha's seat allocation among States has remained frozen at 1971 Census levels for five decades. Discuss the constitutional rationale for this freeze and the federal tension it has created.

Directive: "Discuss" two named things — rationale and tension. Both halves are compulsory. This is a rare Polity question with a demographic-federal dimension, so the value-addition comes from population data and the 2026 timeline.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 81/82 + the freeze
  2. The rationale
  3. The federal tension
  4. The 2026 cliff-edge
  5. Way forward

Model answer

Article 81 requires Lok Sabha seats to be allocated so that the ratio of seats to population is, so far as practicable, the same for all States; Article 82 requires readjustment after every census. The 42nd Amendment (1976) froze allocation at 1971 Census figures until 2000; the 84th Amendment (2001) extended the freeze to the first census taken after 2026.

The constitutional rationale

The freeze was a deliberate incentive for population stabilisation. Without it, States that successfully reduced fertility would lose parliamentary seats to States with higher growth — penalising the very policy the national family-planning programme pursued. It severed the link between demographic performance and political representation. The same 1971 baseline is also used for computing vote values in the Presidential election.

The federal tension

Two opposing grievances now coexist. Southern and smaller States — Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Punjab — argue that having reached replacement-level fertility they will be punished for success if seats are reallocated on current population. Northern States — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan — argue the reverse: their citizens are under-represented, each MP standing for far more people, which offends the one-person-one-vote principle Article 81 itself asserts. The freeze also interacts with the 106th Amendment (2023): women's reservation takes effect only after the first delimitation following the next census.

Way forward

Extend the freeze again; enlarge the House so that no State loses seats in absolute terms while relative weights adjust; strengthen the Rajya Sabha as a genuine chamber of State equality; or compensate through fiscal devolution rather than seats. The question is ultimately about the terms of federal union, and any settlement will need broad political consensus rather than a bare majority.

Value additions used: Arts 81 and 82 · the 42nd (1976) and 84th (2001) Amendments · population-stabilisation rationale · the 1971 baseline's second use in Presidential vote value · both sides' grievances with named States · 106th Amendment linkage · four reform options.
Budget check: 277 / 250 words. Cut: "preserving consistency" as an explanatory tail, and the "options include" framing before the way-forward list. Bought: nothing new — length only, 302 → budget. The four named options survive; on a federal-tension question, offering a menu rather than a single prescription is what signals balance.
What would cost you marks: presenting only the southern States' grievance — the northern under-representation argument is equally constitutional; missing the 106th Amendment connection, which makes the issue urgent.

Last-mile revision — Structure & Composition of Parliament

  • Parliament = President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha (President is part of Parliament, not a House member).
  • Lok Sabha: max 550 post-104th Amendment (552 before it), current actual 543; directly elected; 5-year term (extendable by 1 year at a time only during National Emergency).
  • Rajya Sabha: max 250, current effective 245 (233 elected + 12 nominated); permanent body; 1/3rd retire every 2 years.
  • RS election: State MLAs vote, proportional representation, single transferable vote. Domicile requirement relaxed — upheld in Kuldip Nayar (2006).
  • 12 nominated RS members: literature, science, art, social service — four fields (Art 80(3)). The Governor's Legislative Council list has five (Art 171(5)), adding co-operative movement.
  • Age: Lok Sabha 25, Rajya Sabha 30.
  • Anglo-Indian nomination (both Houses) abolished by 104th Amendment, 2019.
  • Lok Sabha seats frozen at 1971 Census figures since the 42nd Amendment, 1976.
  • 106th Amendment (2023): 1/3rd women's reservation in LS + Assemblies — operative only after post-Census delimitation; 15-year sunset; no RS/Council coverage.
  • Speaker: casting vote only on a tie; decides Money Bill status finally, non-justiciable; resigns to Deputy Speaker.
  • Joint sitting (Art 108): Speaker presides; excludes Money Bills and Constitutional Amendment Bills; used only 3 times ever.
