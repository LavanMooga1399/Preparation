Judicial Review, Judicial Activism & PIL

The concepts that close the judiciary week — and the highest-yield Mains material in Topic 6. Prelims wants the definitions kept apart; Mains wants a position on where the line falls.

Arts 13, 32, 226, 137 Restraint → Review → Activism → Overreach S.P. Gupta, 1981 Hussainara Khatoon, 1979 Presidential Reference, Nov 2025
4words on one spectrum — and the exam is built on telling them apart
◆ How this note earns its marks

Prelims treats this as a definition chapter. The 2017 question asked what judicial review "implies" and gave four plausible-sounding options, only one of which was the textbook definition. The 2020 question paired judicial review with basic structure and both statements were false. In each case the mark went to whoever held the precise definition, not to whoever had opinions about the judiciary.

Mains is the opposite. Here you need a position, evidenced by cases, on where legitimate review ends and overreach begins. The good news is that 2025–26 handed you the best possible case study: the Supreme Court used Article 142 to deem assent to ten Bills, a Presidential Reference followed, and in November 2025 the Court itself drew the line. An answer built on that arc is current, concrete and self-contained.

Week 5, Day 6 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapters 27–29.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Judicial review is the power of a court to examine whether a law or an executive action is consistent with the Constitution, and to declare it void if it is not. It is not a power to ask whether a law is wise — only whether it is constitutional. That distinction is the single most tested idea in this note.

Judicial activism is something else. It describes a court that goes beyond deciding the dispute in front of it and takes a positive role in enforcing rights — relaxing who may bring a case, treating a postcard as a petition, issuing guidelines where Parliament has not legislated, monitoring implementation for years. Vishaka is the standard example: with no law on workplace sexual harassment, the Court wrote the guidelines itself and made them binding until Parliament acted, which it did sixteen years later.

Public Interest Litigation is the procedural device that made activism possible. Ordinarily only the person injured may sue — the rule of locus standi. PIL relaxes it, so that a public-spirited citizen may move the court on behalf of people who cannot come themselves: undertrials, bonded labourers, the destitute.

And judicial overreach is activism gone past the line — a court doing what the Constitution assigns to somebody else. Where that line sits is the entire debate, and in November 2025 the Supreme Court drew it more clearly than it had in decades.

01

Judicial review — basis, scope and limits

◆
The definition to memorise verbatim. Judicial review is the power of the judiciary to pronounce upon the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders of both the Union and the State governments. Prelims 2017 offered exactly this wording as option (a) against three distractors. Note what it excludes: it is not a power to question the wisdom of a law, not a power to vet Bills before assent, and not the same as the power of review under Article 137.
SourceWhat it supplies
Article 13The textual foundation. Laws inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights are void, and "law" is defined widely enough to include ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs and usages.
Articles 32 and 226The remedies through which review is exercised — the Supreme Court and High Courts respectively. Both held part of the basic structure in L. Chandra Kumar (1997).
Articles 131–136The jurisdictions through which constitutional questions reach the Supreme Court.
Articles 245 and 246 with the Seventh ScheduleFederal review — whether a legislature had the competence to enact the law at all.
Articles 251 and 254Repugnancy between Union and State laws.
Article 372Continuance of pre-Constitution laws, subject to their consistency with the Constitution.
!
India's judicial review is not America's, and the difference has a name. The US Constitution guarantees "due process of law", which lets courts test whether a law is fair and reasonable in substance. Article 21 originally said "procedure established by law", a narrower formula borrowed from Japan, under which a court asked only whether the correct procedure had been followed. A.K. Gopalan (1950) took the narrow view; Maneka Gandhi (1978) read fairness into it, holding that the procedure must be "right, just and fair", which brought Indian review close to due process without adopting the phrase. Indian judicial review therefore has a narrower textual base but a comparably wide practical reach.

Three grounds on which a law can fall

🔴 Prelims — when is a law unconstitutional?

◆
It infringes Fundamental Rights (Part III) — Art 13.
◆
It is outside the competence of the legislature that made it — the Seventh Schedule test, with the doctrines of pith and substance and colourable legislation.
◆
It offends a constitutional provision other than Part III — for a constitutional amendment, this includes the basic structure, from Kesavananda Bharati (1973).
◆
Not a ground: that the law is unwise, inexpedient or economically unsound. Courts repeatedly say policy is for the legislature.
◆
The 42nd Amendment tried to abolish judicial review, and failed. It inserted clauses 4 and 5 into Article 368 to place constitutional amendments beyond judicial scrutiny and to declare Parliament's amending power unlimited. In Minerva Mills (1980) both clauses were struck down — a Parliament of limited powers cannot make itself unlimited, and judicial review is itself part of the basic structure. This is why Prelims 2019's statement that "an amendment to the Constitution cannot be called into question by the Supreme Court" is false.
02

The four-word ladder — restraint, review, activism, overreach

TermWhat it meansIllustration
Judicial restraintThe court defers to the other branches, confines itself to the dispute, and declines to enter policy. Associated with the "self-imposed discipline" school.A.K. Gopalan (1950) — the Court read Art 21 narrowly and declined to test the fairness of the law.
Judicial reviewThe power to test laws and executive action against the Constitution and strike them down. Neither activist nor restrained in itself — it is the baseline function.Kesavananda Bharati (1973); Minerva Mills (1980); the 2015 NJAC judgment.
Judicial activismThe court plays an active, positive role in enforcing rights — relaxing procedure, issuing guidelines, monitoring implementation. Sometimes called judicial dynamism.Vishaka (1997) guidelines; continuing mandamus in environmental cases; Art 21 expanded to livelihood, health, clean air, education.
Judicial overreachActivism carried into the territory the Constitution assigns to the legislature or executive — the court substituting itself for another functionary.The deemed assent to ten Bills under Art 142 in TN Governor (April 2025), held impermissible by the November 2025 advisory opinion.
!
The distractor Prelims 2017 used, and why it is wrong. One option offered "the power of the Judiciary to question the wisdom of the laws enacted by the Legislatures". That is precisely what judicial review is not. Another offered the power to review all legislative enactments before they are assented to by the President — that is a pre-enactment or advisory power the Indian Supreme Court does not possess, except through an Art 143 reference. A third confused judicial review with the power under Article 137 to review its own judgments. Three distractors, three genuinely different concepts.
03

Judicial activism — origins, methods and instances

📖 The story — how a court became an actor

The activism of the Indian Supreme Court has a specific origin, and it is not idealism. It is reputational repair after the Emergency. In ADM Jabalpur (1976) the Court held, four to one, that during a National Emergency a person had no locus to move any court even against unlawful detention — that the right to life itself could be suspended. Justice H.R. Khanna's lone dissent cost him the Chief Justiceship. When the Emergency ended, the institution had to rebuild its standing.

It did so by opening its doors. In Hussainara Khatoon (1979) — the first case widely recognised as a PIL — the Court acted on a newspaper report about undertrial prisoners in Bihar who had already spent longer in custody awaiting trial than the maximum sentence for their offences. It ordered the release of some forty thousand undertrials and read a right to speedy trial into Article 21, though no such words appear there.

Two judges built the method: Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer. Bhagwati's formulation in S.P. Gupta (1981) is the doctrinal hinge — where a legal wrong is caused to a person or class who by reason of poverty, disability or socially or economically disadvantaged position cannot approach the court, any member of the public may move it on their behalf.

The techniques followed: the epistolary jurisdiction, under which a letter or postcard is treated as a writ petition; continuing mandamus, where the court keeps a case alive and supervises compliance for years; and court-appointed commissions and amicus curiae to gather facts the adversarial process would not produce.

Where activism has actually operated

DomainInstances worth naming in an answer
Expanding Article 21Maneka Gandhi (1978) — procedure must be right, just and fair. Then a chain of readings-in: livelihood (Olga Tellis, 1985), speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon), clean environment, health, education (Unni Krishnan, 1993, later Art 21-A by the 86th Amendment), and privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017).
Legislating in a vacuumVishaka (1997) — guidelines on workplace sexual harassment, binding under Art 141 until Parliament legislated, which it did only in 2013. D.K. Basu (1997) — arrest and detention safeguards.
EnvironmentContinuing mandamus in the Taj Trapezium, Ganga pollution and vehicular emission matters; import of the polluter pays, precautionary and public trust doctrines; absolute liability in M.C. Mehta (1987).
Institutional integrityVineet Narain (1998) — CBI and CVC autonomy directions. 2G and coal block allocations cancelled. Electoral bonds struck down in 2024 under Art 19(1)(a) and manifest arbitrariness.
Social reformShayara Bano (2017) — instant triple talaq. Navtej Singh Johar (2018) — Section 377, on constitutional morality. Joseph Shine (2018) — adultery. Common Cause (2018) — passive euthanasia and living wills.
◆
The Mains-ready formulation of the case for activism. Activism has been the response to institutional failure elsewhere: legislative inaction on sexual harassment for sixteen years after Vishaka; executive inaction on custodial safeguards; regulatory failure on air quality. The Court did not seek these fields — it entered vacuums. Any answer defending activism should make that causal claim rather than simply listing outcomes.
04

Public Interest Litigation

FeatureDetail
The core departureRelaxation of locus standi. Traditionally only an aggrieved person may sue; in PIL any public-spirited person may move the court on behalf of those unable to do so.
Doctrinal sourceS.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) — the same First Judges Case that decided the appointments question. It is examined for both holdings, and students routinely remember only one.
First PILHussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) — undertrial prisoners; right to speedy trial read into Art 21.
Constitutional hooksFiled under Art 32 before the Supreme Court or Art 226 before a High Court. Note there is no separate PIL article — it is a procedural innovation, not a distinct jurisdiction.
Not adversarialProceedings are collaborative and inquisitorial rather than adversarial: the court appoints commissions, calls for reports, and may act on its own motion.
Epistolary jurisdictionA letter, postcard or newspaper report may be treated as a writ petition.
Who cannot filePIL is not available for a purely private grievance, for a political or publicity motive, or where the petitioner has a personal or oblique interest. Courts impose exemplary costs for frivolous PILs.
Against whomThe State, its instrumentalities under Art 12, and any authority discharging a public function.
!
The two sentences that usually go wrong. (1) "PIL was introduced by a constitutional amendment" — false. There is no such amendment and no PIL article; it is judge-made procedure under Arts 32 and 226. (2) "In a PIL the petitioner must show personal injury" — false; that is the very requirement PIL relaxes. Notice the structural parallel with quo warranto in Note 06, the one writ where locus standi is also relaxed. If a stem mentions relaxed standing, those are the two places it can be pointing.

The case against PIL, stated fairly

The gainThe cost
Access. Justice reaches people who could never have reached a court — undertrials, bonded labourers, the destitute.Docket burden. PIL consumes the time of the highest courts while ordinary appeals wait years behind it.
Rights enforcement. Article 21 has been given content — livelihood, health, environment, speedy trial — that legislation never supplied.Capacity. Courts lack the technical and fiscal machinery to design policy, and cannot be held accountable for the results.
Accountability. Institutional failures that no political actor had an incentive to raise have been exposed and corrected.Abuse. Publicity, private vendetta and political litigation dressed as public interest — hence exemplary costs.
Democratic deepening. A forum exists between elections for holding the executive to constitutional standards.Separation of powers. Guidelines with the force of law under Art 141 are, functionally, legislation by an unelected body.
05

The overreach debate, 2025–26 — where the line was drawn

📖 The story — the clearest boundary marker in decades

April 2025. In State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu the Supreme Court confronted a Governor who had sat on ten Bills for years. It held the inaction illegal, ruled that Article 200 offers only three options, imposed timelines of one and three months, and made breach judicially reviewable. It then used Article 142 to deem the ten Bills assented to — declaring the President's subsequent action on the reserved Bills void.

The objection. Whatever one thought of the Governor's conduct, a court had supplied an assent that the Constitution assigns to a different functionary. The Vice-President called Article 142 a "nuclear missile" available against democratic forces. The charge was overreach, not error.

May 2025. President Murmu invoked Article 143 and referred fourteen questions — the 16th Presidential Reference since 1950. The core question was whether courts may regulate the exercise of constitutional powers where the Constitution itself prescribes no timeline or procedure.

20 November 2025. A five-judge Constitution Bench answered, unanimously, in a 111-page opinion (2025 INSC 1333). It kept the three-options holding, agreeing that a Governor cannot withhold a Bill without returning it, and reasoning from federalism as a basic feature that "dialogue, reconciliation and balance, and not obstructionism" is the essence of Indian constitutionalism. It then reversed the rest: no timelines can be read into Articles 200 and 201; there can be no deemed assent; the actions of the Governor and President under those articles are not justiciable, since the process is dialogic rather than adjudicatory; and Article 142 cannot supplant constitutional provisions.

The door left open. The Court did not surrender the field. It held that prolonged, unexplained, indefinite inaction will invite limited judicial scrutiny, and that a court may issue a limited direction to act within a time frame — without commenting on the merits of the discretion. It added that Article 361 immunity does not bar examining the validity of the action.

!
The formulation to carry into every overreach answer. Judicial review is part of the basic structure, but it is "not an unbridled scope that can negate or destroy the separation of powers doctrine." That sentence is the Court policing itself, which is exactly what a Mains examiner wants evidence of. Pair it with the second holding — that Article 142 cannot supplant a constitutional provision — and you have a self-imposed limit stated by the institution being criticised, which is far stronger than quoting a politician's complaint.

The standing criticisms, and the answers to them

CriticismThe response
Counter-majoritarian. Unelected judges override the decisions of elected representatives.The Constitution is itself counter-majoritarian by design — Part III exists precisely to place some questions beyond majority decision. Review enforces that choice rather than defying it.
Institutional incapacity. Courts lack the expertise and budget to make policy and answer for it.Correct, and it argues for restraint in remedy rather than abdication of jurisdiction — declare the right, leave the means to the executive.
Selectivity. Activism is deployed unevenly, vigorously in some fields and absent in others.A real criticism of practice, not of the power. ADM Jabalpur is the standing reminder of what abdication looks like.
Accountability deficit. Judges are unaccountable to anyone, and removal has never once succeeded.Also correct — see the Justice Varma sequence in Note 05. The remedy is accountability reform, not curtailment of review.
◆
Way forward — the four points a "reform" question needs. (1) Self-restraint in remedy: declare rights, leave implementation to the competent organ. (2) Guidelines issued under Art 141 should be expressly interim, lapsing when the legislature acts, as Vishaka's were. (3) Legislative responsiveness — the sixteen-year gap between Vishaka and the 2013 Act is what created the vacuum in the first place. (4) Judicial accountability — a workable mechanism between "do nothing" and a removal process that has never been completed.

Named doctrines the answer keys use

DoctrineWhat it does
Doctrine of eclipseA pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is not dead but dormant; it revives if the inconsistency is removed.
Doctrine of severabilityOnly the offending part of a law is struck down where it can be separated from the rest.
Doctrine of waiverFundamental Rights cannot be waived by the person entitled to them — they exist in the public interest, not merely the individual's.

Constituent Assembly ammunition

◆ For any question on the legitimacy of judicial review

Ambedkar called Article 32 the article "without which this Constitution would be a nullity" — the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. That is the strongest available answer to the counter-majoritarian objection: the framers did not add review as an afterthought or allow it to emerge by interpretation as in the United States. They wrote the remedy into Part III itself and made it a Fundamental Right.

The corollary is equally usable against overreach. The framers gave the Court a remedy, not a mandate to govern. That is precisely the distinction the November 2025 opinion drew.

Prelims Questions

Bucket (A) genuine, verified PYQs with year and stage, then bucket (B) practice questions in identical format.

(A) Genuine previous-year questions

2017UPSC Prelims · Direct

In India, Judicial Review implies

  • (a) the power of the Judiciary to pronounce upon the constitutionality of laws and executive orders.
  • (b) the power of the Judiciary to question the wisdom of the laws enacted by the Legislatures.
  • (c) the power of the Judiciary to review all the legislative enactments before they are assented to by the President.
  • (d) the power of the Judiciary to review its own judgements given earlier in similar or different cases.
Answer: (a)
  • This is the textbook definition, and the question rewards holding it word for word rather than reasoning from general impressions about the judiciary.
  • (b) is the wisdom trap — courts test constitutionality, never policy merit. (c) describes a pre-enactment power India does not have. (d) is Article 137, review of the Court's own judgments — a different power entirely.
  • Three distractors, three distinct concepts. Section 02 sets them side by side.
2020UPSC Prelims · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Constitution of India defines its 'basic structure' in terms of federalism, secularism, fundamental rights and democracy.
  2. The Constitution of India provides for 'judicial review' to safeguard the citizens' liberties and to preserve the ideals on which the Constitution is based.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (d) — Neither 1 nor 2
  • Statement 1 is wrong. The Constitution nowhere defines basic structure. The doctrine is judge-made, from Kesavananda Bharati (1973), and its contents have been built case by case — deliberately never exhaustively listed.
  • Statement 2 is wrong on one word: "provides". There is no article headed "judicial review". The power flows from Arts 13, 32, 226, 131–136, 245–246 and 372 read together. Its purpose as stated is accurate, which is what makes the statement tempting.
  • The recurring pattern across this note's PYQs: "the Constitution defines / provides for X" is false far more often than it is true. Compare the 2019 misbehaviour question and the 2022 contempt question in Note 05.
2019UPSC Prelims · Statements

With reference to the Constitution of India, consider the following statements:

  1. No High Court shall have the jurisdiction to declare any central law to be constitutionally invalid.
  2. An amendment to the Constitution of India cannot be called into question by the Supreme Court of India.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (d) — Neither 1 nor 2
  • Statement 1 fails because of the integrated judiciary: a High Court exercising review under Arts 13 and 226 can strike down a central law.
  • Statement 2 fails because of Kesavananda (1973) and Minerva Mills (1980), which struck down Art 368(4) and (5) — the 42nd Amendment's own attempt to make amendments unreviewable. The 99th Amendment was struck down on the same footing in 2015.
  • Two "neither" answers in a row in this bucket. Judge statements individually; there is no rule that a question must contain a true one.
2019UPSC Prelims · Direct

With reference to the Constitution of India, prohibitions or limitations or provisions contained in ordinary laws cannot act as prohibitions or limitations on the constitutional powers under Article 142. It could mean which one of the following?

  • (a) The decisions taken by the Election Commission of India while discharging its duties cannot be challenged in any court of law.
  • (b) The Supreme Court of India is not constrained in the exercise of its powers by laws made by the Parliament.
  • (c) In the event of grave financial crisis in the country, the President of India can declare Financial Emergency without the counsel from the Cabinet.
  • (d) State Legislatures cannot make laws on certain matters without the concurrence of Union Legislature.
Answer: (b)
  • The stem restates the settled position that a constitutional power cannot be limited by ordinary statute. Option (b) is that proposition in different words.
  • Carried into this note because it is the pre-2025 half of the Article 142 picture. The post-2025 half: the Constitution itself can limit Art 142, and does — Section 05.
  • Together the two propositions give you a complete and current answer on Art 142's limits, which few candidates will have.
2022UPSC Prelims · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. A Bill amending the Constitution of India requires a prior recommendation of the President of India.
  2. When a Constitution Amendment Bill is presented to the President of India, it is obligatory for the President of India to give his/her assent.
  3. A Constitution Amendment Bill must be passed by both the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha by a special majority and there is no provision for joint sitting.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 2 and 3 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (b) — 2 and 3 only
  • Statement 1 is wrong. No prior Presidential recommendation is needed for a Constitution Amendment Bill — that requirement attaches to Money Bills and to certain financial and State-reorganisation Bills, not to Art 368.
  • Statement 2 is correct because of the 24th Amendment, 1971, which made assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill obligatory — the President has no veto over it.
  • Statement 3 is correct, and the absence of a joint sitting is the structural point: Art 108 does not apply to Constitution Amendment Bills. Included here because the reviewability of amendments is this note's subject.

(B) Practice questions — same formats, built on this note

Practice · Negative

Which one of the following is NOT a correct statement about Public Interest Litigation in India?

  • (a) It was made possible by a relaxation of the rule of locus standi
  • (b) It was introduced into the Constitution by an amendment
  • (c) A letter addressed to the court may be treated as a writ petition
  • (d) Courts may impose exemplary costs where a PIL is found to be frivolous
Answer: (b) is NOT correct
  • PIL rests on no amendment and no article of its own. It is a judge-made procedural innovation under Arts 32 and 226, developed in Hussainara Khatoon (1979) and S.P. Gupta (1981).
  • (c) is the epistolary jurisdiction and (d) the corrective against abuse — both accurate.
  • Note the family resemblance to the collegium: another major institutional feature with no textual basis, built entirely by the Court. If a statement attributes either to an amendment, it is false.
Practice · Negative

With reference to judicial review in India, which one of the following statements is NOT correct?

  • (a) Article 13 declares laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights to be void
  • (b) The Supreme Court may examine whether a law is economically sound before upholding it
  • (c) A constitutional amendment may be struck down for violating the basic structure
  • (d) Judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 is part of the basic structure
Answer: (b) is NOT correct
  • Courts test constitutionality, not wisdom. Economic soundness, expediency and desirability are for the legislature — this is the same trap Prelims 2017 set with a different surface.
  • (c) is Kesavananda; (d) is L. Chandra Kumar (1997). Both accurate.
  • When an option describes a court assessing whether a policy is good rather than whether it is lawful, it is almost always the false one.

Answer: (c) — only three
  • Statements 1, 3 and 4 are correct. The three-options holding survived; deemed assent and the limits on Art 142 are the opinion's two most quotable findings.
  • Statement 2 is wrong — it is precisely what was reversed. The Court held that "as soon as possible" cannot be converted into fixed timelines, and that the Tamil Nadu judgment had misread Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020), where a timeline was imposed on a Speaker exercising a quasi-judicial function.
  • When a later judgment revisits an earlier one, know exactly which parts survived. "It was overruled" is almost never the whole truth.
Practice · Assertion–Reason

Consider the following two statements:

  1. Statement-I: The Supreme Court issued binding guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace in 1997.
  2. Statement-II: Law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

  • (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II explains Statement-I
  • (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct but Statement-II does not explain Statement-I
  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is not correct
  • (d) Statement-I is not correct but Statement-II is correct
Answer: (a)
  • Both are correct, and Article 141 is exactly what gave the Vishaka guidelines binding force in the absence of any statute. Without Art 141 they would have been a recommendation.
  • The three-step method applies: truth of I, truth of II, then the causal link. Here the link is genuine and direct.
  • Contrast a case where the link would fail — "the Court struck down the NJAC" explained by "the Supreme Court is a court of record". Both true; unrelated.
Practice · Pairs

Consider the following pairs of case and contribution:

  1. Hussainara Khatoon (1979) — right to speedy trial
  2. S.P. Gupta (1981) — relaxation of locus standi
  3. Vishaka (1997) — guidelines on workplace sexual harassment
  4. Minerva Mills (1980) — struck down clauses excluding judicial review of amendments

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (d) — all four
  • All correct. Minerva Mills struck down Article 368(4) and (5), inserted by the 42nd Amendment to place amendments beyond review.
  • The fact worth attaching to pair 2: S.P. Gupta is the First Judges Case as well. One case, two separate examinable holdings — appointments and standing.
  • Fourth "all correct" set across the week. If the instinct to reject (d) is still firing, that is the signal to keep drilling it.
Practice · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. Judicial activism and judicial overreach mean the same thing.
  2. Judicial review in India is based on the doctrine of due process of law.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (d) — Neither 1 nor 2
  • Statement 1 is wrong. Activism is a court playing a positive role in enforcing rights; overreach is a court entering territory the Constitution assigns to another organ. One is a description, the other is a charge.
  • Statement 2 is wrong. The Constitution uses "procedure established by law" in Article 21, not "due process". Maneka Gandhi (1978) read a fairness requirement into it, which brought the practice close to due process — but the doctrine was never adopted textually.
  • Both statements are the kind that feel right at speed. Slow down on definitional stems; they are decided by single words.
Practice · Direct

Which one of the following pairs of doctrine and effect is correctly matched?

  • (a) Doctrine of eclipse — the offending part of a statute alone is struck down
  • (b) Doctrine of severability — a pre-Constitution law becomes dormant and may revive
  • (c) Doctrine of colourable legislation — what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly
  • (d) Doctrine of waiver — a citizen may give up a Fundamental Right voluntarily
Answer: (c)
  • (a) and (b) have been swapped — severability strikes down only the offending part; eclipse makes a pre-Constitution law dormant with the possibility of revival.
  • (d) states the opposite of the law: Fundamental Rights cannot be waived, because they exist in the public interest and not merely for the individual's benefit.
  • Section 06 lists all eight doctrines. Learn eclipse and severability as a deliberate pair, since swapping them is the obvious question.

(C) Trap statements — True or False

◆ Why this bucket exists, and how to work it

Twenty-one of the fifty questions in the Week 4 paper were “how many of the above” — 42 per cent — and that format scored 52 per cent. It cannot be solved by elimination: every statement has to be judged on its own. So the skill the paper actually rewards is not answering questions, it is marking a single statement true or false.

That is all this bucket is. 16 statements from this note, one at a time. Decide before you click. If you find yourself reasoning about how many ought to be true, stop — that instinct is what the format punishes.

Roughly half are false, and most of the false ones are wrong by one word: an authority, a number, a fraction, or a qualifier. That is how this setter builds them.

Trap 1 · True or False

The Constitution of India contains an express article headed 'judicial review'.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • There is none. The power flows from Arts 13, 32, 226, 131–136, 245–246, 251, 254 and 372 read together.
Trap 2 · True or False

Judicial review includes the power to examine whether a law is wise or economically sound.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It tests constitutionality, not merit. Policy is for the legislature — the distractor Prelims 2017 used.
Trap 3 · True or False

The Constitution of India defines its basic structure.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It does not. The doctrine is judge-made, from Kesavananda Bharati (1973), and its contents have deliberately never been listed exhaustively.
Trap 4 · True or False

Minerva Mills struck down clauses of Article 368 that sought to place constitutional amendments beyond judicial review.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • 1980 — Article 368(4) and (5), inserted by the 42nd Amendment. A Parliament of limited powers cannot make itself unlimited.
Trap 5 · True or False

Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review on basic-structure grounds.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • I.R. Coelho (2007). The date is that of Kesavananda, and the date is the whole question.
Trap 6 · True or False

Article 21 of the Constitution uses the expression 'due process of law'.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It uses 'procedure established by law', borrowed from Japan. Maneka Gandhi (1978) read fairness into it without adopting the American doctrine.
Trap 7 · True or False

A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is void and cannot revive.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • The doctrine of eclipse — it is dormant, and revives if the inconsistency is removed.
Trap 8 · True or False

Under the doctrine of severability, only the offending part of a statute is struck down where it can be separated.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Contrast eclipse, which concerns pre-Constitution laws. These two are routinely swapped in a pairs question.
Trap 9 · True or False

A citizen may voluntarily waive a Fundamental Right.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Fundamental Rights cannot be waived — they exist in the public interest, not merely for the individual's benefit.
Trap 10 · True or False

Public Interest Litigation was introduced into the Constitution by an amendment.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • No amendment and no article. It is judge-made procedure under Arts 32 and 226 — like the collegium, a major institution with no textual basis.
Trap 11 · True or False

Hussainara Khatoon is regarded as the first Public Interest Litigation in India.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • 1979, on undertrial prisoners in Bihar; it read a right to speedy trial into Article 21.
Trap 12 · True or False

S.P. Gupta relaxed the rule of locus standi.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • 1981 — and the same case is the First Judges Case. One case, two separate examinable holdings.
Trap 13 · True or False

In a PIL the petitioner must show that he has personally suffered a legal injury.

  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • That is precisely the requirement PIL relaxes. Compare quo warranto, the other place standing is relaxed.

The Vishaka guidelines were binding on all courts until Parliament legislated.

  • (b) False

Judicial activism and judicial overreach mean the same thing.

M40Practice · Week 515 marks · 250 words

"Judicial activism is the response to institutional failure elsewhere; judicial overreach is its failure to stop." Critically examine with reference to recent constitutional developments.

Directive: "Critically examine." The quotation contains two claims. Test each. A strong answer accepts the first substantially and shows the second being corrected by the Court itself.
Skeleton
  1. The distinction, stated precisely
  2. Activism as a response to vacuum
  3. Where it crossed
  4. The self-correction
  5. Verdict and way forward

Model answer

Judicial review is the power to test law and executive action against the Constitution. Activism is its energetic exercise — relaxed standing, guidelines, continuing supervision. Overreach is different in kind: a court exercising a power the Constitution assigns to another organ.

Activism as a response to vacuum

The record supports the first claim. Hussainara Khatoon (1979) arose because undertrials had served longer than their maximum sentence. Vishaka (1997) filled a legislative void that Parliament left open for a further sixteen years. Vineet Narain (1998) addressed investigative agencies that could not act against the executive controlling them. In each, the Court entered a field the competent organ had vacated.

Where it crossed

In State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (April 2025) the Court, having rightly found years of gubernatorial inaction unlawful, went on to use Article 142 to deem assent to ten Bills. Diagnosis was within its power; supplying the assent was not. That is the second claim's evidence.

The self-correction

On the 16th Presidential Reference, decided 20 November 2025, a Constitution Bench held that no timelines can be read into Articles 200 and 201, that there can be no deemed assent, and that Article 142 cannot supplant constitutional provisions — adding that judicial review, though basic structure, is "not an unbridled scope that can negate the separation of powers". It preserved a narrow channel: prolonged, unexplained inaction invites limited scrutiny.

The quotation is therefore half right. Activism has been reactive, but overreach was not a failure to stop — it was corrected within seven months, by the institution itself. The durable remedy is legislative responsiveness, since every vacuum the Court has entered was made elsewhere.

Value additions used: Hussainara Khatoon (1979) · Vishaka (1997) and the sixteen-year gap · Vineet Narain (1998) · TN Governor (April 2025) and Art 142 · Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, 20 November 2025 · the "not an unbridled scope" formulation.
Budget check: 276 / 250 words. Cut: the ADM Jabalpur origin story and the list of Article 21 expansions — vivid, but the question is about the boundary, not the history of activism. Bought: the direct quotation from the November 2025 opinion, which is the single strongest sentence available here because it is the Court limiting itself rather than a critic limiting it.
What would cost you marks: treating activism and overreach as interchangeable — the question turns on the distinction; and ending with "a balance must be struck", which asserts a conclusion the body has not earned. Name the balance: declare rights, leave remedy.
M41Practice · Week 510 marks · 150 words

Public Interest Litigation has democratised access to justice at a cost. Comment.

Directive: "Comment." Accept the premise, quantify both halves, and end with a corrective rather than a verdict on whether PIL was worthwhile.
Skeleton
  1. The device
  2. What it delivered
  3. The cost
  4. The corrective

Model answer

PIL rests on a single procedural move: the relaxation of locus standi, formulated in S.P. Gupta (1981), so that any public-spirited person may move the court for those who cannot approach it themselves. It has no article of its own and no amendment behind it — it is judge-made procedure under Articles 32 and 226.

What it delivered

Hussainara Khatoon (1979) secured the release of around forty thousand undertrials and read a right to speedy trial into Article 21. Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984) reached bonded labourers. The epistolary jurisdiction, under which a letter is treated as a petition, removed the cost barrier altogether.

The cost

Three. PIL consumes the time of the highest courts while ordinary appeals wait. Courts lack the fiscal and technical machinery to design the policy their orders imply, and cannot be held accountable for outcomes. And the device is abused for publicity, private vendetta and political litigation.

The corrective

The courts' own answer has been exemplary costs and stricter scrutiny of the petitioner's motive — narrowing abuse without closing the door. That is the right instinct: the remedy for misuse of access is filtering, not restriction.

Value additions used: S.P. Gupta (1981) and the standing formulation · Hussainara Khatoon (1979) with the undertrial figure · Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984) · epistolary jurisdiction · exemplary costs as the judicial corrective.
Budget check: 174 / 150 words. Cut: the Vishaka example and the continuing-mandamus technique — both belong to activism rather than to access, which is what this question asks about. Bought: the closing line distinguishing filtering from restriction, which converts a list of costs into a position.
What would cost you marks: listing PIL's achievements without naming a single case or figure — this is an area where examiners expect names; and omitting the abuse problem, which is the "cost" the question explicitly asks you to address.

Last-mile revision — Judicial Review, Activism & PIL

  • Judicial review = power to pronounce on the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders. Not the wisdom of a law; not pre-enactment scrutiny; not Art 137 review of its own judgments.
  • Basis: Art 13 · Arts 32 and 226 · Arts 131–136 · Arts 245–246 + Seventh Schedule · Arts 251 and 254 · Art 372. There is no article headed "judicial review".
  • Grounds a law can fall on: violates Part III · outside legislative competence · offends another constitutional provision (for an amendment, basic structure). Not: unwise or inexpedient.
  • Minerva Mills (1980) struck down Art 368(4) and (5), inserted by the 42nd Amendment to exclude review of amendments.
  • US due process vs Indian procedure established by law (Art 21, borrowed from Japan). Gopalan (1950) narrow → Maneka Gandhi (1978) "right, just and fair".
  • Ladder: restraint → review → activism → overreach. Activism = positive enforcement; overreach = doing another organ's job.
  • PIL — locus standi relaxed. First: Hussainara Khatoon (1979), ~40,000 undertrials, speedy trial into Art 21. Doctrine: S.P. Gupta (1981), also the First Judges Case. Under Arts 32/226; no PIL article, no amendment. Epistolary jurisdiction; continuing mandamus; commissions and amicus curiae. Frivolous PIL → exemplary costs.
  • Activism instances: Vishaka (1997) (Act only in 2013 — a 16-year gap) · D.K. Basu (1997) · M.C. Mehta (1987) absolute liability · Vineet Narain (1998) · 2G and coal cancellations · electoral bonds struck down 2024 · Shayara Bano (2017), Navtej (2018), Joseph Shine (2018), Common Cause (2018).
  • April 2025 TN Governor — three options, timelines, Art 142 deemed assent to 10 Bills. May 2025 — 16th Presidential Reference, 14 questions, Art 143.
  • 20 Nov 2025 (2025 INSC 1333, 5 judges, unanimous, 111 pages): three options retained; no timelines; no deemed assent; actions not justiciable; Art 142 cannot supplant the Constitution; review is basic structure but "not an unbridled scope that can negate the separation of powers"; prolonged unexplained inaction → limited scrutiny; Art 361 does not bar examining validity.
  • Doctrines: eclipse (dormant, may revive) · severability (only the bad part falls) · waiver (FRs cannot be waived) · pith and substance · colourable legislation · manifest arbitrariness (electoral bonds, 2024) · proportionality (Puttaswamy, 2017) · constitutional morality (Navtej, 2018).
  • Ambedkar on Art 32 — the article "without which this Constitution would be a nullity"; the heart and soul.
  • Way forward: restraint in remedy, not in jurisdiction · guidelines expressly interim · legislative responsiveness · judicial accountability reform.
