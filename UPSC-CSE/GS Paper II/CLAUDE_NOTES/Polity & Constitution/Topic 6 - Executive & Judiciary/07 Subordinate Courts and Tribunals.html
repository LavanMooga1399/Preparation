Subordinate Courts & Tribunals

Articles 233–237 and Part XIV-A. The layer where almost every case in India actually begins — and the parallel system of tribunals that has been fought over in court for three decades, most recently in November 2025.

Articles 233–237 Arts 323A & 323B L. Chandra Kumar, 1997 Tribunals Reforms Act struck down, 2025 NALSA & Lok Adalats
7years at the Bar — the qualification for direct recruitment as a district judge
◆ A short chapter with an unusually high question density

Laxmikanth gives this chapter few pages, and students under-prepare it in proportion. That is a mistake: the Article 233 versus Article 234 split is the single most reliable question in the whole judiciary block, because it is a clean who-appoints-whom distinction with a genuine trap inside it. The tribunals half then supplies the current-affairs surface — the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 was struck down in November 2025, which puts it squarely inside the exam's news window.

Treat the access-to-justice material — NALSA, Lok Adalats, Gram Nyayalayas — as a fourth section of equal weight. Between 2009 and 2020 UPSC asked at least four questions from it, which is more than it asked about the district judiciary itself.

Week 5, Day 5 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 35.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Below the High Court, each district has two parallel ladders. On the civil side: the District Judge at the top, then subordinate judges, then munsiffs. On the criminal side: the Sessions Judge at the top, then chief judicial magistrates, then judicial magistrates. In practice the same person is usually both — District and Sessions Judge — wearing a civil hat in one court and a criminal hat in the other.

The appointment rule has a twist that the exam exists to test. For a district judge, the Governor appoints, in consultation with the High Court. For judges below district level, the Governor also appoints, but this time in consultation with the State Public Service Commission and the High Court. And once anyone is appointed, control over them — posting, promotion, leave, discipline — belongs to the High Court alone, not to the State government. Appointment is shared; control is not.

Tribunals are a different animal. They were added in 1976 to take specialised disputes out of the ordinary courts and decide them faster with expert members. The constitutional problem is that a tribunal decides cases where the government is usually the other party — so if the government also controls who sits on the tribunal, how long they serve and what they are paid, the tribunal is not independent. That single problem is the whole of the litigation from 1997 to 2025.

◆
Terminology worth fixing. A judge is called a District Judge when trying civil cases and a Sessions Judge when trying criminal ones — one office, two designations. In presidency towns the equivalent is styled the City Civil Court Judge and the Metropolitan Magistrate below it. Separately, do not confuse judicial magistrates, who are under the High Court, with executive magistrates, who are under the State government — the separation of the two is the practical content of Article 50, the Directive Principle on separating the judiciary from the executive.
!
Article 50 is a DPSP, and it is examined as one. "In India, separation of judiciary from the executive is enjoined by" — the answer, asked in Prelims 2020, is a Directive Principle of State Policy, not the Preamble, not the Seventh Schedule, not convention. Article 50 directs the State to take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State. It is non-justiciable, which is why executive magistrates still exist.

Appointment & control — Articles 233 to 237

ArticleProvisionThe detail that gets tested
233(1)Appointment of district judgesBy the Governor, in consultation with the High Court exercising jurisdiction in that State. The State Public Service Commission has no role.
233(2)Direct recruitment as district judgeA person not already in the service of the Union or the State is eligible only if they have been an advocate or pleader for not less than seven years, and are recommended by the High Court for appointment. Both conditions.
233AValidationInserted by the 20th Amendment, 1966 to validate appointments of district judges made otherwise than in accordance with Art 233, and judgments delivered by them.
234Recruitment of persons other than district judgesBy the Governor, in accordance with rules made after consultation with the State Public Service Commission and the High Court. Here the SPSC does appear.
235Control over subordinate courtsVested in the High Court — including posting, promotion and grant of leave to persons belonging to the State judicial service and holding a post inferior to that of district judge. Control is not shared with the executive.
236InterpretationDefines "district judge" to include a judge of a city civil court, additional district judge, joint district judge, assistant district judge, chief judge of a small cause court, chief presidency magistrate, additional chief presidency magistrate, sessions judge, additional sessions judge and assistant sessions judge. A wide definition, and occasionally examined as one.
237Application to magistratesThe Governor may, by public notification, direct that Arts 233–236 apply to any class of magistrates in the State.
!
The single highest-yield distinction in this note. Art 233 — district judges: Governor + High Court only. Art 234 — everyone below: Governor + State PSC and High Court. The State Public Service Commission is the discriminating item, and a statement inserting it into Article 233 is false. Attach a second fact to it: under Art 235, control over the whole subordinate judiciary lies with the High Court regardless of who appointed whom. Appointment is a shared power; control is exclusive.
03

Tribunals — Part XIV-A, Articles 323A and 323B

Part XIV-A was inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Two articles, and nearly every examinable point is a difference between them.

Article 323A — Administrative TribunalsArticle 323B — Tribunals for other matters
Only public service matters — recruitment and conditions of service.A listed range of subjects: taxation, foreign exchange, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, ceiling on urban property, elections to Parliament and State legislatures, food-stuffs, rent and tenancy.
Tribunals may be established only by Parliament.May be established by Parliament or a State legislature, according to their legislative competence.
Provides for one tribunal for the Union and one for each State, or a joint tribunal for two or more States — so a hierarchy of tribunals is not permitted.A hierarchy of tribunals is permitted.
Gave rise to the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 — the Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) and State Administrative Tribunals.Gave rise to a wide family of subject tribunals, several later abolished by the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021.
!
Three contrasts, and a fourth fact that overrides both articles. 323A is service matters / Parliament only / no hierarchy. 323B is listed subjects / Parliament or State legislature / hierarchy allowed. The overriding fact: both articles originally allowed the exclusion of the jurisdiction of all courts except the Supreme Court's Art 136 — and in L. Chandra Kumar (1997) the Supreme Court held those exclusion clauses unconstitutional, because judicial review by the High Courts under Arts 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure. So no tribunal today is immune from High Court review, whatever its parent statute says.

The Central Administrative Tribunal

🔴 Prelims — CAT facts

◆
Set up under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, during the Prime Ministership of Rajiv Gandhi — not Lal Bahadur Shastri, which is the distractor UPSC used in 2009.
◆
Members are drawn from both judicial and administrative streams — the feature that distinguishes a tribunal from a court.
◆
Jurisdiction: recruitment and service matters of All-India Services and Central civil services. It does not cover the defence forces, officers and servants of the Supreme Court, or the secretarial staff of Parliament.
◆
It is not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure; it is guided by the principles of natural justice — which is what makes it faster and cheaper than a court.
◆
Appeals from CAT lie to the High Court — a division bench of the High Court within whose jurisdiction the tribunal sits — following L. Chandra Kumar. Before 1997 they went straight to the Supreme Court.
◆
A State Administrative Tribunal is set up by the Centre on request of the State. There is also provision for a Joint Administrative Tribunal for two or more States.
!
Natural justice — its components, and what is not one. A tribunal is not bound by the CPC but is bound by natural justice, which means three things: audi alteram partem (both sides must be heard); nemo judex in causa sua (no one a judge in his own cause — hence recusal where there is a conflict of interest); and reasoned orders, so the decision can be understood and tested. What is not part of natural justice, and is offered as a distractor: judicial review of laws, and access to cheap justice. Both are desirable; neither is a rule of natural justice.
◆
Why tribunals exist at all — the case in one line each. Speed (no CPC, no adjournment culture); expertise (technical members alongside judicial ones); cost (cheaper, less formal); relief to the courts (removing whole categories of dispute from the docket). Every one of these advantages is real, and every one of them depends on the tribunal being independent — which is the argument of the next section.
04

The tribunals litigation — and the November 2025 judgment

📖 The story — thirty years of the same argument

1997 — L. Chandra Kumar. A seven-judge bench held that the power of judicial review vested in the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227, and in the Supreme Court under Article 32, is part of the basic structure. Clauses in Articles 323A and 323B excluding that review were struck down. Tribunals could continue as courts of first instance, but their decisions would now be appealable to a division bench of the High Court.

2019 — Rojer Mathew. The Finance Act, 2017 had reorganised tribunals and left appointments and service conditions to executive rule-making. The Court struck down those rules for undermining judicial independence. It also referred a separate question to a larger bench — whether the Speaker's certification of a Bill as a Money Bill is immune from review — which remains pending and is worth carrying into any Money Bill answer.

2021 — the Act. Parliament enacted the Tribunals Reforms Act on 13 August 2021. It abolished several appellate tribunals — the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal, the Intellectual Property Appellate Board, the Airport Appellate Tribunal among them — and moved their work to the High Courts. It fixed a four-year term, a minimum age of 50, a search-cum-selection committee that would send two names per vacancy for the government to choose from, and service conditions tied to those of equivalent civil servants.

The problem: almost all of this had already been struck down in the Madras Bar Association cases, where the Court had held that tenure must be at least five years and that advocates with ten years of practice must be eligible.

20 November 2025 — Madras Bar Association. The Court struck down the impugned provisions as an impermissible legislative override. Parliament, it held, cannot re-enact in slightly altered form what has already been invalidated without curing the underlying defect. It directed that a National Tribunal Commission be constituted within four months as an "essential structural safeguard", and that until a compliant law is passed, the directions in the earlier Madras Bar Association judgments would govern.

!
The concept to carry into Mains: the legislative override. A legislature may validly remove the basis of a judgment — by curing the defect the court identified. It may not simply re-enact the invalidated provision in new words. The 2025 judgment is the cleanest recent illustration, and the doctrine transfers directly to any question on the separation of powers or on Parliament's response to judicial decisions.

Tribunalisation — the balanced case

The case forThe case against
Rationalisation. Abolishing small, under-used tribunals reduces fragmentation and makes judicial review more consistent.Judicial independence. A four-year term with executive-controlled reappointment creates dependence — decisive when the Union is the largest litigant before these bodies.
Uniformity. One statute covering appointments and service conditions brings predictability across dozens of bodies.Re-enactment of struck-down provisions. Restoring invalidated tenure and age rules in new language is a direct challenge to the Court's authority.
Speed in appointments. A single nodal authority avoids delays caused by multiple ministries.Exclusion of talent. A minimum age of 50 shuts out capable lawyers and domain experts in their forties.
Experience. An age threshold ensures maturity in technically complex adjudication.Burden shifting. Moving abolished tribunals' caseloads onto already-overloaded High Courts hurts access to justice and loses technical expertise.
◆
Way forward — the four points a Mains answer needs. (1) A fresh, Constitution-compliant statute with a minimum five-year tenure and reasonable age limits. (2) Constitute and empower the National Tribunal Commission to handle appointments, evaluation and administration, insulating tribunals from ministerial control. (3) Retain genuinely specialised tribunals while preserving High Court supervision through Arts 226 and 227. (4) Transparent, merit-based appointments with published criteria and reasoned decisions.
05

Access to justice — NALSA, Lok Adalats, Gram Nyayalayas

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Article 39A, a Directive Principle inserted by the 42nd Amendment, directs the State to secure that the legal system operates to promote justice on the basis of equal opportunity, and in particular to provide free legal aid so that no citizen is denied justice by reason of economic or other disabilities. Everything in this section is the machinery built to implement that one article.

Free legal aid comes from a pyramid of authorities under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987: NALSA at the top, State Legal Services Authorities below, then District and Taluk committees. Lok Adalats are the settlement forums those authorities organise — not courts in the ordinary sense, but bodies whose settlements have the force of a court decree.

◆
Article 39A is a Directive Principle, but the courts made it enforceable through Article 21 — and that is the examinable move. A DPSP is not justiciable on its own; the Supreme Court instead read free legal aid into the fair-procedure guarantee of Art 21, which is. Three cases carry the story: M.H. Hoskot (1978) — a convicted person is entitled to free legal services for an appeal; Hussainara Khatoon (1979) — the undertrial-prisoners case, which held free legal aid to be an essential ingredient of reasonable, fair and just procedure under Art 21, and also gave us the right to a speedy trial; and Khatri v. State of Bihar (1981) — the State's duty arises when the accused is first produced before a magistrate, not merely at trial, and cannot be refused on grounds of cost. Read alongside Note 08, where Hussainara Khatoon appears again as one of the founding PILs.
!
Who is entitled, and the one figure to carry. Under section 12 of the 1987 Act, legal services are available to a member of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe, a victim of trafficking or beggar, a woman or a child, a person with a disability, a victim of a mass disaster or ethnic violence, an industrial workman, a person in custody, and any person whose annual income falls below the prescribed limit. Note the structure the setter exploits: the income test applies only to the last category — a woman, a child, or a person in custody is entitled irrespective of income. A statement making legal aid conditional on income across the board is therefore false.

BodyWhat to know
NALSAConstituted under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (in force from 1995). Patron-in-Chief: the Chief Justice of India. Executive Chairman: the second senior-most judge of the Supreme Court. Its objective is to provide free and competent legal services to weaker sections on the basis of equal opportunity, and it issues guidelines for the State authorities. At State level, the Chief Justice of the High Court is Patron-in-Chief. Both limbs were tested in Prelims 2013.
Who gets free legal aid — s.12Regardless of income: a member of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe; a woman; a child; a victim of trafficking or beggar; a person with a disability; a person in custody; a victim of a mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster; an industrial workman. Subject to an income ceiling: any other person, where annual income is below the limit prescribed — ₹5 lakh for cases before the Supreme Court and as prescribed by the State government otherwise.
Lok AdalatA forum for compromise or settlement. It has jurisdiction over matters pending before a court and matters at the pre-litigation stage. It can take civil matters and compoundable criminal matters — but not non-compoundable offences. It can take matrimonial and family disputes; only the divorce itself and non-compoundable criminal matters are outside it.
The Lok Adalat awardDeemed a decree of a civil court and final and binding on all parties. No appeal lies against it to any court — because it rests on the parties' own consent. If no settlement is reached, the case simply returns to the court. Court fees already paid are refunded.
CompositionPresided over by a serving or retired judicial officer, but with other persons — typically a lawyer and a social worker. So the statement that a Lok Adalat consists of judicial officers only is false — tested in Prelims 2010.
Permanent Lok AdalatCreated by a 2002 amendment for public utility services — transport, postal, telegraph, power, water, sanitation, hospitals, insurance. Pecuniary jurisdiction up to ₹1 crore. Crucially, if the parties fail to settle, a Permanent Lok Adalat may decide the dispute on merits — unlike an ordinary Lok Adalat, which can only record a settlement.
Gram NyayalayaUnder the Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 (in force 2009), at the intermediate panchayat level. Presided over by a Nyayadhikari with the rank and status of a First Class Judicial Magistrate, appointed by the State government in consultation with the High Court. It hears both civil and criminal cases — so a statement confining it to civil cases is false. It is a mobile court, is not bound by the Indian Evidence Act, and may accept local social activists as mediators or conciliators. Tested in Prelims 2016.
National Lok AdalatNot a separate institution but a mode of organisation: NALSA holds Lok Adalats on a single day across the whole country — every court from the taluk level to the Supreme Court sitting simultaneously — usually once a quarter, often on a chosen subject. It is the vehicle behind the very large disposal figures quoted for Lok Adalats, and the mechanism most likely to appear in a current-affairs framing.
Family CourtsUnder the Family Courts Act, 1984, set up by State governments in consultation with the High Court, mandatory in cities with population above one million. Jurisdiction covers matrimonial causes — nullity, restitution, judicial separation and divorce — plus maintenance, guardianship and custody, and property disputes between spouses. Two design features are examinable: the Act requires the court to attempt settlement first and is not bound by the Indian Evidence Act; and a party has no right to be represented by a lawyer as of right — the court may permit a legal expert as amicus curiae. Appeal lies to the High Court, and preference is given to women in appointing judges.
!
The two facts most often reversed. (1) A Lok Adalat award is final with no appeal — but that is because it is a consent settlement, not because it is beyond challenge in principle; if the parties do not settle, nothing binding happens at all. (2) A Gram Nyayalaya hears both civil and criminal matters, and does use local social activists as mediators. Prelims 2016 offered both propositions and only the second was true. Learn them as a pair, since they appear together.

Alternative dispute resolution — the statutes, in order

StatuteYearWhat it does
Arbitration and Conciliation Act1996Based on the UNCITRAL Model Law. Amended in 2015, 2019 and 2021; the 2019 amendment created the Arbitration Council of India. An arbitral award is enforceable as a decree; the ground of challenge is narrow.
Legal Services Authorities Act1987NALSA, SLSAs, Lok Adalats; Permanent Lok Adalats added in 2002.

Provides for pre-litigation mediation, a Mediation Council of India, and makes a mediated settlement agreement enforceable as a judgment or decree of a court. !
The four ADR mechanisms are not interchangeable, and that is the question. Arbitration — a third party decides, and the award binds. Conciliation — a third party proposes terms; the settlement binds only when accepted. Mediation — a third party facilitates; the parties craft their own terms. Negotiation — no third party at all. A statement saying a mediator "decides" the dispute is false; a statement saying an arbitrator does is true.

(A) Genuine previous-year questions

2009UPSC Prelims · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) was set up during the Prime Ministership of Lal Bahadur Shastri.
  2. The Members for CAT are drawn from both judicial and administrative streams.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (b) — 2 only
  • Statement 1 is wrong on the date by two decades. CAT came from the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 — Rajiv Gandhi's Prime Ministership. Shastri's tenure was 1964–66, before Part XIV-A even existed; Articles 323A and 323B were inserted in 1976.
  • Statement 2 is the defining feature of a tribunal: judicial and administrative members sitting together, which is what supplies the technical expertise a court lacks.

M38Practice · Week 515 marks · 250 words

"Tribunalisation promised speed and expertise but has repeatedly been used to expand executive control over adjudication." Critically examine in the light of recent judicial pronouncements.

Directive: "Critically examine." Concede the genuine case for tribunals before attacking their design, and end with a workable remedy rather than a complaint.
Skeleton
  1. Why tribunals exist
  2. The structural defect
  3. The judicial lineage, 1997 to 2025
  4. Way forward
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Part XIV-A, inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, created tribunals to deliver speed, expertise and lower cost — freed from the Code of Civil Procedure, staffed by technical members alongside judicial ones, and relieving the ordinary courts of whole categories of dispute. That case remains sound.

The structural defect

Tribunals adjudicate disputes in which the Union is usually the opposing party. Where the same executive controls appointment, tenure, reappointment and service conditions, independence is compromised at the point it matters most. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 concentrated exactly these levers: a four-year term, a minimum age of 50, two names per vacancy for the government to pick from, and service conditions pegged to equivalent civil servants.

The judicial lineage

S.P. Sampath Kumar (1987) upheld tribunals as an effective substitute for High Courts. L. Chandra Kumar (1997) reversed that premise: review under Arts 226 and 227 is basic structure, so tribunals are supplementary and their orders appealable to a High Court division bench. Rojer Mathew (2019) struck down executive rule-making on appointments. In November 2025 the Court struck down the 2021 Act's provisions as an impermissible legislative override — Parliament may remove the basis of a judgment, but not re-enact an invalidated rule in new words.

Way forward

The Court's own direction supplies it: a National Tribunal Commission within four months, and a fresh statute with a minimum five-year tenure and eligibility for advocates of ten years' standing.

Tribunalisation is not the problem; tribunal design by the largest litigant is. Independence is the precondition of the efficiency tribunals were created to deliver, not a trade-off against it.

Value additions used: Arts 323A/323B and the 42nd Amendment · Sampath Kumar (1987) → L. Chandra Kumar (1997) → Rojer Mathew (2019) → Madras Bar Association (Nov 2025) · the four struck-down provisions with figures · legislative override versus removal of basis · the National Tribunal Commission direction.
Budget check: 279 / 250 words. Cut: the 323A-versus-323B contrast table and the list of tribunals abolished in 2021 — both are Prelims material and neither advances the independence argument. Bought: the override-versus-removal-of-basis distinction, which is the one line that shows you understand why the Act fell rather than merely that it fell.
What would cost you marks: treating this as a pros-and-cons list of tribunals in general — the question is about control; and omitting L. Chandra Kumar, which is the constitutional anchor for everything after it.
M39Practice · Week 510 marks · 150 words

Examine the role of Lok Adalats and Gram Nyayalayas in realising the promise of Article 39A.

Directive: "Examine." Assess how far the machinery delivers what the article promises, and name what holds it back.
Skeleton
  1. What Art 39A requires
  2. What Lok Adalats deliver
  3. What Gram Nyayalayas were meant to deliver
  4. The gap
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Article 39A, inserted by the 42nd Amendment, requires the State to secure equal justice and to provide free legal aid so that no citizen is denied justice by economic or other disability. The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 is its principal implementing statute.

Lok Adalats

They reach both pre-litigation disputes and pending cases, their award is deemed a decree of a civil court with no appeal, and court fees are refunded on settlement. Because the outcome rests on consent, it disposes of a dispute rather than merely deciding it. Permanent Lok Adalats, added in 2002 for public utility services up to ₹1 crore, go further and may decide on merits where settlement fails.

Gram Nyayalayas

The 2008 Act took the court to the litigant: a mobile court at intermediate panchayat level, presided over by a Nyayadhikari of First Class Judicial Magistrate rank, hearing civil and criminal matters and not bound by the Indian Evidence Act.

The gap

Lok Adalats work only where parties are willing, so they cannot reach contested rights claims. Gram Nyayalayas have been notified in a small fraction of the numbers envisaged, chiefly for want of State funding and judicial officers.

Article 39A's promise is being met in volume but not in reach: settlement machinery has scaled, adjudicatory machinery at the village level has not.

Value additions used: Art 39A and the 42nd Amendment · Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 · award as deemed decree, no appeal, fee refund · Permanent Lok Adalats, 2002, ₹1 crore, merits jurisdiction · Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008, Nyayadhikari, Evidence Act exemption.
Budget check: 181 / 150 words. Cut: the NALSA hierarchy and the section 12 eligibility list — both are Prelims facts, and the question is about Lok Adalats and Gram Nyayalayas specifically. Bought: the closing volume-versus-reach distinction, which converts a description into an assessment and is what the directive "examine" is asking for.
What would cost you marks: describing both institutions without ever returning to Article 39A — the article is the yardstick, and an answer that never measures against it has not answered the question.

Last-mile revision — Subordinate Courts & Tribunals

  • Art 233 district judges — Governor + High Court only. Direct recruit: 7 years as advocate/pleader and recommended by the High Court. 233A validation, 20th Amdt, 1966.
  • Art 234 below district judge — Governor + State PSC + High Court. The SPSC is the discriminator.
  • Art 235 — control (posting, promotion, leave) with the High Court. 236 wide definition of "district judge". 237 extension to magistrates by the Governor.
  • Art 50 DPSP — separation of judiciary from executive. Non-justiciable; hence executive magistrates persist. Art 39A DPSP — free legal aid, 42nd Amdt.
  • Sessions judge may pass any sentence including death — no effect until the High Court confirms, appeal or not. CJM up to 7 years; JM up to 3 years.
  • Part XIV-A, 42nd Amdt 1976, on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation.
  • 323A — service matters · Parliament only · no hierarchy · one for Union + one per State. 323B — listed subjects (taxation, foreign exchange, labour, land reforms, elections, rent, food-stuffs) · Parliament or State legislature · hierarchy allowed.
  • CAT — Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 (Rajiv Gandhi, not Shastri). Judicial and administrative members. Not for defence forces, SC staff, parliamentary secretarial staff. Not bound by CPC. Appeals to a division bench of the High Court after 1997.
  • Lineage: Sampath Kumar (1987) substitute → L. Chandra Kumar (1997) supplementary, Arts 226/227 review is basic structure → Rojer Mathew (2019) → MBA IV & V (5-yr tenure, 10-yr advocates) → Nov 2025 Tribunals Reforms Act struck down as a legislative override; National Tribunal Commission in 4 months.
  • 2021 Act's struck-down features: 4-year tenure · minimum age 50 · two names per vacancy · service conditions pegged to civil servants.
  • NALSA — Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. Patron-in-Chief: CJI. Executive Chairman: second senior-most SC judge.
  • Free legal aid irrespective of income: SC/ST, women, children, trafficking victims, persons with disabilities, persons in custody, industrial workmen, mass-disaster victims. Others: below the prescribed ceiling (₹5 lakh for the Supreme Court).
  • Lok Adalat — pending and pre-litigation; civil and compoundable criminal; matrimonial matters included (not the divorce decree); bench includes non-judicial members; award is a deemed civil decree, no appeal; court fees refunded. Permanent Lok Adalat — 2002, public utilities, up to ₹1 crore, may decide on merits.
