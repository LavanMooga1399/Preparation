The High Court

Articles 214–231. Almost every fact here is examined as a contrast with the Supreme Court — a different retirement age, a wider writ power, a supervisory jurisdiction the apex court does not have, and an appointment process with one extra participant.

Articles 214–231 25 High Courts Art 226 — wider than Art 32 Art 227 — superintendence Retire at 62
25High Courts for 28 States and 8 Union Territories — the mismatch is the point
◆ Study this as a difference table, not as a fresh chapter

Roughly seventy per cent of this chapter repeats Note 05 with different numbers. That is exactly why setters mine it: the question is rarely "what does a High Court do", it is "which of these is true of the High Court but not the Supreme Court". Build the contrasts deliberately — 62 against 65, ten years' advocacy but no distinguished-jurist route, Art 226 wider than Art 32, Art 227 with no Supreme Court equivalent.

One structural fact carries disproportionate weight: a High Court is not a State organ in the ordinary sense. Its judges are appointed by the President, its jurisdiction is set by Parliament, and one High Court may serve several States and Union Territories. It sits inside the integrated judiciary, which is why it can strike down a central law — a proposition tested directly in Prelims 2019.

Week 5, Day 4 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 34.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Every State has a High Court at the top of its own court system. But the mapping is not one-to-one. Article 231 lets Parliament establish a common High Court for two or more States, and for Union Territories. That is why there are 25 High Courts rather than 28 — the Punjab and Haryana High Court serves two States and Chandigarh, the Bombay High Court serves Maharashtra, Goa and two Union Territories, and the Guwahati High Court serves four north-eastern States.

The High Court's most powerful tool is Article 226. Like the Supreme Court, it can issue writs. Unlike the Supreme Court, it can issue them not only to enforce Fundamental Rights but also "for any other purpose" — meaning for the enforcement of an ordinary legal right too. So on writs, the High Court's door is wider than the apex court's. Students routinely get this backwards.

It also has something the Supreme Court simply does not: Article 227, a power of superintendence over every court and tribunal within its territory. That is administrative as well as judicial — calling for returns, prescribing forms, settling practice. It is the reason the High Court, not the Supreme Court, is the real supervisor of the justice system on the ground.

Composition & appointment — Articles 214–224A

PointDetail
EstablishmentArticle 214 — there shall be a High Court for each State. Article 231 — Parliament may establish a common High Court for two or more States and/or Union Territories.
StrengthThe Constitution fixes no number. Each High Court has a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary — so strength is fixed by the President, not by Parliament. This is a clean contrast with the Supreme Court, where Parliament by law sets the number.

!
Four contrasts that decide most High Court questions. (1) Supreme Court judge strength is fixed by Parliament by law; High Court strength by the President, with no number in the Constitution. (2) Retirement 65 versus 62. (3) The distinguished jurist route exists only for the Supreme Court; a High Court needs ten years of judicial office or advocacy. (4) A retired judge is recalled to the Supreme Court by the CJI under Art 128, and to a High Court by that High Court's Chief Justice under Art 224A — in both cases with the President's previous consent.
◆
The judicial-office route has an asymmetry worth noticing. For the Supreme Court, Art 124(3)(a) requires five years as a High Court judge — service in a subordinate court does not count. For a High Court, Art 217(2)(a) requires ten years in judicial office, which does include the district judiciary. So a district judge can rise to a High Court, but cannot go directly to the Supreme Court.

How many High Courts, and the ones worth naming

🔴 Prelims — the institutional facts

◆
25 High Courts in total. The most recent is the Andhra Pradesh High Court (2019), created on the bifurcation of the common Hyderabad High Court into separate Telangana and Andhra Pradesh High Courts.
◆
The three oldest were established under the Indian High Courts Act, 1861 — Calcutta, Bombay and Madras, all in 1862. Calcutta is the oldest.
◆
Delhi is the only Union Territory with its own High Court (since 1966). Every other UT falls under some State's High Court.
◆
Common High Courts: Punjab & Haryana (Punjab, Haryana, Chandigarh); Bombay (Maharashtra, Goa, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu); Guwahati (Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh); Madras (Tamil Nadu, Puducherry); Kerala (Kerala, Lakshadweep).
◆
Seats and benches: the seat is fixed by the President; benches elsewhere are established by the President too. Several High Courts sit away from their State capital — Allahabad, Jabalpur, Nainital, Kolkata.
◆
Pendency stands at roughly 6.4 crore cases across the High Courts, with the Allahabad High Court alone above 11.9 lakh. Against a sanctioned strength of about 1,122 judges, roughly 371 posts were vacant in early 2026 — about a third.

Jurisdiction & powers

The Constitution does not list the High Court's jurisdiction the way it lists the Supreme Court's. Article 225 simply continues whatever jurisdiction existed immediately before the Constitution, which is why the standard taxonomy is textbook-derived rather than article-by-article.

JurisdictionWhat it covers
OriginalLimited. Covers admiralty, probate, matrimonial and contempt matters; disputes relating to elections to Parliament and State legislatures; revenue matters; and, in Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi, ordinary original civil jurisdiction above a pecuniary limit. Note that election petitions come here, not to the Supreme Court's Art 131.
Writ — Art 226To any person or authority, including government, for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose. See Section 03 — this is the most examinable provision in the chapter.
AppellateCivil — appeals from district courts and subordinate courts. Criminal — appeals from sessions courts, with confirmation of death sentences being mandatory: a death sentence passed by a sessions court has no effect until confirmed by the High Court, whether or not the convict appeals.
Supervisory — Art 227Superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territorial jurisdiction — except any court or tribunal constituted under a law relating to the Armed Forces. Both judicial and administrative: it may call for returns, make and issue general rules, prescribe forms, and settle the fees of officers.
Control over subordinate courts — Art 235Control over district courts and courts subordinate to them, including posting, promotion and leave of persons in the State judicial service below district judge. Developed in Note 07.
Court of record — Art 215Records are of evidentiary value and cannot be questioned; power to punish for contempt of itself. The High Court also has power to punish for contempt of subordinate courts, which the Supreme Court does not exercise in the same way.
Transfer of cases — Art 228If satisfied that a case pending in a subordinate court involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, the High Court shall withdraw the case and either dispose of it itself or determine the question and return the case.
Judicial reviewNot expressly conferred, but implicit in Arts 13 and 226. A High Court can declare a central law unconstitutional — see the 2019 PYQ in Section 06.
!
The armed-forces exclusion appears twice, in two different articles, and means two different things. Art 136 — the Supreme Court cannot grant special leave to appeal from an armed-forces court or tribunal. Art 227 — a High Court has no power of superintendence over one. Same carve-out, different power. A statement swapping the two articles is false, and this is exactly the kind of pairing a matching question is built from.

Article 226 versus Article 32 — the comparison that gets tested most

Article 32 — Supreme CourtArticle 226 — High Court
Available only for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.Available for Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose — including an ordinary legal right. Wider.
Is itself a Fundamental Right (Part III). Ambedkar: the "heart and soul" of the Constitution.Is a constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right.
The Supreme Court cannot refuse relief where a Fundamental Right is violated — the remedy is guaranteed.The High Court's writ power is discretionary; it may decline, for instance where an alternative remedy exists.
Territorial reach is all India.Reach is the High Court's own territorial jurisdiction — extended by the 15th Amendment, 1963, which added Art 226(2): a High Court may act where the cause of action arises wholly or in part within its territory, even if the authority sits outside it.
Can be suspended during a National Emergency — Art 359 allows the President to suspend the right to move any court for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights. !
The one that catches nearly everyone. On scope, Art 226 is wider than Art 32 — because of the words "and for any other purpose". On strength of the guarantee, Art 32 is stronger, because it is itself a Fundamental Right and the Court cannot refuse relief. A statement saying "Article 32 is wider in scope than Article 226" is false; a statement saying "the remedy under Article 32 is a Fundamental Right whereas that under Article 226 is not" is true. Both propositions live in the same question.
📖 The story — why the 15th Amendment had to fix Article 226

The original Article 226 let a High Court issue writs only to an authority located within its own territory. That produced a peculiar result: because the Union Government sits in Delhi, a challenge to almost any central decision had to be brought in the Punjab High Court, which then had Delhi within its jurisdiction — no matter where in India the person affected actually lived.

In Election Commission v. Saka Venkata Rao (1953) the Supreme Court confirmed this narrow territorial reading. The consequence was plain injustice: a fisherman in Kerala aggrieved by a central order had to litigate at the other end of the country.

The 15th Amendment, 1963 added Article 226(2), allowing a High Court to act if the cause of action arose wholly or in part within its territory, even where the authority sits elsewhere. The same amendment raised the High Court retirement age from 60 to 62 — so if a question links the 15th Amendment to High Courts, both facts are candidates.

04

The five writs

Borrowed from English law and available under both Art 32 and Art 226. Learn each by its literal meaning, then by the one situation it does not cover.

WritLiterallyIssued to do what — and where it does not lie
Habeas corpus"to have the body"Orders a person detaining another to produce them before the court. Lies against both public authorities and private persons — the only writ that does. Not available where detention is lawful, by a competent court, or for contempt of a legislature or court.
Mandamus"we command"Commands a public official, corporation, inferior court, tribunal or government to perform a public duty it has failed to perform. Does not lie against a private individual or body unless entrusted with a public duty; against a duty that is discretionary rather than mandatory; against the President or a State Governor; or against a Chief Justice acting in an administrative capacity. Tested in Prelims 2022.
Prohibition"to forbid"Issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to stop it exceeding its jurisdiction — preventive, and issued while proceedings are still pending. Lies only against judicial and quasi-judicial bodies, never against administrative bodies, legislatures or private individuals. Tested in Prelims 2024.
Certiorari"to be certified"Issued by a higher court to transfer a pending case to itself or to quash an order already passed — so it is both preventive and curative, which is the standard contrast with prohibition. Since 1991 it also lies against administrative authorities affecting rights, not only judicial ones.
Quo warranto"by what authority"Enquires into the legality of a person's claim to a public office. Lies only where the office is substantive, public and permanent and created by statute or the Constitution — never for a private or ministerial office. Uniquely, any interested person may apply; the applicant need not be personally aggrieved. Tested in Prelims 2022.
!
Three "only" facts about writs, which is where statement questions come from. Habeas corpus is the only writ available against a private person. Quo warranto is the only writ any interested person may seek without being personally aggrieved. Prohibition and certiorari lie only against judicial or quasi-judicial bodies — with the post-1991 extension of certiorari to administrative authorities being the exception that proves the rule. Prohibition is preventive; certiorari is preventive and curative.

Constituent Assembly ammunition

◆ For any "should the High Courts be strengthened?" question

The Assembly deliberately made the High Court a constitutional court rather than a State court. Judges are appointed by the President, removed by Parliament, paid from the State fund but pensioned from the Union fund, and may be transferred between States. Each of those choices pulls the institution away from the State government it must most often rule against.

The argument this gives you in Mains: complaints that the High Courts are "State" courts subject to local pressure misread the design. Their weakness is not constitutional but administrative — a third of sanctioned posts lie vacant and pendency stands above six crore. The remedy is filling the bench, not redesigning the institution.

◆
Additional judges under Art 224 are appointed for not more than two years — and never beyond age 62.
◆
A death sentence from a sessions court has no effect until the High Court confirms it — appeal or no appeal.

!
Article 224 and 224A are two different provisions and are routinely merged. Art 224 covers additional judges (appointed for up to two years to clear arrears) and acting judges (a qualified person acting while a permanent judge is temporarily absent, or is himself acting as Chief Justice). Art 224A is the retired judge, requested to sit by the High Court's Chief Justice with the President's previous consent. Calling 224A "acting judge" merges the two. Hold them apart as: 224 = extra serving judges · 224A = a judge brought back.

Prelims Questions

(A) Genuine previous-year questions

2019UPSC Prelims · Statements

With reference to the Constitution of India, consider the following statements:

  1. No High Court shall have the jurisdiction to declare any central law to be constitutionally invalid.
  2. An amendment to the Constitution of India cannot be called into question by the Supreme Court of India.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (d) — Neither 1 nor 2
  • Statement 1 is wrong. India has an integrated judiciary, and a High Court exercising judicial review under Arts 13 and 226 can strike down a central law. There is no rule confining High Courts to State legislation.
  • Statement 2 is wrong. Since Kesavananda Bharati (1973) a constitutional amendment can be struck down for violating the basic structure — as the 99th Amendment was in 2015.
  • This is a "both wrong" answer set, which candidates under-select. Judge each statement on its own; do not assume a question must contain at least one true statement.
2021UPSC Prelims · Statements

With reference to the Indian judiciary, consider the following statements:

  1. Any retired judge of the Supreme Court of India can be called back to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge by the Chief Justice of India with prior permission of the President of India.
  2. A High Court in India has the power to review its own judgment as the Supreme Court does.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (b) — 2 only
  • Statement 2 is the High Court fact worth holding: as a court of record under Art 215, a High Court has an inherent power of review. The Supreme Court's is express, under Art 137.
  • Statement 1 fails on the word "any" — Art 128 also requires the retired judge's own consent.
  • Repeated here from Note 05 deliberately: the same PYQ tests one Supreme Court fact and one High Court fact.
2024UPSC Prelims · Direct

A Writ of Prohibition is an order issued by the Supreme Court or High Courts to

  • (a) A government officer prohibiting him from taking a particular action.
  • (b) The Parliament/Legislative Assembly to pass a law on Prohibition.
  • (c) The lower court prohibiting continuation of proceedings in a case.
  • (d) The Government prohibiting it from following an unconstitutional policy.
Answer: (c)
  • Prohibition runs from a higher court to a lower court or tribunal, and only while proceedings are pending. That directional fact is the whole question.
  • (a) and (d) are wrong because prohibition does not lie against administrative or executive authorities — only judicial and quasi-judicial bodies.
  • If the situation involves an order that has already been passed, the writ is certiorari, not prohibition. See Fig 2 in Section 04.
2022UPSC Prelims · Statements

With reference to the writs issued by the Courts in India, consider the following statements:

  1. Mandamus will not lie against a private organisation unless it is entrusted with a public duty.
  2. Mandamus will not lie against a Company even though it may be a Government Company.
  3. Any public minded person can be a petitioner to move the Court to obtain the writ of Quo Warranto.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 2 and 3 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (c) — 1 and 3 only
  • Statement 1 states the rule correctly: the test for mandamus is a public duty, not the public or private character of the body.
  • Statement 2 is wrong for the same reason — a Government Company discharging a public duty can be subject to mandamus. The word "even though" gives away the trap.
  • Statement 3 is the quo warranto exception to locus standi: the petitioner need not be personally aggrieved. It is the only writ with that feature.
2013UPSC Prelims · Direct

Which one of the following statements is correct?

  • (a) In India, the same person cannot be appointed as Governor for two or more States at the same time
  • (b) The Judges of the High Court of the States in India are appointed by the Governor of the State just as the Judges of the Supreme Court are appointed by the President
  • (c) No procedure has been laid down in the Constitution of India for the removal of a Governor from his/her post
  • (d) In the case of a Union Territory having a legislative setup, the Chief Minister is appointed by the Lt. Governor on the basis of majority support
Answer: (c)
  • Included in this note for option (b), which is the High Court fact being tested and is wrong: High Court judges are appointed by the President, not the Governor. The Governor is only consulted under Art 217(1).

Practice · Negative

Which one of the following statements regarding the High Courts is NOT correct?

  • (a) A judge of a High Court holds office until attaining the age of sixty-two years
  • (b) The Constitution provides that a distinguished jurist may be appointed a judge of a High Court
  • (c) The salaries of High Court judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State
  • (d) A judge of a High Court may resign by writing addressed to the President
Answer: (b) is NOT correct

Practice · Negative

Which one of the following is NOT a correct statement about Article 226?

  • (a) It empowers a High Court to issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose
  • (b) The power conferred by it is discretionary and a High Court may decline relief where an alternative remedy exists
  • (c) It is a Fundamental Right and cannot be suspended during a National Emergency
  • (d) Its territorial reach was widened by the Fifteenth Amendment, 1963
Answer: (c) is NOT correct
  • Article 226 is a constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right. Only Article 32, sitting in Part III, is itself a Fundamental Right.
  • The second half of (c) is a further error compounding the first: Art 359 permits suspension of the right to move any court for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights during a National Emergency — with Arts 20 and 21 protected since the 44th Amendment.

  • (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is not correct
  • (d) Statement-I is not correct but Statement-II is correct
Answer: (a)
  • Both are correct, and Statement-II is the exact reason Statement-I is true. The words "and for any other purpose" are what make Art 226 wider; Art 32 is confined to Fundamental Rights.
  • The reflex to resist: "wider" feels wrong for a High Court against the Supreme Court. It is right — but only on scope. On the strength of the guarantee, Art 32 is the stronger remedy.
  • Three-step method: truth of I, truth of II, then the link. Do not judge the link first.
Practice · Pairs

Consider the following pairs of Article and subject:

  1. Article 215 — High Court as a court of record
  2. Article 222 — transfer of a judge from one High Court to another
  3. Article 224A — appointment of additional judges
  4. Article 231 — common High Court for two or more States

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) Only three
  • (d) All four
Answer: (c) — only three
  • Pair 3 is the mismatch. Article 224 covers additional and acting judges; Article 224A is the retired judge sitting at the request of the High Court's Chief Justice.
  • Pairs 1, 2 and 4 are correct as stated.
  • The 224 / 224A pair is exactly the kind of near-identical numbering the setter looks for. Learn them as opposites: 224 = extra serving judges, 224A = a judge brought back.
Practice · Statements

Consider the following statements:

  1. A common High Court for two or more States can be established by the President by order.
  2. Delhi is the only Union Territory with its own High Court.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (b) — 2 only
  • Statement 1 is wrong on the instrument. Under Article 231 it is Parliament by law that establishes a common High Court — not a Presidential order. The President's powers here are to fix judge strength and to appoint benches.
  • Statement 2 is correct. Every other Union Territory falls under a State's High Court.
  • Same construction as the 2026 strength question in Note 05: the substance is right and the instrument is wrong. Always check who acts, and by what device.
Practice · Direct

Under which one of the following Articles does a High Court exercise superintendence over all courts and tribunals within its territorial jurisdiction?

  • (a) Article 226
  • (b) Article 227
  • (c) Article 228
  • (d) Article 235
Answer: (b) — Article 227
  • Article 227 is the supervisory jurisdiction, judicial and administrative, subject to the armed-forces exclusion.
  • The neighbours are all real powers and all different: 226 writs, 228 withdrawal of a case involving a constitutional question from a subordinate court, 235 control over the district judiciary.
  • 226–228 and 235 sit close together and do four distinct jobs. Learn them as a block of four, not individually.

(C) Trap statements — True or False

◆ Why this bucket exists, and how to work it

Twenty-one of the fifty questions in the Week 4 paper were “how many of the above” — 42 per cent — and that format scored 52 per cent. It cannot be solved by elimination: every statement has to be judged on its own. So the skill the paper actually rewards is not answering questions, it is marking a single statement true or false.

That is all this bucket is. 20 statements from this note, one at a time. Decide before you click. If you find yourself reasoning about how many ought to be true, stop — that instinct is what the format punishes.

Roughly half are false, and most of the false ones are wrong by one word: an authority, a number, a fraction, or a qualifier. That is how this setter builds them.

Trap 1 · True or False

There shall be a High Court for each State, and Parliament may establish a common High Court for two or more States.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Articles 214 and 231. This is why there are 25 High Courts for 28 States and 8 Union Territories.
Trap 2 · True or False

A common High Court for two or more States is established by the President by order.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • By Parliament, by law — Article 231. The President's powers here are to fix judge strength and establish benches.
Trap 3 · True or False

The number of judges of a High Court is fixed by Parliament by law.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • By the President. The Constitution fixes no number. It is Supreme Court strength that Parliament sets by law.
Trap 4 · True or False

A judge of a High Court holds office until the age of sixty-two years.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Raised from 60 by the 15th Amendment, 1963 — the same amendment that widened Article 226.
Trap 5 · True or False

A distinguished jurist may be appointed a judge of a High Court.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • That route exists only for the Supreme Court. Article 217(2) requires ten years in judicial office or ten years as a High Court advocate.
Trap 6 · True or False

A judge of a High Court resigns by writing addressed to the Governor.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • To the President, under Art 217(1)(a) — even though the oath under Art 219 is administered by the Governor.
Trap 7 · True or False

Additional judges of a High Court may be appointed for a period not exceeding two years.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 224, to clear arrears, and never beyond age 62.
Trap 8 · True or False

Article 224A empowers the Chief Justice of a High Court to request a retired judge to sit and act, with the previous consent of the President.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Contrast Article 128, where the request for the Supreme Court is made by the CJI.
Trap 9 · True or False

Article 224A deals with the appointment of acting judges of a High Court.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • 224A is the retired judge. Article 224(2) is the acting judge — a qualified person acting while a permanent judge is temporarily absent. Two provisions, routinely merged.
Trap 10 · True or False

Salaries of High Court judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Salaries are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State; pensions on the Consolidated Fund of India. The split is the trap.
Trap 11 · True or False

Article 226 is wider in scope than Article 32.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Because of the words 'and for any other purpose' — it reaches ordinary legal rights, not only Fundamental Rights.
Trap 12 · True or False

The remedy under Article 226 is itself a Fundamental Right.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It is a constitutional right. Only Article 32, sitting in Part III, is itself a Fundamental Right.
Trap 13 · True or False

A High Court may decline relief under Article 226 where an alternative remedy exists.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Art 226 relief is discretionary; Art 32 relief cannot be refused once a Fundamental Right is breached.
Trap 14 · True or False

A High Court may issue a writ to an authority outside its territorial jurisdiction if the cause of action arises wholly or in part within its territory.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 226(2), added by the 15th Amendment, 1963 to cure the narrow reading in Election Commission v. Saka Venkata Rao (1953).
Trap 15 · True or False

A High Court's power of superintendence under Article 227 extends to all courts and tribunals within its jurisdiction without exception.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It excludes any court or tribunal constituted under a law relating to the Armed Forces — the same carve-out that limits Art 136.
Trap 16 · True or False

The Supreme Court has a power of superintendence over all courts in India corresponding to Article 227.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • There is no Part V equivalent of Article 227. The Supreme Court's control runs through appeal and Article 141.
Trap 17 · True or False

A High Court is a court of record and may punish for contempt of itself.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Article 215. It may also punish for contempt of subordinate courts.
Trap 18 · True or False

A High Court has no jurisdiction to declare a law made by Parliament unconstitutional.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • It can. India has an integrated judiciary, and a High Court exercising review under Arts 13 and 226 may strike down a central law.
Trap 19 · True or False

Habeas corpus is the only writ that can be issued against a private individual.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (a) — True
  • Every other writ runs against the State or a body discharging a public duty.
Trap 20 · True or False

The writ of prohibition is issued to a lower court after it has passed its order.

  • (a) True
  • (b) False
Answer: (b) — False
  • Prohibition is preventive and issues while proceedings are pending. The post-order writ is certiorari, which is both preventive and curative.

M36Practice · Week 515 marks · 250 words

"The High Court, not the Supreme Court, is the workhorse of constitutional remedy in India." Examine with reference to Articles 226 and 227.

Directive: "Examine." Test the claim against evidence. You are expected to agree substantially but to identify what limits the claim.
Skeleton
  1. The textual case — Art 226's wider door
  2. The supervisory case — Art 227
  3. The structural case — basic structure and tribunals
  4. What limits the claim
  5. Verdict

Model answer

The claim rests first on text. Article 32 opens the Supreme Court only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Article 226 opens a High Court for Fundamental Rights "and for any other purpose" — that is, for the enforcement of ordinary legal rights as well. On scope, the subordinate forum has the wider door, and the 15th Amendment, 1963 widened it further by allowing a High Court to act wherever the cause of action arises in part within its territory.

The supervisory case

Article 227 has no counterpart in Part V. A High Court superintends every court and tribunal in its territory — judicially and administratively, calling for returns and prescribing forms — subject only to the armed-forces exclusion. Combined with Article 235's control over the district judiciary, this makes the High Court the institution that actually supervises the justice system day to day.

The structural case

L. Chandra Kumar (1997) settled that judicial review under Arts 226 and 227 is itself part of the basic structure, so no tribunal can be insulated from it. That holding governed the striking down of the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 in November 2025.

What limits the claim

Two things. Relief under Art 226 is discretionary and may be refused where an alternative remedy exists, whereas Art 32 relief cannot be denied once a Fundamental Right is breached. And capacity is failing: with roughly 371 of 1,122 sanctioned posts vacant and pendency above 6.3 crore, the workhorse is lame.

The proposition holds in law and is undermined in practice. The constitutional architecture makes the High Court the primary forum; the vacancy record prevents it from behaving like one.

Value additions used: Arts 32, 226, 226(2), 227, 235 · 15th Amendment, 1963 · L. Chandra Kumar (1997) and basic structure · Tribunals Reforms Act struck down, November 2025 · 371 of 1,122 vacancies, 6.3 crore pendency.

Bought: the vacancy and pendency figures, which are the only quantitative evidence available and convert the last section from assertion into finding.
What would cost you marks: asserting that Art 226 is "wider" without the words "and for any other purpose" — the phrase is the argument; and omitting the counter-limit that Art 226 relief is discretionary, which is what turns an "examine" into a one-sided essay.
M37Practice · Week 510 marks · 150 words

Judicial vacancies, not judicial design, are the principal cause of delay in India's High Courts. Comment.

Directive: "Comment." Take a position on a contested causal claim and support it, while conceding what the claim leaves out.
Skeleton
  1. The scale of the problem
  2. The vacancy evidence
  3. Why design is not the culprit
  4. What the claim misses
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Pendency in the High Courts stands at roughly 6.3 crore cases, with the Allahabad High Court alone carrying about 11.9 lakh.

The vacancy evidence

Against a sanctioned strength of about 1,122, roughly 371 posts — close to one in three — lay vacant in early 2026. No institution can clear its docket at two-thirds capacity, and the bottleneck is the appointment process itself: the Memorandum of Procedure the Supreme Court directed be revised in 2015 remains unfinalised, leaving collegium and government recommendations to stall without recorded reasons or timelines.

Why design is not the culprit

The constitutional design is sound. Article 224 permits additional judges for up to two years specifically to clear arrears, and Article 224A permits recalling retired judges. Both are under-used; the tools exist and are left idle.

What the claim misses

Vacancies are necessary but not sufficient as an explanation. Adjournment culture, government litigation — the State is the largest litigant — and the transfer of abolished tribunals' caseloads to already-burdened High Courts after the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 all add load independently.

Filling the bench is therefore the first-order fix, and a time-bound Memorandum of Procedure the reform that makes it possible.

Value additions used: 6.3 crore pendency; 11.9 lakh at Allahabad; 371 of 1,122 vacancies · Arts 224 and 224A as unused capacity · the unfinalised Memorandum of Procedure · tribunal abolition shifting caseload.
Budget check: 176 / 150 words. Cut: a sentence on the National Judicial Data Grid as the source and a second on the 2019 creation of the Andhra Pradesh High Court, neither of which bears on causation. Bought: the Arts 224 / 224A point, which is what converts "there are vacancies" into the sharper claim that the remedies already exist and are unused.
What would cost you marks: quoting pendency without quoting capacity — a backlog figure alone proves nothing without the judge-strength denominator beside it.

Last-mile revision — the High Court

  • 25 High Courts. Oldest three — Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1862), under the Indian High Courts Act, 1861. Newest — Andhra Pradesh, 2019. Delhi is the only UT with its own High Court.
  • Art 214 one per State · Art 231 Parliament by law may create a common High Court · Art 230 extension to UTs.
  • Strength fixed by the President — no number in the Constitution. Contrast: Supreme Court strength fixed by Parliament by law.
  • Art 217 — appointed by the President, consulting CJI + Governor (+ that HC's Chief Justice for a puisne judge). Qualification: 10 yrs judicial office or 10 yrs HC advocate. No distinguished-jurist route. Retire at 62 (15th Amdt, 1963). Resignation to the President.
  • Art 218 applies Art 124(4)–(5) removal · Art 219 oath before the Governor · Art 220 practice restriction · Art 222 transfer + compensatory allowance · Art 223 Acting Chief Justice.
  • Art 224 additional/acting judges, max 2 years, never past 62 · Art 224A retired judge recalled by the HC Chief Justice with the President's prior consent. (Supreme Court equivalent: Art 128, by the CJI.)
  • Art 221 — salaries on the State Consolidated Fund; pensions on the Union's.
  • Art 226 vs Art 32: 226 is wider in scope ("and for any other purpose") but discretionary; 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and cannot be refused. 226(2) cause-of-action reach added by the 15th Amendment, 1963, curing Saka Venkata Rao (1953).
  • Art 227 superintendence over all courts and tribunals — except armed forces; judicial and administrative. No Supreme Court equivalent. Compare the identical carve-out in Art 136.
  • Art 215 court of record + contempt (of itself and of subordinate courts) · Art 228 withdrawal of a constitutional question from a subordinate court · Art 235 control over the district judiciary.
  • Writs: habeas corpus — the only one against private persons · mandamus — public duty; not against President/Governor, not for discretionary duties · prohibition — preventive, judicial bodies only · certiorari — preventive and curative, extended to administrative bodies in 1991 · quo warranto — substantive public office; locus standi relaxed.
  • A death sentence from a sessions court has no effect until confirmed by the High Court — appeal or not.
  • L. Chandra Kumar (1997) — review under Arts 32 and 226/227 is basic structure; tribunals cannot be insulated from High Court review.
  • Capacity: about 1,122 sanctioned, roughly 371 vacant in early 2026; pendency about 6.3–6.4 crore, Allahabad above 11.9 lakh.
