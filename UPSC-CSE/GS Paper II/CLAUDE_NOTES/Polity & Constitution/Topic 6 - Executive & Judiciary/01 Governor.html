Governor

Senior-mentor notes on the State's nominal executive head — appointed, not elected, holding office "during the pleasure of the President," and carrying a discretionary-powers list far longer and far more politically contested than the President's, precisely because a Governor sits between an elected State government and an appointing Union government.

Articles 153–167 Article 200 — three-way veto Discretionary Powers S.R. Bommai (1994) Nabam Rebia (2016)
Art 163Discretion only where the Constitution expressly requires it
◆ Why the Governor gets more exam attention than the office's ceremony suggests

On paper, the Governor is the President's mirror image at State level — nominal head, acts on ministerial advice. In practice, the Governor's office generates far more Supreme Court litigation and far more live current-affairs material (Assembly floor tests, Bill reservations, delayed assent) than the President's ever does, because the Governor is appointed by the Union while the State government is elected locally — a structural tension the President's office simply doesn't have. That tension is exactly why this note spends an entire section on discretionary powers and another on case law — that's where nearly all the real marks are.

Week 3, Day 2 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 30.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Think of the Governor as the Union government's representative sitting inside every State's own government machinery — appointed by the President (in practice, on the Union Cabinet's advice), not elected by anyone in the State, and answerable upward to the President rather than downward to the State's voters. For nearly everything the Governor does, they act exactly like the President does at the Union level: sign what the elected State government (the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers) advises.

The exam-relevant twist is that the list of situations where a Governor is allowed to use personal judgment instead of just following advice is longer and murkier than the President's — and because Governors sit at the fault line between Union and State politics, those discretionary moments (a hung Assembly, a floor test, a Bill sent up to the President instead of being signed locally) show up constantly in the news and just as constantly in MCQs.

01

Appointment & qualifications — Articles 153–157

PointDetail
AppointmentBy the President — by warrant under his hand and seal — effectively on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers. There is no election and no nomination by the State's Council of Ministers — a frequently tested contrast with how a State's own government is formed.
Common Governor for two or more StatesPermitted since the 7th Amendment, 1956 — one person may serve as Governor of more than one State simultaneously.
Qualifications (Art 157)Citizen of India; at least 35 years of age. That's the complete list — unlike the President, there is no requirement that the Governor be qualified for election to any legislative house.
Conventions (not binding law)By convention, the Governor is usually an "outsider" — not a permanent resident of the State they are appointed to — and is appointed after informal consultation with the State's Chief Minister, a practice recommended by the Sarkaria Commission to reduce Centre-State friction. Read the modal verb in any statement about this. The President may consult the Chief Minister; the President is not required to. A statement saying the President "should" or "must" consult the Chief Minister before appointing a Governor is incorrect — the convention has never been made mandatory, which is exactly why Sarkaria, and later Punchhi, had to recommend that it be.
⚠

Trap: "Office of profit" disqualification applies to the Governor too, but sitting Governors, like sitting Presidents, are specifically exempted while they hold office — so a Governor's own post is never treated as disqualifying them from later contesting for President or Vice-President.

📖 Constituent Assembly ammunition — why an appointed Governor at all?

The Assembly seriously considered a directly elected Governor (the Draft Constitution even carried the option) and deliberately rejected it. The arguments that won: an elected Governor with a State-wide mandate would become a rival to the elected Chief Minister, replicating at State level exactly the dual-power problem avoided at the Union; direct election would inflame separatist and provincial tendencies the framers feared in 1949; and an appointed Governor would serve as a constitutional link between Union and State — the agent of national unity inside each State's machinery. Members like K.T. Shah pushed back, warning an appointed Governor would become the Centre's political instrument — a warning the subsequent seven decades of Article 356 misuse and Bill-reservation stand-offs largely vindicated. For any "do we need the office of Governor?" Mains question: open with the framers' unity logic, concede Shah's prophecy with Bommai/Rameshwar Prasad as evidence, close with Punchhi's unimplemented tenure fix as the way forward.

02

Term & removal — Article 156

PointDetail
Term5 years — but this is not a fixed guarantee; the Governor holds office "during the pleasure of the President," meaning the term can end earlier at any time.
RemovalThe President can remove a Governor at any time, without giving any reason, and without any impeachment-style process — the single sharpest contrast with the President's own Article 61 removal, which requires a specific ground and a 2/3rd-majority quasi-judicial process in Parliament. A Governor has essentially no security of tenure against the Union government of the day.
ResignationSubmitted in writing to the President.
ContinuationLike the President, a Governor continues in office beyond the 5-year term until a successor takes charge.
⚠

Punchhi Commission (2010) recommendation, frequently tested as a "has this been implemented" trap: that Governors should have a fixed 5-year tenure and be removable only for proven misconduct after a due process, similar to judges — explicitly to end the practice of Governors being replaced en masse whenever the ruling party changes at the Centre. This recommendation has not been constitutionally implemented; "at the pleasure of the President" (Art 156) remains the operative legal rule.

03

Powers of the Governor

The same six-category checklist as the President (Executive, Legislative, Financial, Judicial — no Diplomatic or Military role at State level) — but each category has State-specific content and at least one important gap versus the President's equivalent power.

CategoryKey content
ExecutiveAppoints the Chief Minister and, on the CM's advice, other State Ministers; appoints the State's Advocate General, State Election Commissioner's staff support, and members of the State Public Service Commission (jointly with UPSC in some matters); can seek information from the CM on State administration (State-level parallel to the President's Article 78).
LegislativeSummons, prorogues the State Legislature, can dissolve the Legislative Assembly; addresses the Legislature; nominates 1/6th of the members of a Legislative Council (where one exists) from persons with special knowledge in literature, science, art, cooperative movement, or social service. The Governor's power to nominate one Anglo-Indian member to the Legislative Assembly was, like the President's equivalent, abolished by the 104th Amendment, 2019.
FinancialMoney Bills can only be introduced in the State Legislature with the Governor's prior recommendation; State Budget laid on the Governor's behalf; controls the State Contingency Fund.
Ordinance-making (Article 213)The Governor's mirror of the President's Article 123 power — promulgated when the State Legislature (or, in a bicameral State, either House) is not in session; same force as a State Act; must be laid before the Legislature and ceases six weeks from reassembly unless approved earlier. The extra condition that distinguishes it from Article 123: for certain matters — where a Bill on the subject would have required the President's prior sanction, or would need reservation for the President's consideration — the Governor needs the President's prior instruction before promulgating. Same case law applies: re-promulgation is unconstitutional (D.C. Wadhwa, 1987; Krishna Kumar Singh, 2017).
JudicialAppoints District Judges and other members of the State's judicial service, in consultation with the State High Court. Pardoning power under Article 161 — covers only offences against State laws, and (Section 04 of the President/VP note) excludes court-martial sentences entirely — those are President-only. On a death sentence the line is finer, and it is exactly what gets tested: the Governor cannot grant a pardon, but can suspend, remit or commute it. Only the President can pardon a death sentence.
04

Discretionary powers — the longest list in this note series

Article 163 says the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers "except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions... in his discretion" — meaning discretion here is textually anchored, unlike the President's mostly unwritten discretion.

🔴 The core discretionary-powers list

●Reserving a Bill passed by the State Legislature for the President's consideration (Art 200) — see the Bill-assent table below.
●Recommending President's Rule in the State under Article 356, based on a report that the State's government cannot be carried on per the Constitution.
●Exercising functions as Administrator of an adjoining Union Territory, if additionally appointed to that role.
●Determining amounts payable by a State to an autonomous Sixth Schedule district as royalty (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram).
●Choosing a Chief Minister when no party has a clear Assembly majority — the direct State-level parallel to the President's PM-selection discretion.
●Dismissing a CM/Council of Ministers that has lost its majority but won't resign, or dissolving the Assembly on the same logic.

Special Article 371-series discretionary responsibilities — a distinct, State-specific list

Governor ofSpecial discretionary responsibility

Nagaland (Art 371A)Special responsibility for law and order relating to Naga insurgency-affected areas, exercised in individual judgment after consulting the Council of Ministers.
Assam (Art 371B), Manipur (Art 371C)Responsibilities connected to tribal-area committees within the State Assembly.
Sikkim (Art 371F)Special responsibility for peace and for the social and economic advancement of different sections of Sikkim's population.

Article 200 — the Governor's three-way choice on a Bill, and why it's more complex than the President's

OptionWhat happens next
(a) AssentBill becomes an Act — closed.
(b) Withhold assentBill fails to become law.
(c) Return the BillOnly for non-Money Bills, for reconsideration by the State Legislature. If passed again with or without amendments, the Governor must give assent — same suspensive-veto logic as the President's Article 111.
(d) Reserve the Bill for the President's considerationThe Governor's extra option the President doesn't have over their own Bills. Once reserved, the President may assent, withhold assent, or (for non-Money Bills) direct the Governor to return it to the State Legislature for reconsideration. Critical asymmetry: even if the State Legislature re-passes a reserved-and-returned Bill, the President is not bound to give assent — unlike the Governor's own suspensive veto, there is no forced second-round assent once a Bill has gone up to the President.
⚠

Trap: the Governor's decision to reserve a Bill for the President is itself listed as a discretionary function under Article 200's proviso — the Governor does not need Council of Ministers' advice to decide whether to reserve a Bill, even though signing an ordinary Bill is otherwise done on advice.

05

Case law — from S.R. Bommai to Nabam Rebia

Three judgments, each closing off a different way the Governor's discretion had been misused in practice — worth learning as one sequence, not three isolated facts.

✚

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) — the single most important Governor/President's-Rule case. A 9-judge bench held: (1) the majority of a Council of Ministers must be tested on the floor of the Assembly, not by the Governor's own subjective assessment; (2) a proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review — courts can strike it down if mala fide or based on irrelevant grounds; (3) if a proclamation is struck down, the Assembly (if dissolved) or the dismissed government can be restored. This case is the standard citation whenever a question probes limits on Article 356.

✚

Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) — the Supreme Court held Bihar Governor Buta Singh's 2005 report recommending Assembly dissolution (to pre-empt a possible majority-claim by a coalition) was unconstitutional and mala fide — but declined to restore the dissolved Assembly since fresh elections had already been notified by the time of judgment. A key lesson examiners like to draw out: judicial review can declare an action unconstitutional without necessarily being able to undo its practical consequences.

✚

Nabam Rebia and Bamang Felix v. Deputy Speaker (2016) — arising from the Arunachal Pradesh political crisis, the Supreme Court held that a Governor's power to summon, prorogue, or advance an Assembly session is ordinarily exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, not the Governor's own discretion — a Governor cannot use internal dissidence within the ruling party as a ground to unilaterally advance a session or alter its agenda. This case sharply narrowed how "discretionary" the summoning power really is in day-to-day practice.

The 2025 assent-timeline saga — the most current-affairs-live Governor topic there is

⚠

State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (April 2025) — Article 200 sets no time limit for a Governor to act on a Bill, and Tamil Nadu's Governor had sat on multiple Bills for years. A two-judge bench held that "as soon as possible" in Article 200 has real content: acting on ministerial advice, the Governor should act within about 1 month; withholding assent contrary to advice requires the Bill's return, and reserving it for the President should happen within about 3 months; a Bill re-passed by the Assembly should receive assent within about 1 month. Using Article 142, the Court even deemed the long-pending Tamil Nadu Bills to have received assent.

The counter-move: the President then invoked Article 143 (advisory jurisdiction) and referred a set of questions to the Supreme Court — including whether courts can prescribe timelines the Constitution's text omits, and whether a two-judge bench could effectively interpret constitutional provisions at all, given Article 145(3) requires a minimum five-judge Constitution Bench for substantial questions of constitutional interpretation. In November 2025, the five-judge bench answering the reference substantially pulled the timelines back — holding that fixed judicial deadlines and "deemed assent" cannot be imposed on the Governor or President, while still affirming that indefinite inaction is not constitutionally open and remains subject to judicial review in egregious cases.

Why this matters for the test: this is exactly the "recent judgment layer" a current-affairs-aware question setter reaches for. Testable anchors: the two-judge vs five-judge (Art 145(3)) tension; Article 143 being used to revisit a judgment's logic; Article 142 as the deemed-assent device; and the final position — no fixed timelines, no deemed assent, but no indefinite sitting on Bills either.

📖 The story, told once properly — then the flowchart to remember it by

Tamil Nadu's Assembly passes Bills — mostly about state universities — and sends them to the Governor. The Governor simply does not act. Not a rejection, not a return, just silence, for years, because Article 200's phrase "as soon as possible" has no number attached. The State finally sues. In April 2025, two judges of the Supreme Court decide that "as soon as possible" cannot mean "never": they read in working timelines (about a month to act on advice, about three to reserve against advice) and then do something extraordinary — using Article 142's complete-justice power, they declare the ten stuck Bills to have been deemed assented, as though the Governor had signed. For the first time in the Republic's history, laws came into force that no Governor and no President ever signed.

The Union blinks. The President invokes Article 143 — the advisory jurisdiction — and asks the Court itself: can judges impose deadlines the Constitution's text deliberately omitted? And could two judges even do this, when Article 145(3) demands five for substantial constitutional interpretation? In November 2025, a five-judge bench answers: the timelines and deemed assent go — courts cannot rewrite Article 200's silence into a schedule — but the Governor wins no licence to sit forever either: prolonged, unexplained inaction remains challengeable case by case. The saga ends where good constitutional stories usually do — both sides restrained, the text unchanged, and the conventions around it permanently sharpened.

PYQ analysis & practice questions

(A) Genuine UPSC PYQs

Attempt each before reading the verdict. Cross-check years against official UPSC papers as a habit.

Practice · Statements (Week 3 test — you answered "2 only")

Consider the following statements:

  1. The President should consult the Chief Minister of the State while appointing the Governor.
  2. The Election Commission can take a suo motu decision to disqualify a Member of Parliament.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (d) — neither
  • Statement 1 fails on the modal verb. The President may consult the Chief Minister; there is no requirement to. The consultation is a convention the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions recommended be made binding — which it never was. "Should" converts a courtesy into an obligation, and that is the trick.
  • Statement 2 inverts the machinery. On Article 102 grounds the President decides; the Election Commission only tenders an opinion, which binds the President. The EC has no power to disqualify an MP on its own motion.
  • Two different tricks in one question — a modal verb in the first, a reversed actor in the second. Both are standard.
Practice · Statements

With reference to the Governor's power under Article 161, consider the following statements:

  1. The Governor can commute a death sentence awarded for an offence against a State law.
  2. The Governor can grant a pardon in a case where a death sentence has been awarded.
  3. The Governor can remit a sentence awarded by a court martial held in the State.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (a) — only one
  • Statement 1 is correct. Suspension, remission and commutation of a death sentence are all within the Governor's Article 161 power. This is the half of the rule most candidates get wrong.
  • Statement 2 is wrong. A pardon in a death-sentence case is exclusively the President's, under Article 72.
  • Statement 3 is wrong. Court-martial sentences are outside Article 161 altogether — the Governor has no power over them of any kind.
  • The pattern to hold: on a death sentence the Governor loses only the pardon; on a court martial the Governor loses everything.

2018Prelims

Consider the following statements:

  1. No criminal proceedings shall be instituted against the Governor of a State in any court during his term of office.
  2. The emoluments and allowances of the Governor of a State shall not be diminished during his term of office.

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (c)
  • Both are correct, under Article 361. The Governor enjoys personal immunity from criminal proceedings during the term of office (and from civil proceedings relating to personal acts unless 2 months' notice is given), and emoluments cannot be reduced mid-term.

M232019 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

Whether the Supreme Court Judgement (July 2018) can settle the political tussle between the Lt. Governor and the elected government of Delhi? Examine.

Directive: "Whether… Examine." A yes/no framed question demanding dissection — but the honest answer is qualified. Examine the judgment's holdings, then test its actual settling power against what followed. Events after 2018 are legitimate and powerful evidence.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 239AA + the dispute
  2. What the 2018 judgment held
  3. Why it did not settle matters
  4. What would actually settle it
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Delhi's peculiar status flows from Article 239AA: the NCT has a legislative assembly but not land, police or public order; the Lieutenant Governor acts on the Council of Ministers' aid and advice, but may refer a "difference of opinion" to the President.

What the 2018 Constitution Bench held

The LG is not an independent decision-maker and has no general discretionary power; he is bound by the elected government's advice except on the three reserved subjects. The difference-of-opinion reference must be exceptional, not routine, and must rest on cogent reasons. The Court anchored this in constitutional morality, representative democracy and collective responsibility, expressly rejecting any requirement of the LG's concurrence for every executive act.

Why it did not settle the tussle

The structural ambiguity survived the judgment. It laid down principles but left subject-allocation unresolved: a two-judge bench split in 2019 over "services", requiring a further reference; a Constitution Bench in May 2023 then held that services outside land, police and public order fall to the elected government; and within months Parliament enacted the GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023, creating an authority with the LG's primacy over services — reversing the judicial outcome by legislation. That challenge is pending.

Verdict

The judgment settled the constitutional principle but not the political dispute. Where Parliament can alter the statutory basis of an arrangement, adjudication alone cannot end the contest. Durable settlement requires either full statehood, or an explicit constitutional demarcation of Delhi's subjects with a defined, time-bound procedure for the LG's reference — the same institutional lesson the 2025 Governor-assent litigation delivered for the States.

M24Practice15 marks · 250 words

"Judicial review has narrowed the Governor's discretion more than any constitutional amendment has." Examine with reference to S.R. Bommai (1994) and Nabam Rebia (2016).

Directive: "Examine" a comparative claim. The comparison is the point: judiciary versus amendment. So the answer must show what courts did AND what amendments did not do — otherwise the comparison is never actually made.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 163's textual discretion
  2. What judicial review narrowed
  3. What amendments did not do
  4. The limits of judicial narrowing
  5. Verdict

Model answer

Article 163 requires the Governor to act on ministerial advice "except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions in his discretion" — deliberately open-ended language that the text has never since narrowed.

What judicial review narrowed

S.R. Bommai (1994), nine judges, transformed Article 356: majority must be tested on the floor of the House, not by the Governor's subjective assessment; a proclamation is justiciable and may be struck down for mala fides or irrelevant grounds; and a dissolved Assembly can be revived. Rameshwar Prasad (2006) applied this, holding the Bihar dissolution on the Governor's report unconstitutional. Nabam Rebia (2016) narrowed a different power — summoning, prorogation and advancing a session are ordinarily exercised on ministerial advice, and intra-party dissidence is no ground for unilateral action. The 2025 assent litigation kept indefinite inaction on Bills judicially examinable, even after the Constitution Bench declined to fix timelines.

What amendments did not do

Article 163 stands unamended. Article 156 still holds the Governor in office "during the pleasure of the President" — removable at will, without cause or process. The Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions recommended fixed tenure, removal only for proven misconduct, and consultation with the Chief Minister before appointment. None has been enacted.

The limits of judicial narrowing

Courts act only after the event, and remedies are often hollow — in Rameshwar Prasad the dissolution was declared mala fide, yet the Assembly was not revived, elections having been notified.

The claim therefore holds: every meaningful restraint on the Governor has come from the bench, not from Parliament. But judicial narrowing is remedial and episodic; a structural fix still requires the constitutional amendment that has never come.

Last-mile revision — Governor

  • Appointed by President, on Union Cabinet's advice — no election, no State CoM role in the appointment itself.
  • Qualifications: citizen, 35+. No legislative-membership qualification, unlike the President.
  • One person can be Governor of 2+ States (7th Amendment, 1956).
  • Term 5 years, but holds office "during the pleasure of the President" — removable anytime, no grounds needed, no impeachment-style process.
  • Punchhi Commission recommended fixed tenure/misconduct-only removal — not implemented.
  • Article 161: State-law offences only; no power at all over court-martial sentences. On a death sentence the Governor cannot pardon but can suspend, remit or commute — only the President can pardon one.
  • Article 200: assent / withhold / return (non-Money Bills, forces re-assent if re-passed) / reserve for President (Governor's extra option).
  • Reserved-Bill asymmetry: President is never bound to assent even if State Legislature re-passes it — unlike the Governor's own forced second-round assent.
  • Discretionary list: reserve a Bill, recommend Art 356, UT administrator role, Sixth Schedule royalty amounts, choosing/dismissing a CM in a hung/no-confidence scenario.
  • Special Art 371-series discretion: Nagaland (371A), Sikkim (371F), Arunachal (371H), Karnataka (371J) — law/order or regional-development responsibilities.
  • Bommai (1994): floor test required, Art 356 is judicially reviewable. Rameshwar Prasad (2006): mala fide dissolution struck down but not reversible in practice. Nabam Rebia (2016): summoning/proroguing Assembly is ordinarily on ministerial advice, not Governor's own call.
  • 2025 assent saga: Apr 2025 two-judge TN judgment set ~1/3-month timelines + Art 142 deemed assent → President's Art 143 reference → Nov 2025 five-judge bench: no fixed timelines, no deemed assent, but indefinite inaction still reviewable.
