President & Vice-President

Senior-mentor notes on India's nominal executive head — election by a specially-weighted Electoral College, the near-total shift from personal discretion to Cabinet-bound formality across the 42nd and 44th Amendments, and the Vice-President's much simpler, Parliament-only counterpart role.

This is the first Topic 6 note, and the first note built after two weekly tests' worth of hard evidence about how this coaching test actually examines Polity: not by inventing exotic questions, but by testing the same handful of things — precise numbers (35 years, not 25 or 30), the exact difference between two similar-looking powers (President's pardon vs Governor's pardon), and named case law behind a doctrine everyone half-remembers. This is Week 3, Day 1 of the study plan — the first day of the Union & State Executive block, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and M. Laxmikanth Chapters 18–19.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

India has a President the way Britain has a King — a Head of State who signs everything, but almost never decides anything on their own. The actual decisions are made by the Prime Minister and Cabinet; the President's constitutional job, in the overwhelming majority of cases, is to act on their advice, not substitute their own judgment. This is the "nominal vs real executive" idea from the Parliamentary System note (Topic 5, Note 00) applied to a real office.

Two things make the President's office worth a full note rather than one paragraph: first, the election method is unusual and heavily tested — the President is not directly elected by citizens, but by an "Electoral College" of elected lawmakers, weighted by a formula that took decades to fully appreciate. Second, even a mostly-ceremonial office has a handful of genuinely discretionary moments — picking a Prime Minister when no party has a clear majority is the big one — and knowing exactly where the ceremonial default ends and real judgment begins is what most President-related MCQs are actually testing.

01

Election & qualifications — Articles 54–58

The President is elected indirectly, by an Electoral College, using a proportional-representation system with a single transferable vote and secret ballot — three technical terms that are each independently testable.

In the Electoral College (Art 54)Excluded — the classic trap list
Elected members of both Houses of ParliamentNominated members of either House — up to 12 in the Rajya Sabha, and the erstwhile Anglo-Indian nominees in the Lok Sabha. They do not vote.
Elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of all StatesNominated members of any State Legislative Assembly.
Elected members of the Assemblies of Delhi and Puducherry (added by the 70th Amendment, 1992)Members of any Legislative Council (MLCs). Only Assembly — lower-house — members count, in both Parliament and the States.
!
The pattern in every row is the same word: elected. Nominated members are out at the Union level, out at the State level, and the entire upper house of a State is out. If a question puts "nominated" or "Council" anywhere in a statement about the Presidential electoral college, that statement is wrong.
⚠

Trap: "Elected members of Union Territories" is not a blanket rule — only Delhi (NCT) and Puducherry are included, and only because each has its own Legislative Assembly; UTs without an Assembly (Chandigarh, Lakshadweep, etc.) have no vote in a Presidential election at all.

The vote-value formula — the single most numbers-heavy fact in this note

Not every legislator's vote carries equal weight; the formula deliberately balances the value of MLA votes against MP votes so that no single large State can dominate, and Parliament as a whole roughly balances all the States put together.

VoterVote value formula
Each MLA(State's total population ÷ Total number of elected MLAs in that State) ÷ 1,000
Each MP (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha)(Total value of all MLA votes across all States) ÷ Total number of elected MPs

Population used: since the 84th Amendment (2001) and extended by the 2026-relevant freeze, the 1971 Census population figures continue to be used for this calculation (not the latest census) — a deliberate freeze meant to avoid penalising States that controlled population growth.

✚

Qualifications (Art 58): citizen of India; at least 35 years of age; qualified for election as a member of the Lok Sabha; must not hold any office of profit under the Union, a State, or any local/other authority. Exception: the offices of President, Vice-President, Governor, and Union/State Minister are specifically excluded from "office of profit" for this purpose — so a sitting Vice-President or Governor can validly contest for President.

📖 Constituent Assembly ammunition — why indirect election, not direct?

A Mains question on the President's election method is really asking whether you know the framers' own reasoning, debated at length in the Assembly. Three arguments carried the day: (1) in a parliamentary system where real power sits with the Cabinet, directly electing a largely ceremonial head would be an enormous expenditure of national effort for a symbolic office; (2) a directly-elected President with a nationwide personal mandate would inevitably become a rival power centre to the Prime Minister — destabilising the very parliamentary accountability the system is built on; (3) Ambedkar's framing — the President "represents the nation but does not rule the nation"; a symbol requires legitimacy, not a plebiscite. The weighted Electoral College was the middle path: democratic legitimacy through elected representatives of both Union and States, without a competing popular mandate. Quote any one of these three and an "evaluate the election method" answer immediately rises above the textbook floor.

Term, vacancy & impeachment — Articles 56, 61–62

PointDetail
Term5 years from the date of entering office; eligible for re-election any number of times (no term limit, unlike the US President's two-term cap).
ResignationSubmitted in writing to the Vice-President (not the Chief Justice, a common trap option).
Continuation in officeEven after the 5-year term expires, the President continues in office until a successor enters office — India is never without a President.
EmolumentsFixed by Parliament; charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (hence discussed but not voted — see Topic 5, Note 03) and cannot be diminished during the term of office, except during a Financial Emergency under Article 360.
Election disputesAll doubts and disputes arising out of a Presidential or Vice-Presidential election are decided by the Supreme Court alone, and its decision is final. Note the trap: an election cannot be challenged on the ground that the Electoral College was incomplete (e.g., some Assembly seats vacant), and acts done by the President before the election is set aside remain valid.

Vacancy — who officiates, and for how long

SituationWho acts
President's office falls vacant (death, resignation, removal) before term endsThe Vice-President acts as President — for a maximum of 6 months, within which a fresh Presidential election must be held.
Both President's and Vice-President's offices are vacant simultaneouslyThe Chief Justice of India (or, if unavailable, the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court) acts as President.
President temporarily unable to discharge functions (illness, foreign travel)Vice-President discharges functions until the President resumes — this is not a vacancy, just a temporary stand-in.
⚠

Impeachment (Article 61) — the only removal process, and it has never once been used. The sole ground is "violation of the Constitution" — deliberately undefined, unlike the specific, listed grounds for judge removal. Procedure: the charge can be initiated in either House of Parliament; it must be signed by at least 1/4th of that House's total membership and 14 days' advance notice given; the resolution to impeach needs a 2/3rd majority of the total membership of that House to pass (not just those present and voting — a frequent trap). Once the first House passes it, the other House investigates the charge — the President has the right to appear and be represented — and must itself pass the charge by a 2/3rd majority of total membership for removal to take effect. This is a quasi-judicial process, unusual in that a legislative body, not a court, both investigates and decides.

Ordinance-making power — Article 123, the President's most significant legislative power

✚

When both Houses are not in session (or either one isn't) and the President is satisfied that circumstances require immediate action, an ordinance may be promulgated. Key anchors: an ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, but is temporary — it must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble and ceases to operate six weeks from reassembly (or earlier if disapproved by resolutions of both Houses). Effectively it can survive a maximum of about six months and six weeks without becoming law. The power is co-extensive with Parliament's legislative power — so an ordinance cannot cover what Parliament itself cannot legislate on, and cannot amend the Constitution. It is exercised on the Council of Ministers' advice, not personal discretion. The Governor has the identical power under Article 213, with one addition: for certain subjects the Governor needs the President's prior instruction before promulgating.

Case law that makes this a live topic: D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) — Bihar had re-promulgated ordinances for years without ever passing them as laws; the Court condemned this as a "fraud on the Constitution" and a subversion of the legislative process. Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017, seven judges) went further: re-promulgation of ordinances is unconstitutional, and the mandatory laying of an ordinance before the legislature is not a formality but a constitutional obligation.

⚠

Trap: "the President can issue an ordinance at any time" is false — both Houses (or at least one) must not be in session. And "an ordinance is permanent until repealed" is false — the six-week clock from reassembly is the whole point.

⚠

The three highest-value traps here: (1) National Emergency needs a special majority, President's Rule and Financial Emergency need only a simple majority — the most swapped attribute; (2) Financial Emergency, once approved, runs indefinitely without renewal, unlike the other two's 6-month cycles; (3) Article 352 approval window is 1 month, the other two are 2 months. And the standing fact: Financial Emergency has never been proclaimed.

✚

Comparative layer — impeachment in India vs the USA (a Week 2 question tested exactly this). In the USA, the President, Vice-President, and all civil officers of the federal government (including judges) are impeachable — the House impeaches by simple majority, the Senate tries and convicts by two-thirds of members present; grounds are "treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors." Three US Presidents have been impeached by the House — Andrew Johnson (1868), Bill Clinton (1998), Donald Trump (2019 and again 2021) — and none was convicted and removed. In India, Article 61 impeachment covers the President alone (judges are removed by a different address procedure, the VP by a simpler resolution); the sole ground is "violation of the Constitution"; both Houses need two-thirds of total membership; and the process has never been initiated. The tested contrast: India's design is narrower in who it covers, vaguer in its ground, and stricter in its majority.

Powers of the President

Six power categories are worth holding as a fixed checklist — Executive, Legislative, Financial, Judicial, Diplomatic, Military, and Emergency. Prelims tests the specific content of each category; Mains tests how far real discretion survives inside a formally "on advice" system.

CategoryKey content
ExecutiveAppoints PM (and other Ministers on PM's advice), Governors, CJI and other SC/HC judges, Attorney General, CAG, Election Commissioners, UPSC Chairman/members, Finance Commission — all formally by the President, substantively on someone else's binding advice or recommendation. Can require the PM to furnish information on Union administration/legislation proposals (Article 78 — the President's one direct information-channel to the Council of Ministers).
LegislativeSummons and prorogues Parliament sessions; can dissolve the Lok Sabha; addresses both Houses (especially the first session after each general election, and the first session of each year); nominates 12 members to Rajya Sabha with special knowledge/practical experience in literature, science, art, or social service. Note: the President's power to nominate one Anglo-Indian member to the Lok Sabha was abolished by the 104th Amendment, 2019 — this representation itself lapsed and is a live trap in "which nomination powers does the President still have" MCQs.
FinancialMoney Bills can only be introduced in Lok Sabha with the President's prior recommendation; causes the Union Budget to be laid before Parliament; controls the Contingency Fund of India; constitutes the Finance Commission every 5 years.
JudicialPardoning power under Article 72 — see Section 04 for the full detail and how it differs from the Governor's Article 161 power.
Diplomatic & MilitaryAll international treaties/agreements are negotiated and concluded in the President's name (subject to Parliamentary ratification where required); Supreme Commander of the Defence Forces. Note the limit: the President cannot declare war or conclude peace independently — only with Parliament's authorisation.
EmergencyCan proclaim National Emergency (Art 352), President's Rule in a State (Art 356), and Financial Emergency (Art 360) — the full comparison table sits immediately below, since "Emergency Powers" is a listed Week 3 topic for both the President and the Governor.
04

Discretion, veto power & case law — where the real marks are

Where genuine discretion still survives — unwritten, but real

🔴 Situational discretion — not in the Constitution's text, but consistently exercised

●Choosing a Prime Minister when no single party/coalition has a clear Lok Sabha majority (a "hung Parliament").
●Dismissing a Council of Ministers that has lost its majority but refuses to resign or advise dissolution.
●Dissolving the Lok Sabha if the Council of Ministers has lost confidence and no alternative government can be formed.
●Deciding whether to return Cabinet advice once under the 44th Amendment's safety valve (a genuine judgment call on timing and substance).

Veto power over ordinary Bills — three distinct options, one trap

Veto typeWhat it means
Absolute vetoPresident withholds assent outright; the Bill dies. Rarely used post-1950 — the most-cited historical instance is a Bill from before real party-system maturity.
Suspensive vetoPresident returns a Bill (other than a Money Bill) for reconsideration once; if Parliament passes it again, with or without amendments, the President must give assent — cannot return it a second time.
Pocket vetoPresident simply does not act — neither assents, rejects, nor returns — and the Constitution sets no time limit for the President to decide. Classic example: President Zail Singh sat on the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986, and it lapsed only because his term ended before he acted.
⚠

Trap: Money Bills have no suspensive veto option. The President can only assent to or withhold assent from a Money Bill — never return it for reconsideration, since it was introduced only on the President's own prior recommendation in the first place.

Pardoning power — Article 72 — and exactly how it differs from the Governor's Article 161

PointPresident (Art 72)Governor (Art 161)
Death sentenceCan pardon a death sentence — and equally suspend, remit or commute it.Cannot pardon it — that is exclusively the President's. But the Governor can suspend, remit or commute a death sentence. The two powers are concurrent except for pardon, and this half-distinction is the trap: a statement saying the Governor "has no power over a death sentence" is wrong.
Court-martial (military) sentencesCan pardon, suspend, remit, or commute sentences by a court martial.Cannot — Governor has no power over court-martial punishments at all.
Offences against Union lawCovers offences against any law relating to a matter within the Union's executive power.Covers offences against State laws only.

Five distinct forms the pardoning power can take, worth naming precisely rather than lumping together as "pardon": pardon (removes both sentence and conviction, wiping out the offence entirely), commutation (substitutes a lighter form of punishment), remission (reduces the period of sentence without changing its nature), respite (awards a lesser sentence on special grounds, e.g. pregnancy), reprieve (temporarily stays execution of a sentence, usually to allow time to seek a pardon or commutation).

✚

Case law worth naming: in Kehar Singh v. Union of India (1989), the Supreme Court held the Article 72 pardoning power is an executive act, not subject to judicial review on the merits of how the President arrived at the decision — but in Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006), the Court clarified that the exercise of this power can still be judicially reviewed if it is shown to be arbitrary, mala fide, or based on wholly irrelevant considerations. The two cases together are the standard "is pardon really unreviewable" pairing examiners like to test.

✚

Article 74(2) — the advice is beyond the courts. A companion rule that completes the picture: the question of what advice the Council of Ministers tendered to the President shall not be inquired into by any court. So courts may review the President's action (a proclamation, an appointment) but cannot compel disclosure of the ministerial advice behind it. Trap: this does not immunise the action itself from judicial review — Bommai reviewed an Article 356 proclamation without ever opening the advice file.

The concept behind "on advice" — Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974): the seven-judge foundation for everything this note says about the President's (and Governor's) role. The Court held that the President and Governors are constitutional or formal heads who exercise their powers on the aid and advice of their Council of Ministers "save in a few well-known exceptional situations" — cementing that India's Republic has a parliamentary, not presidential, executive even though the text formally vests executive power in the President (Art 53). Name this case whenever an answer needs the doctrinal source of the nominal-executive principle, rather than just asserting it.

⚠

Recent-judgment layer — Article 201 and the 2025 assent-timeline saga: the same 2025 litigation covered in the Governor note (Topic 6, Note 01, Section 05) reached the President too. Once a Governor reserves a Bill, Article 201 sets no time limit for the President's decision — the April 2025 two-judge judgment read in an indicative ~3-month window, but the November 2025 five-judge bench answering the President's Article 143 reference held that courts cannot impose fixed timelines or "deemed assent" on the President either, while indefinite inaction remains judicially examinable in egregious cases. If a statement claims a court-mandated deadline currently binds the President under Article 201 — that statement is wrong as of the Constitution Bench opinion.

05

Vice-President — Articles 63–71

The Vice-President's office is deliberately simpler than the President's — a much smaller electoral college, no impeachment-style removal, and one clean day-job: chairing the Rajya Sabha. But "simpler" is not "different in every respect", and that is precisely where questions are set: the voting method, the term, and the disputes procedure are word-for-word identical to the President's, while the electoral college, the oath, the removal route and the vacancy rule differ. The second table below sets the two side by side, stage by stage.

PointDetail
Primary roleEx-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha — this is the Vice-President's main constitutional function, distinct from the President's role entirely.
Electoral collegeMembers of both Houses of Parliament only — MLAs are not included, the single biggest difference from the President's much larger electoral college. Nominated MPs of both Houses do vote here (unlike the Presidential election, where nominated members are excluded) — a frequently confused pairing.
QualificationsCitizen of India; at least 35 years; qualified for election as a Rajya Sabha member (not Lok Sabha, unlike the President); must not hold any office of profit.
Term5 years; eligible for re-election any number of times.
Removal (Art 67(b))By a resolution of the Rajya Sabha passed by an effective majority (majority of all then-members, not total membership) and agreed to by the Lok Sabha by a simple majority. 14 days' notice of the intention to move the resolution is required — the same notice period as the President's impeachment. What is absent is any specified ground: Article 61 confines the President's impeachment to "violation of the Constitution", while Article 67(b) names no ground at all. The resolution can also originate only in the Rajya Sabha.
Acting as President (Art 65)Officiates when the President's office is vacant, for up to 6 months. While so acting, the Vice-President does not perform Rajya Sabha Chairman duties — those pass to the Deputy Chairman — and draws the President's emoluments, not the Chairman's.

(4) Mid-term vacancy — the six-month deadline binds the President's election only; Article 68 says merely "as soon as possible" for the Vice-President.
✚

India's VP vs America's VP — the comparison Laxmikanth draws, and a ready Prelims pairing. The office was modelled on the American Vice-President, and the similarity is real: both preside over the Upper House (Rajya Sabha / Senate) and both step in when the top office falls vacant. But two differences matter: (1) the American VP, on the President's death/resignation/removal, becomes President for the remainder of the full term — India's Vice-President only acts as President for a maximum of 6 months, within which a fresh election must be held; (2) the American VP is the President of the Senate and a member of it in a sense that allows a casting vote in case of a tie, whereas India's Vice-President chairs the Rajya Sabha without being a member of it. Statement questions swap exactly these two.

2013Prelims

Consider the following statements:

  1. The Chairman and the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha are not members of that House.
  2. While the nominated members of the two Houses of the Parliament have no voting right in the presidential election, they have the right to vote in the election of the Vice-President.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither 1 nor 2
Answer: (b)
  • The Chairman (the Vice-President) is not a member — but the Deputy Chairman is an elected member of the House, which makes statement 1 false.

2020Prelims

Consider the following statements:

  1. The President of India can summon a session of the Parliament at such place as he/she thinks fit.
  2. The Constitution of India provides for three sessions of the Parliament in a year, but it is not mandatory to conduct all three sessions.
  3. There is no minimum number of days that the Parliament is required to meet in a year.

  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 2 and 3 only

Practice · Statements

Which of the following can be pardoned by the President of India but not by a State Governor?

  1. A death sentence
  2. A sentence awarded by a court martial
  3. An offence against a State law

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 1 and 2 only
  • (c) 2 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (b)

The verb "pardoned" decides this question. Offences against State law are squarely within Article 161. A death sentence cannot be pardoned by the Governor — though it can be suspended, remitted or commuted by them — and court-martial sentences are outside Article 161 altogether (Section 04's table).

  2. It requires 14 days' notice signed by one-fourth of the House's total membership
  3. The same House that frames the charge also investigates it

Which is/are correct?

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 1 and 3 only
  • (c) 2 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (a) — 1 and 2 only

The OTHER House investigates the charge, with the President entitled to appear (Section 02).

M212018 · GS-II15 marks · 250 words

Under what circumstances can the Financial Emergency be proclaimed by the President of India? What consequences follow when such a declaration remains in force?

Directive: two factual halves — "under what circumstances" and "what consequences." No debate is demanded, so this is an elucidate-type answer where precision and completeness score. Split physically into the two halves; a brief evaluative close adds value without being required.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: Art 360 + never used
  2. Circumstances + procedure
  3. Consequences — Union, State, salaries
  4. Safeguards + brief assessment

Model answer

Article 360 empowers the President to proclaim a Financial Emergency if satisfied that a situation has arisen threatening the financial stability or credit of India or of any part of its territory. It has never been proclaimed — not even during the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis.

Circumstances and procedure

The threshold is the President's satisfaction as to that threat. The proclamation needs approval by both Houses within two months, by simple majority — unlike a National Emergency, which needs a special majority within one month. Once approved it continues indefinitely, with no periodic renewal, until revoked by the President; revocation needs no parliamentary approval. The 38th Amendment (1975) had made emergency proclamations non-justiciable; the 44th Amendment (1978) removed that bar, so the satisfaction is now reviewable for mala fides.

Consequences while in force

Union control over State finances: States may be directed to observe specified canons of financial propriety. Salary reductions: the President may direct reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving the Union or a State — expressly including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, one of the very few situations in which judicial salaries may be reduced. Money Bills reserved: all Money Bills and other financial Bills passed by State legislatures may be required to be reserved for the President's consideration. The federal financial structure thus becomes temporarily unitary — though, unlike Article 352, Fundamental Rights are not suspended.

Assessment

Its disuse is itself constitutionally significant: severe fiscal stress has been managed through ordinary instruments — FRBM discipline, borrowing limits under Article 293, Finance Commission transfers — so a provision this centralising has become a last resort by convention, even though its text remains fully available.

Value additions used: Art 360 ground · two-month / simple-majority contrast with Art 352 · indefinite continuance · the 38th (1975) → 44th (1978) arc on justiciability · judges' salaries · State Money Bills reserved · FRs not suspended · never used, including 1991 · Art 293 / FRBM.

Bought: the 38th Amendment (1975), which is what the 44th was reversing. Naming both ends of the arc turns "subject to judicial review" from an assertion into a datable constitutional fact.

M22Practice15 marks · 250 words

"The 44th Amendment did not restore the President's discretion — it merely gave the office one procedural pause button." Critically examine with reference to Articles 74 and 78.

Directive: "Critically examine" a sharp claim. Deconstruct the claim, test it both ways, then judge. The claim is largely right but overstated — the strongest answers show where the President does retain real discretion, which the quotation ignores.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: the 42nd→44th arc
  2. Why the claim is right
  3. Where it understates — real discretion
  4. Art 78 as a live instrument
  5. Balanced verdict

Model answer

Before 1976, Article 74 said the Council of Ministers shall "aid and advise" the President without expressly binding him. The 42nd Amendment (1976) made that advice binding. The 44th Amendment (1978) added one proviso: the President may require reconsideration, but shall act in accordance with the advice tendered after it.

Why the claim is largely correct

The proviso confers delay, not choice. Reconsideration may be sought once; the reconsidered advice binds whatever it says. It creates no power to refuse, and Article 74(2) bars courts from inquiring into the advice tendered. Shamsher Singh (1974) had already settled that the President is a constitutional head acting on advice "save in a few well-known exceptional situations."

Where the claim understates the office

Real discretion survives outside Article 74: appointing a Prime Minister in a hung Parliament; dismissing a Council that has lost its majority but will not resign; deciding dissolution when no alternative government is available. Article 111 sets no time limit for assent, making the pocket veto possible — as President Zail Singh showed with the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986. The November 2025 Constitution Bench held that courts cannot fix assent timelines or deem assent, while keeping indefinite inaction reviewable.

Article 78 as a live instrument

Article 78 obliges the Prime Minister to communicate all Cabinet decisions, furnish information sought, and place before the Council any matter a Minister has decided alone — making the President an informed interlocutor, not a signatory.

Verdict

Accurate as to Article 74, incomplete as to the office. The 44th restored no discretion over policy; the President's real authority lies in situational discretion and in the moral force of Article 78 — influence rather than power.

Value additions used: Arts 74 / 74(2) / 78 / 111 · the 42nd → 44th arc · Shamsher Singh (1974) quotation · the situational-discretion list · Zail Singh's pocket veto, 1986 · November 2025 Constitution Bench.

Last-mile revision — President & Vice-President

  • Electoral College for President: elected MPs + elected MLAs of States + Delhi & Puducherry. No MLCs, no nominated members.
  • Vote value: MLA = (State pop ÷ elected MLAs) ÷ 1000; MP = (total MLA vote value) ÷ elected MPs. 1971 Census population still used.
  • Age 35+ for both President and VP; President must be LS-qualified, VP must be RS-qualified.
  • Term 5 years both; resignation letter goes to VP (President) / President (VP).
  • Impeachment (President, Art 61): only ground "violation of Constitution"; 2/3rd of total membership in both Houses; never used.
  • VP removal (Art 67(b)): RS effective majority + LS simple majority, 14 days' notice required — what is missing is a stated ground, not the notice. RS alone may initiate.
  • VP election: 20 proposers + 20 seconders (President 50 + 50). Oath by the President (Art 69); President's oath by the CJI (Art 60).
  • 11th Amendment (1961): VP electoral college no longer meets in joint sitting; election unchallengeable for any vacancy in the college.
  • Mid-term vacancy: President within 6 months (Art 62); VP "as soon as possible", no outer limit (Art 68). Both successors get a full 5-year term.
  • VP draws no salary as VP — paid as Rajya Sabha Chairman; President's emoluments only while acting as President.
  • Art 71: election disputes go only to the Supreme Court; acts done before an election is voided stay valid. Same for both offices.
  • Vacancy chain: VP officiates for President (≤6 months); CJI officiates if both vacant.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976): Cabinet advice binding. 44th Amendment (1978): President can return advice once, then must comply.
  • President: absolute / suspensive / pocket veto. No suspensive veto for Money Bills.
  • Death sentence: only the President can pardon one; the Governor can still suspend, remit or commute it. Court-martial sentences are President-only outright.
  • Kehar Singh (1989): pardon is executive, not usually reviewable. Epuru Sudhakar (2006): reviewable if arbitrary/mala fide.
  • VP's day job: ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha — stops when acting as President.
