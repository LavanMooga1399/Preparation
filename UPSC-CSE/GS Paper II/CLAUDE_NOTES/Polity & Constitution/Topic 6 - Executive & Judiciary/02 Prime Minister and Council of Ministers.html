Prime Minister & Council of Ministers

Senior-mentor notes on the "real" executive — the office that actually governs while the President signs. Appointment mechanics, the tight Article 75(3) attendance/membership rules everyone forgets a number on, and the difference between collective and individual ministerial responsibility.

◆ Closing the executive triangle

This note completes the picture the first two Topic 6 notes set up: the President is the nominal Union head, the Governor the nominal State head — and the Prime Minister, backed by the Council of Ministers, is where actual governing power sits at the Union level (the Chief Minister and State Council of Ministers being the exact State-level mirror, covered separately). Where the earlier two notes were heavy on discretion and case law, this one is heavier on precise numbers and definitions — Article 75(3)'s collective-responsibility rule and the 91st Amendment's ministry-size cap are two of the most number-precise facts in the whole Polity syllabus.

Week 3, Day 3 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapters 20–21.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

The Prime Minister is not directly elected by voters as "PM" — they become PM by being the leader who commands the confidence of the Lok Sabha, usually because they lead the party or coalition with a majority there. The President formally appoints the PM, but has almost no real choice when one party has a clear majority — the discretion only shows up in a hung Parliament (already covered in Note 00).

The Council of Ministers is the PM's team, and it comes in three ranks — Cabinet Ministers (the senior, most-consulted tier), Ministers of State, and Deputy Ministers — but the Constitution's own accountability rule, Article 75(3), makes the whole Council collectively answerable to the Lok Sabha as one unit: if the Lok Sabha passes a no-confidence motion, every Minister falls together, Cabinet rank or not. That "sink or swim together" idea — collective responsibility — is the single concept this entire note orbits around.

01

Appointment & tenure — Article 75

PointDetail
AppointmentThe President appoints the Prime Minister — no election "for PM" as such exists; the President invites the person most likely to command Lok Sabha confidence, ordinarily the leader of the majority party/coalition. Other Ministers are appointed by the President on the PM's advice.
Membership qualificationMust be a member of either House of Parliament. A person who is not yet an MP can still be appointed PM/Minister, but must get elected to either House within 6 months of appointment, or cease to hold office.
TermNo fixed term — holds office "during the pleasure of the President," but in practice this is entirely notional: so long as the PM commands the Lok Sabha's confidence, the President cannot remove them. The real check is a Lok Sabha no-confidence motion, not Presidential pleasure.
OathAdministered by the President — oath of office and oath of secrecy.
⚠

Trap: "During the pleasure of the President" appears for the PM (Art 75(2)) exactly as it does for the Governor (Art 156) — but the practical meaning is completely different. A Governor genuinely can be removed at the Union government's whim; a PM commanding a Lok Sabha majority cannot be removed by the President acting alone, because the President's own "pleasure" is itself constitutionally understood to track the House's confidence, not personal discretion.

02

Powers & functions of the Prime Minister

RoleContent
In relation to the Council of MinistersRecommends who becomes a Minister (and their portfolios) to the President; can ask a Minister to resign or advise the President to dismiss one; presides over Cabinet meetings, guiding and coordinating the work of different Ministries.
In relation to the PresidentPrincipal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers (Article 78) — communicates all Cabinet decisions relating to Union administration and legislative proposals; furnishes information the President may call for.
In relation to ParliamentAnnounces major policies on the floor of the House; recommends dissolution of the Lok Sabha to the President; is the Leader of the House in the Lok Sabha (or, if a Rajya Sabha member, in that House).
Other rolesChairman of NITI Aayog; chairs the Cabinet Committees on Appointments, Security, Political Affairs and Economic Affairs; chairman of the National Integration Council, Inter-State Council, National Water Resources Council and the National Disaster Management Authority.
The five descriptive roles (Mains vocabulary)Laxmikanth's framing, useful for any "assess the PM's position" answer: the PM is the chief spokesman of the Union government; the crisis manager-in-chief at the political level; the leader of the nation (as distinct from the President as head of state); the political head of the services; and the chief coordinator of the entire administration. Add the standard scholarly quotations: Britain's counterpart was described by Munro as "the keystone of the Cabinet arch," by Laski as "the pivot of the whole system of government," by Jennings as "the sun around which planets revolve," and by Morley as primus inter pares — "first among equals." The Indian PM occupies the same structural position.
03

Council of Ministers — composition, categories & the 91st Amendment cap

Three ranks — not interchangeable terms

RankRole
Cabinet MinistersHead major Ministries, sit in Cabinet meetings, take the most significant policy decisions. The Cabinet is the smaller, most powerful inner ring of the full Council of Ministers — not a separate body, but a subset of it.
Ministers of StateMay hold independent charge of a smaller Ministry/Department, or be attached to assist a Cabinet Minister; do not normally attend Cabinet meetings unless specially invited for a relevant item.
Deputy MinistersAttached to assist Cabinet Ministers or Ministers of State; hold no independent charge of any Department.

The constitutional articles behind the office (the Bare Act layer)

ArticleProvision
Article 74Council of Ministers with the PM at the head to aid and advise the President, who shall act in accordance with that advice (42nd Amendment) but may require reconsideration once (44th Amendment). 74(2): the question whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered shall not be inquired into by any court.
Article 75Appointment (75(1)); the 15% cap and anti-defection bar (75(1A) and 75(1B), inserted by the 91st Amendment); ministers hold office during the President's pleasure (75(2)); collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha (75(3)); oath (75(4)); the six-month non-member rule (75(5)); salaries determined by Parliament (75(6)).
Article 77Conduct of business of the Government of India: all executive action is expressed to be taken in the name of the President; orders so authenticated cannot be questioned on the ground that they are not made by the President; the President makes rules for the allocation of business among Ministers (the Transaction of Business Rules).
Article 78Duties of the PM towards the President — communicate all Cabinet decisions on administration and legislative proposals; furnish information the President calls for; submit for Cabinet consideration any matter on which a Minister has decided but the Council has not considered, if the President so requires.
Article 88Every Minister (and the Attorney General) has the right to speak in, and take part in the proceedings of, either House and any joint sitting or committee of which they are named a member — but is entitled to vote only in the House of which they are a member. This is the article behind the "speaks in both, votes in one" trap.

Three kinds of responsibility — and the one that does not exist in India

TypePosition in India
Collective responsibilityArticle 75(3) — the fundamental principle of the parliamentary system. The whole Council sinks or swims together, answerable to the Lok Sabha alone.
Individual responsibilityArticle 75(2) — ministers hold office during the President's pleasure, exercised on the PM's advice. This is how a single Minister can be dropped without the government falling.
No legal responsibilityUnlike Britain, India has no system of legal responsibility of ministers — a Minister's countersignature is not required on the President's order, and a Minister is not legally answerable in court for advice tendered to the President (reinforced by Article 74(2)). A statement claiming Indian ministers bear legal responsibility for their advice is false.
✚

"Council of Ministers" vs "Cabinet" — the distinction examiners love: every Cabinet Minister is a member of the Council of Ministers, but not every member of the Council of Ministers sits in the Cabinet. Collective responsibility (Article 75(3)) legally binds the entire Council, not just the Cabinet tier — a Minister of State falls along with everyone else if a no-confidence motion succeeds, even if they never once attended a Cabinet meeting.

The 91st Amendment, 2003 — the ministry-size cap

RuleDetail
Size capTotal strength of the Council of Ministers, including the PM/CM, cannot exceed 15% of the total membership of the Lok Sabha (Union) or the State Legislative Assembly (State) — inserted specifically to curb bloated "jumbo" ministries formed to accommodate coalition partners.
Minimum floor for small StatesFor smaller States/UTs with Legislative Assemblies, the number of Ministers, including the CM, shall not be less than 12.
Anti-defection linkageThe same 91st Amendment also barred a defector from being appointed Minister for the remainder of their term (or until re-elected), closing the "defect and get a ministerial berth as reward" loophole under the Tenth Schedule.

Cabinet Committees — a listed Week 3 "Important Topic"

✚

Nature: Cabinet Committees are extra-constitutional — the Constitution nowhere mentions them; they are created under the Rules of Business framed under Article 77(3). They are of two kinds, standing (permanent) and ad hoc (temporary, for a specific problem). They are set up by the Prime Minister according to the needs of the time, so their number and composition change from government to government. Membership varies from three to eight; they usually include only Cabinet Ministers, though non-Cabinet Ministers can also be members. They not only sort out issues and formulate proposals for Cabinet decision — they also take decisions themselves, though the Cabinet may review them.

The standing committees usually cited: Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs ("super-Cabinet", deals with major policy and all problems relating to foreign/domestic affairs), Economic Affairs, Appointments (senior civil-service appointments — chaired by the PM), Security (defence and national security — chaired by the PM), Parliamentary Affairs (the one usually chaired by the Home Minister, not the PM), Accommodation, Investment and Growth, and Employment and Skill Development. Trap: the PM chairs most but not all — Parliamentary Affairs and Accommodation are conventionally chaired by other senior ministers.

⚠

Kitchen Cabinet — the informal body that questions like to contrast with the formal one. It is a small, unofficial circle of the PM's most trusted colleagues and sometimes non-official persons (friends, family members, advisers) that becomes the real centre of decision-making. Merits cited: it is a much smaller unit and so more efficient a decision-making body; it can meet more often and transact business faster; it helps the PM maintain secrecy. Demerits: it reduces the authority of the full Cabinet, allows outsiders to influence government functioning, and enlarges the PM's personal power. The phrase is borrowed from the USA; in Britain the equivalent is the "Inner Cabinet."

✚

Recent-judgment layer — the defection-to-ministership fight didn't end in 2003. In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur (2020), a Manipur MLA who defected was made a Minister while the Speaker simply sat on the disqualification petition for years. The Supreme Court (Nariman, J.) directed the Speaker to decide within a set period, and when defiance continued, used Article 142 to restrain the Minister from entering the Assembly — effectively removing him. The judgment also recommended that Parliament consider replacing the Speaker (a party-political figure) with an independent tribunal for Tenth Schedule disqualifications. This is the standard current-affairs pairing for any question on whether anti-defection machinery actually works: the 91st Amendment closed the reward loophole on paper; Speaker delay reopened it in practice; the Court's Article 142 intervention and tribunal proposal are the live reform debate.

📖 Pros / cons / way forward — the anti-defection law itself

For: stability of governments; prevents wholesale horse-trading; an elected mandate belongs to the party platform, not the individual's resale value. Against: converts legislators into rubber stamps (a member can't vote conscience even on non-confidence matters); the Speaker's partisan position makes enforcement selective and slow; it weakens Parliament's deliberative character by making the whip supreme. Way forward (liftable conclusion): retain disqualification for genuine floor-crossing but transfer adjudication to an independent tribunal as Keisham Meghachandra recommends, and narrow the whip's reach to confidence motions and Money Bills — preserving both stability and legislative conscience.

04

Collective & individual responsibility — Article 75(3)

✚

Collective responsibility (Article 75(3)) is owed specifically and only to the Lok Sabha — the Rajya Sabha cannot remove the government via a no-confidence motion, even though Ministers may sit in either House. This is the same "confidence flows from the Lower House only" logic that runs through the whole Parliamentary-system design (Topic 5, Note 00). Every Cabinet decision binds every Minister publicly, even one who privately disagreed and was outvoted inside Cabinet — a Minister who cannot accept a collective decision is expected to resign, not dissent openly.

⚠

Trap: individual responsibility does not mean a Minister can be removed by a Lok Sabha vote against them personally — the House can only act against the government collectively. The President's power to drop a single Minister exists only through the pleasure doctrine, exercised on the Prime Minister's advice, not through any direct legislative vote against that one Minister.

!
Confidence is not only lost by a no-confidence motion. The Council must also resign if the Budget is rejected, if any cut motion is carried against the government, if the Motion of Thanks is defeated, or if a confidence motion it moves is lost. A censure motion is the exception — the Council need not resign but must seek the confidence of the House as soon as possible. The working rule: money defeat = confidence defeat; criticism is not. Full treatment in Topic 5, Note 03.

PYQ analysis & practice questions

Trend verdict: UPSC rarely asks the PM's powers directly — it tests the machinery around the office (Cabinet Secretariat, Cabinet Committees) and the defining principle of the system (responsibility to the Lok Sabha). Note in both genuine questions below how the correct answer turns on knowing what a body does not do.

(A) Genuine UPSC PYQs

2014Prelims

Which of the following are the functions of the Cabinet Secretariat?

  1. Preparation of agenda for Cabinet Meetings
  2. Secretarial assistance to Cabinet Committees
  3. Allocation of financial resources to the Ministries

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 3 only
  • (c) 1, 2 and 3
  • (d) None of the above
Answer: (a)
  • Allocating financial resources is the Ministry of Finance's job, not the Cabinet Secretariat's — the classic function-swap trap.
  • The Cabinet Secretariat provides secretarial assistance to the Cabinet and its Committees (Section 03's Cabinet Committees box) and is headed by the Cabinet Secretary, the senior-most civil servant.
2015Prelims

There is a Parliamentary System of Government in India because the

  • (a) Lok Sabha is elected directly by the people
  • (b) Parliament can amend the Constitution
  • (c) Rajya Sabha cannot be dissolved
  • (d) Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha
Answer: (d)
  • Verdict: direct hit on Article 75(3) and Section 04's collective-responsibility content.
  • The other three options are all true statements about India — but none of them is what makes the system parliamentary.
  • A model example of UPSC testing the definitional core rather than a fact.

M25Practice10 marks · 150 words

Distinguish between collective and individual ministerial responsibility in the Indian parliamentary system, and explain why the distinction matters for Cabinet decision-making discipline.

Directive: "Distinguish… and explain why it matters." Two demands. The distinction is straightforward; the marks are in the second half — the consequence for how Cabinet actually functions. Most answers stop after the distinction.
Skeleton
  1. Intro: both in Art 75
  2. Collective — to whom, effect
  3. Individual — mechanism
  4. Why it matters — the discipline
  5. Close: no legal responsibility

Model answer

Both principles sit in Article 75 but do different work.

Collective

Under Article 75(3) the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha alone — not to the Rajya Sabha, and not to the President. A successful no-confidence motion brings down the entire Council, including Ministers of State and Deputy Ministers.

Individual

Under Article 75(2) Ministers hold office during the President's pleasure, exercised on the Prime Minister's advice, so one Minister may be dropped for personal or departmental failure without the government falling. The limit: the Lok Sabha cannot remove an individual Minister; it can act only against the Council as a whole.

Why it matters

Together they produce Cabinet discipline. Because all sink together, dissent must be voiced inside Cabinet and abandoned outside it — a Minister who cannot accept a collective decision is expected to resign. Because the Prime Minister can have any individual removed, that expectation is enforceable. Collective responsibility supplies the norm; individual responsibility supplies the sanction. Unlike Britain, India has no legal ministerial responsibility: no countersignature is required, and Article 74(2) bars courts from examining the advice tendered.

Value additions used: Arts 75(2), 75(3), 74(2) · Lok-Sabha-only responsibility · all ranks fall together · resign-don't-dissent convention · the norm-versus-sanction formulation · absence of legal responsibility, contrasted with Britain.

★ 90-second recall

Last-mile revision — PM & Council of Ministers

  • President appoints PM (no separate election "for PM"); other Ministers appointed on PM's advice.
  • Non-MP appointee must get elected to either House within 6 months or lose office.
  • "During the pleasure of the President" for PM ≠ real removability — Lok Sabha confidence is the actual check.
  • Three ranks: Cabinet Ministers > Ministers of State > Deputy Ministers. Cabinet is a subset of the full Council.
  • 91st Amendment, 2003: Council size capped at 15% of Lok Sabha/Assembly membership; small-State floor of 12; also barred defectors from ministerial posts.
  • Article 75(3): collective responsibility owed to the Lok Sabha only, binds the entire Council regardless of rank.
  • Individual responsibility: President can drop one Minister on PM's advice — not a House vote mechanism.
  • PM is Chairman of NITI Aayog and Leader of the House in whichever House they belong to.
