The Supreme Court

Articles 124–147. The single highest-yield institution in Polity: it supplies the numbers, the jurisdictions, the collegium story and the two articles — 141 and 142 — that every judicial-activism question is ultimately built on.

38sanctioned strength from 2026 — 37 judges plus the Chief Justice
◆ Where the marks actually are

This chapter is examined on numbers, jurisdictions and who-decides-what far more than on doctrine. Across 2009–2025 the Judiciary microtheme produced questions on the power to increase judge strength (Parliament, not the President), on which disputes fall in original jurisdiction, on advisory jurisdiction being available only on the President's request, and on Article 142's limits. Every one of those is a fact, not an argument.

The doctrine still matters, but it earns its marks in Mains. Learn the table first, the story second.

Week 5, Day 3 of the study plan, sourced from NCERT Class 11 Indian Constitution at Work and Laxmikanth Chapter 26.

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Think of the Indian courts as a pyramid. At the bottom sit the district and subordinate courts, where almost every case begins. Above them, one High Court per State or group of States. At the apex, a single Supreme Court for the whole country. Unlike the United States, India does not run separate federal and State court systems side by side — we have one integrated judiciary, and the Supreme Court sits on top of all of it.

That court does four different jobs, and the exam tests whether you can tell them apart. Sometimes it is the first court to hear a matter — a fight between the Union and a State has nowhere else to go. Sometimes it hears an appeal from a High Court. Sometimes it protects your Fundamental Rights directly, by issuing a writ. And occasionally the President asks it for an opinion on a legal question, which the Court gives without deciding anybody's case.

Two extra powers make it unusually strong. Under Article 141 whatever the Supreme Court declares is law binding on every other court in India. Under Article 142 it may pass any order needed to do "complete justice" — a phrase with no fixed boundary, which is precisely why it is controversial.

Composition & strength — Article 124

Article 124(1) is one of the few places where the Constitution sets a number and then immediately hands Parliament the power to change it. That structure is itself examinable.

PointDetail
The original textArticle 124(1): there shall be a Supreme Court consisting of a Chief Justice of India and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, of not more than seven other Judges. The Constitution therefore fixed the original bench at 8.
Who can change itParliament, by ordinary law — not the President, not the Chief Justice, not a constitutional amendment. This exact point was asked in Prelims 2014. The governing statute is the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956.
Qualifications — Art 124(3)Citizen of India, and one of: (i) a High Court judge for five years; (ii) a High Court advocate for ten years; (iii) a distinguished jurist in the President's opinion. There is no minimum age prescribed for a Supreme Court judge.
The distinguished-jurist routeAvailable since 1950 and never once used. A favourite "which of these has never happened" statement.
Retirement age65 years for the Supreme Court; 62 for a High Court. Learn them as a pair — this is a classic numbers-with-a-twin trap.
Acting CJI — Art 126The President appoints a judge of the Supreme Court as Acting Chief Justice when the office is vacant or the CJI is absent or unable to act.
Ad hoc judges — Art 127With the President's prior consent, the CJI may request a High Court judge (qualified for the Supreme Court) to attend sittings as an ad hoc judge when quorum is short.
Retired judges — Art 128The CJI may request a retired judge of the Supreme Court, or a retired High Court judge qualified for the Supreme Court, to sit and act — but only with the President's previous consent and with the consent of the person concerned. Both consents are essential; a statement omitting either is false. Asked in Prelims 2021.
Seat — Art 130Delhi, or such other place as the CJI may, with the President's approval, appoint. So the seat is not constitutionally frozen in Delhi.
!
The strength number changed in 2026 — carry the new figure and the old one. On 5 May 2026 the Union Cabinet approved the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026, raising puisne judges from 33 to 37, i.e. total sanctioned strength from 34 to 38 including the CJI. An Ordinance was promulgated on 16 May 2026 and Parliament has since passed the Bill replacing it. The request came from CJI Surya Kant, citing rising filings and the difficulty of constituting Constitution Benches alongside regular work. Before this, the last increase was 2019 (30 → 33 puisne, 31 → 34 total). A statement saying "the sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court is 34" was correct until May 2026 and is now wrong.
02

Appointment & the collegium — the constitutional text versus what actually happens

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Read Article 124(2) and you would conclude that the President appoints judges after consulting whichever judges he thinks fit. That is the text. It is not the practice, and the gap between the two is entirely the work of three judgments the profession calls the Three Judges Cases.

The fight was over one word: consultation. Does consulting the Chief Justice mean merely asking his view, which the government may then ignore? Or does it mean the Chief Justice's view prevails? In 1981 the Court said the first. In 1993 it reversed itself and said the second. In 1998 it kept the second answer but made the Chief Justice share the decision with a group of senior colleagues — the collegium.

So today judges are chosen by judges. No statute says so; no amendment created it. It is a system built entirely out of the interpretation of one word.

Next → the three cases, in order, with what each actually held
CaseYear & popular nameWhat it held
S.P. Gupta v. Union of India1981 — First Judges Case"Consultation" does not mean concurrence. The executive has primacy; the CJI's opinion can be departed from for cogent reasons. Also famous for a second reason — it is the case that relaxed locus standi and formally opened the door to PIL (see Note 08).
SC Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India1993 — Second Judges CaseOverruled S.P. Gupta. "Consultation" means concurrence; the CJI's view has primacy. Created the collegium — at this stage the CJI plus the two senior-most judges.
In re Presidential Reference1998 — Third Judges CaseArose from an Article 143 reference by President K.R. Narayanan. Held that the CJI must consult a plurality of judges: the collegium is expanded to the CJI plus the four senior-most judges for Supreme Court appointments. A recommendation made by the CJI alone is not binding on the government.
◆
The collegium sizes are a numbers-with-a-twin trap. For Supreme Court appointments: CJI + 4 senior-most judges (total five). For High Court appointments and transfers: CJI + 2 senior-most judges (total three), consulting the concerned High Court collegium. Confusing the two is one of the cheapest marks a setter can take from you.

The NJAC episode, in order

📖 The story — how Parliament tried to take the pen back, and lost

By 2014 the collegium had few public defenders. It was opaque, kept no records worth the name, and had produced its own controversies. Parliament acted with unusual unanimity: the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014 were passed by both Houses and ratified by more than half the State legislatures.

The NJAC was to have six members: the CJI as chairperson, the two next senior-most Supreme Court judges, the Union Law Minister, and two eminent persons chosen by a committee of the Prime Minister, the CJI and the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha. One of the two eminent persons had to be from the SC/ST, OBC, minorities or be a woman. The detail that decided the case was a veto rule: any two members could block a recommendation.

In October 2015, in the Fourth Judges Case (SC Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India), a five-judge bench struck down both the amendment and the Act by 4:1, Justice Chelameswar dissenting. The reasoning: judicial primacy in appointments is part of the independence of the judiciary, which is part of the basic structure. Because two non-judge members could veto the three judges, judicial primacy was gone. The collegium revived.

What has not happened since is as examinable as what did: the Memorandum of Procedure, which the Court directed be revised to improve transparency, has still not been finalised more than a decade later.

!
Prelims 2022 asked this and the answer surprises people. The statement "The Supreme Court struck down the 99th Amendment as being violative of the independence of judiciary" was marked correct — while the companion statement in the same question was wrong. Note the precision required: it was struck down as violating the independence of the judiciary as a basic feature, not for any procedural defect in its passage. The 99th Amendment was ratified properly; that was never the problem.

Tenure, removal & the machinery of independence

Removal — Article 124(4) and (5)

StageRequirement
GroundsOnly proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The Constitution does not define either term — a point tested directly in Prelims 2019.
The word "impeachment"Strictly, judges are removed, not impeached. Impeachment in the Constitution refers only to the President under Article 61. Newspapers use "impeachment" loosely; a careful stem may not.
NoticeSigned by 100 members of the Lok Sabha or 50 members of the Rajya Sabha — under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968, not the Constitution.
AdmissionThe Speaker or Chairman may admit or refuse the motion. There is no obligation to admit — again Prelims 2019 turned on this.
InquiryA three-member committee: a Supreme Court judge, a High Court Chief Justice, and a distinguished jurist.
VotingIn each House separately: a majority of the total membership of that House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. Both conditions, both Houses, and — under Art 124(4) — in the same session.
OutcomeThe President then passes the removal order. No judge of the Supreme Court has ever been removed by this process.
📖 The story — the case that showed how the process really ends

On 14 March 2025, during a firefighting operation at the Delhi residence of Justice Yashwant Varma, then a judge of the Delhi High Court, personnel reported finding sacks of burnt currency notes in an outhouse. The then Chief Justice, Sanjiv Khanna, invoked the judiciary's in-house procedure — an internal mechanism with no statutory basis at all — and constituted a committee, which reported on 4 May 2025 that the judge had "tacit" control of the storeroom. The collegium repatriated him to his parent High Court, Allahabad.

Parliament then moved. On 12 August 2025 Speaker Om Birla announced a removal notice signed by 146 members across the treasury and opposition benches, including the Leader of the Opposition — comfortably above the 100 required. He admitted it and constituted the three-member committee under the Judges (Inquiry) Act.

And then it simply stopped. On 10 April 2026, before the committee reported, Justice Varma resigned under Article 217(1)(a). Because an inquiry under the Judges (Inquiry) Act can only run against a sitting judge, the proceedings abated the moment he left office.

The lesson to carry into an answer: the removal process is so demanding that in practice it is never completed. Resignation is the real exit. That is the strongest available argument for judicial accountability reform — and the reason the National Judicial Accountability framework keeps returning to the agenda.

!
The exception to hold. A removal inquiry abates on resignation, because it can only be conducted against a sitting judge. Contrast this with the general rule you have learnt elsewhere — that constitutional processes, once begun, run their course. Here they do not. Only one removal motion has ever been carried in one House (Justice V. Ramaswami, 1993, where the Lok Sabha motion failed for want of the required majority after abstentions), and none has ever completed both Houses.

The independence toolkit — what actually protects a judge

🔴 Prelims — the safeguards, itemised

◆
Security of tenure. Removal only by the Art 124(4) process; not at the executive's pleasure.
◆
Salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund of India — non-votable. Parliament cannot vote them down. Asked in Prelims 2012.
◆
Emoluments cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment — except during a Financial Emergency under Article 360.
◆
Conduct cannot be discussed in Parliament or a State legislature except on a removal motion — Article 121 (Parliament) and Article 211 (State legislature).
◆
Contempt power — Article 129 makes the Supreme Court a court of record with power to punish for its own contempt.
◆
Ban on post-retirement practice — a retired Supreme Court judge may not plead before any court or authority in India. A retired High Court judge may not plead in that High Court or courts subordinate to it, but may appear in the Supreme Court and other High Courts.
◆
Staff appointments — the CJI appoints the Court's officers and servants (Art 146), and expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund.
◆
Jurisdiction cannot be curtailed — Parliament may extend but not reduce the Supreme Court's jurisdiction. Asked in Prelims 2019.
!
The trap inside the salary rule. Judges' salaries are charged on the Consolidated Fund — meaning not submitted to Parliament's vote — but they are still discussable. And the protection against reduction has exactly one carve-out: Article 360, Financial Emergency, under which the President may direct a reduction in the salaries of judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts. A statement claiming the protection is absolute is false.

The five jurisdictions

Nearly every Supreme Court PYQ is really a jurisdiction question in disguise. Learn which box each situation falls into and most of them answer themselves.

JurisdictionArticleWhat it covers — and the limits that get tested
Original
(exclusive)		131Disputes between the Union and one or more States; between the Union and States on one side and States on the other; between two or more States. Strictly federal disputes only, and only where a question of legal right is involved. Excluded: a dispute involving a Union Territory (a UT is not a State), election disputes, and — expressly — disputes arising out of a pre-Constitution treaty or agreement still in operation. Tested in Prelims 2012 and 2014.
Writ
(also original, but concurrent)		32Enforcement of Fundamental Rights only. Five writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto. Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right — Ambedkar's "heart and soul of the Constitution". Shared with the High Courts under Art 226, so it is not exclusive.
Appellate132–134AFour channels: constitutional (132 — substantial question of law as to interpretation of the Constitution); civil (133); criminal (134, including the death-sentence-on-reversal route); and by special leave (136).
Special leave136Discretionary leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination or order of any court or tribunal in India — except a court or tribunal constituted under any law relating to the Armed Forces. That armed-forces exclusion is the single most examinable limit on Art 136.
Advisory143The President may refer a question of law or fact of public importance, or a dispute arising out of a pre-Constitution treaty. Two crucial asymmetries: for ordinary references under 143(1) the Court may refuse to answer; for treaty references under 143(2) it must report. And the opinion is advisory only — not binding on the President, and not binding as a precedent under Art 141 in the strict sense. The Court cannot take up an advisory reference on its own initiative — Prelims 2010.
Review137The Court may review its own judgment or order, subject to any law made by Parliament and to rules under Article 145. A review petition is normally filed within 30 days. Where review fails, a curative petition may lie — a purely judge-made remedy created in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002), not found anywhere in the Constitution.
!
What Article 131 does NOT cover — added after the Week 5 paper, where this cost a question. A note that lists what a jurisdiction includes must also list what it excludes. Article 131 does not extend to: a dispute involving a Union Territory (not a State); an election dispute to Parliament or a State legislature (those go to the High Court under the RPA); a dispute arising out of a pre-Constitution treaty still in operation (expressly excluded by the proviso); inter-State water disputes, which Article 262 assigns to a tribunal and from which Parliament may bar the jurisdiction of all courts including the Supreme Court; and matters referred to the Finance Commission, and the adjustment of certain expenses between the Union and the States, which are constitutionally assigned elsewhere. The Art 262 exclusion is the sharpest, because it is the one place the Constitution lets Parliament shut the Supreme Court out entirely.
!
Three "only" statements worth memorising as a block. (1) Advisory jurisdiction can be triggered only by the President — never suo motu, never by a State, never by Parliament. (2) Article 131 covers only disputes where a State is a party in its federal capacity — a private citizen cannot invoke it. (3) Article 136 reaches every court and tribunal except armed-forces tribunals. Each of these is a rule with a hard edge, and hard edges are where statement questions are built.

Two further jurisdictions worth a line each

!
Two contempt facts the Week 5 paper took marks for. (1) The three limbs of CRIMINAL contempt — publication or an act that scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers the authority of, any court; that prejudices or interferes with the due course of any judicial proceeding; or that obstructs the administration of justice in any other manner. Against that: wilful disobedience of an order or breach of an undertaking is CIVIL contempt, and fair and reasonable criticism of a judicial act is not contempt at all, nor is comment on the judiciary's administrative side. (2) Under Section 10 of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 it is the High Court that punishes contempt of subordinate courts, and the power to order arrest is a punitive measure.
!
The protection under Articles 121 and 211 is narrower than it looks. Parliament and State legislatures may not discuss the conduct of a judge except on a removal motion — but that immunity covers a judge's official conduct only. It does not extend to his private conduct. A statement claiming the protection covers both is false, and that single word decided a question in the Week 5 paper.
◆
Court of record — Art 129. Its judgments are recorded as evidence and cannot be questioned in any court, and it has the power to punish for contempt of itself. Note the division of labour tested in Prelims 2022: the Constitution empowers the Supreme Court (Art 129) and the High Courts (Art 215) to punish for contempt, but the Constitution does not define civil and criminal contempt — the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 does, following the H.N. Sanyal Committee report, and Parliament has the power to legislate on it under Entry 77 of the Union List.
◆
Transfer of cases — Art 139A. Inserted by the 42nd Amendment. The Court may withdraw cases involving the same substantial questions of law pending before it and one or more High Courts, or transfer a case from one High Court to another.
05

Articles 141, 142 and 144 — the force multipliers

🌱 Start here — plain English first

Three short articles turn the Supreme Court from a court that decides cases into an institution that governs. Article 141: whatever the Court declares is law binding on all courts in India. Article 142: it may pass whatever decree or order is necessary for doing complete justice in a matter before it. Article 144: every authority, civil and judicial, in India shall act in aid of the Supreme Court.

Article 142 is the interesting one because "complete justice" is undefined. It has been used generously — to dissolve irretrievably broken marriages without waiting out statutory periods, to transfer the Bhopal gas settlement, to order the cleaning of the Taj Mahal. And it has been used in ways that provoked a constitutional confrontation.

Next → the 2025–26 confrontation, which is now the standard illustration of Article 142's limits
📖 The story — Article 142 and the assent case, in four beats

Beat 1 — April 2025. In State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu the Supreme Court found the Governor's years-long inaction on ten Bills "illegal" and "erroneous". It held the Governor has only three options under Article 200, imposed timelines measured in one and three months, made breach judicially reviewable — and then used Article 142 to deem the ten Bills to have received assent. That last step meant the Bills became law by judicial order, without a Governor's signature.

Beat 2 — the backlash. The Vice-President publicly described Article 142 as a "nuclear missile" available to the judiciary against democratic forces. The objection was not to the finding of delay; it was to a court supplying an assent the Constitution assigns to someone else.

Beat 3 — May 2025. President Droupadi Murmu invoked Article 143 and referred fourteen questions on the powers of the Governor and the President under Articles 200 and 201. This was the 16th Presidential Reference since 1950.

Beat 4 — 20 November 2025. A five-judge Constitution Bench headed by CJI B.R. Gavai delivered a unanimous 111-page opinion (Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, 2025 INSC 1333). It kept the three-options holding — the Governor cannot simply sit on a Bill without returning it. It reversed almost everything else: no timelines can be read into Articles 200 and 201; there can be no "deemed assent"; the Governor's and President's actions under those articles are not justiciable; and Article 142 cannot supplant a constitutional provision. It left one door open — prolonged, unexplained, indefinite inaction invites limited judicial scrutiny, and a court may issue a limited direction to act, without commenting on the merits.

!
The examinable sentence. Article 142 is a power to do complete justice within the constitutional scheme, not a power to displace it. That is the holding of the November 2025 advisory opinion, and it is the single most current line available on the limits of judicial power. Pair it with the Prelims 2019 question, which had already established the converse — that ordinary laws cannot limit the constitutional power under Article 142, i.e. the Court is not constrained by parliamentary legislation in exercising it. Both propositions are true and they are not in conflict: statutes cannot cut Article 142 down, but the Constitution itself can.
ProvisionWhat to carry into the exam
Article 141Law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India. Note the wording: "all courts" — which on the settled reading does not bind the Supreme Court itself, leaving it free to overrule its own precedent.
Article 142(1)Decrees and orders necessary for doing complete justice. Enforceable throughout India as Parliament may prescribe.
Article 142(2)Powers as to discovery, production of documents and investigation or punishment of contempt, subject to law made by Parliament.

◆
The five-judge rule is narrower than students assume. Article 145(3) requires a minimum of five judges only for cases involving a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of this Constitution, or for an advisory reference under Art 143. It is not a general rule for "important" cases, and a Constitution Bench is not required merely because a case is high-profile.

Constituent Assembly ammunition

◆ For any "should judges appoint judges?" question

Ambedkar addressed the appointment question directly in the Assembly and rejected both extremes. He refused to leave appointments to the executive alone, saying it would be "dangerous" to allow the President an unfettered choice. He equally refused to give the Chief Justice a veto, in a line worth quoting in substance: to make the CJI's concurrence necessary would be to transfer to him a power that "we are not prepared to vest in the President" — no one, he said, is a person with no failings.

The value of this for Mains is that it cuts against the current arrangement as much as against the NJAC. It lets you write a balanced answer: the collegium is not what the framers designed, and neither was the NJAC. The framers wanted consultation without primacy on either side, and we have never built that.

Pass 2 — the adjacent surface

◆ Why this section exists — read this first

The Week 4 paper is the reason. Eleven of the twenty-five questions you lost turned on facts that were not in the notes at all — consultative committees, the State Finance Commission, who is not the ex-officio chairman of a committee, the Krishna Menon Committee behind the COPU. None of them was in the schedule's "Important Topics" column. Every one of them sat one step outside it.

That is this setter's signature. He treats a schedule bullet as a chapter reference, then asks about the bodies, statutes and office-holders orbiting the listed institution. So this section deliberately collects the orbit of the judiciary: the statutes, the specialised courts, the named committees and the reform vocabulary. It is not padding — it is the specific surface that cost you twenty-two marks last week.

If a stem asks who is not something, work the list rather than the option.

M34Practice · Week 515 marks · 250 words

"The collegium system was a remedy for executive dominance that has become a problem of its own." Critically examine in the light of the Fourth Judges Case and subsequent developments.

Directive: "Critically examine." Take the proposition seriously on both sides and arrive at a position. A list of merits followed by a list of demerits, with no verdict, is a mid-range answer at best.
Skeleton
  1. The lineage — why the collegium exists
  2. What it fixed
  3. What it created
  4. Why the NJAC was not the answer either
  5. Verdict and way forward

Model answer

Judicial appointments in India have been governed not by the text of Article 124(2) but by four readings of a single word in it. S.P. Gupta (1981) read "consultation" as leaving primacy with the executive; the Second Judges Case (1993) reversed that and created the collegium; the Third Judges Case (1998), on an Article 143 reference, fixed it at the CJI and four senior-most judges.

What it fixed

The 1993 shift responded to a real pathology — the supersessions of 1973 and 1977 and the mass transfers of the Emergency had shown that executive primacy over appointments translated directly into a pliant bench. Insulating selection from the government of the day was, and remains, a defensible constitutional objective.

What it created

The remedy carries no procedural discipline: no published criteria, no recorded reasons, no fixed timelines, and an outcome the Constitution never contemplated — judges selecting judges with no external participation. The Memorandum of Procedure that the 2015 bench itself directed be revised remains unfinalised more than a decade later, which is the sharpest available evidence that self-correction has failed.

Why the NJAC was not the answer

The 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act were struck down in 2015 by 4:1 because two non-judicial members could veto three judges, extinguishing judicial primacy and, with it, a basic feature. The verdict was correct on the veto and evasive on the diagnosis: it defended primacy without addressing opacity.

Ambedkar refused both extremes in the Assembly, declining to give the President an unfettered hand or the Chief Justice a veto. Neither the collegium nor the NJAC is what he described. The reform worth pursuing is therefore procedural rather than structural — a statutory secretariat, published criteria and reasons, and enforceable timelines — leaving primacy where the basic structure now requires it.

Value additions used: Arts 124(2), 124A–124C, 143 · the four Judges Cases with years and the 4:1 split · supersessions of 1973 and 1977 · the unfinalised Memorandum of Procedure · Ambedkar's rejection of both extremes.

M35Practice · Week 510 marks · 150 words

Discuss the scope and limits of Article 142 with reference to recent constitutional developments.

Directive: "Discuss." Both sides, then a position. The examiner is looking for whether you know the November 2025 opinion; without it this is a 2015-vintage answer.
Skeleton
  1. What the power is
  2. How far it has reached
  3. The boundary now drawn
  4. Verdict

Model answer

Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to pass any decree or order necessary for doing complete justice in a cause before it — a phrase the Constitution leaves undefined, and therefore a power whose limits are set by the Court itself.

Its reach

It has underwritten the Union Carbide settlement, the creation of the curative petition in Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002) — a remedy with no textual basis at all — and the dissolution of irretrievably broken marriages without statutory waiting periods.

The boundary

In State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (April 2025) the Court used Article 142 to deem assent to ten Bills. A Presidential Reference followed, and on 20 November 2025 a Constitution Bench held that there is no deemed assent, that no timelines can be read into Articles 200 and 201, and — decisively — that Article 142 cannot supplant constitutional provisions.

The settled position is therefore narrow and clear. Article 142 fills gaps; it does not substitute the Court for another constitutional functionary. Ordinary law cannot confine it, as the Court has long held, but the Constitution can — and now expressly does.

Last-mile revision — the Supreme Court

  • Art 124(1) — CJI + 7 originally, "until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number". Parliament changes it, by ordinary Act.
  • Strength ladder (excluding CJI): 7 → 10 (1956) → 13 (1960) → 17 (1977) → 25 (1986) → 30 (2008) → 33 (2019) → 37 (2026). Total now 38.
  • Art 124(3) — HC judge 5 yrs / HC advocate 10 yrs / distinguished jurist. No minimum age. Jurist route never used. Retire at 65 (HC: 62).
  • 126 Acting CJI · 127 ad hoc · 128 retired judges (both consents) · 130 seat.
  • Three Judges Cases: 1981 executive primacy → 1993 judicial primacy, collegium of 3 → 1998 collegium of 5 (Art 143 reference). HC collegium = CJI + 2.
  • NJAC — 99th Amendment + NJAC Act, 2014; six members; any two could veto; struck down Oct 2015, 4:1, for violating judicial independence as basic structure.
  • Art 124(4) removal — proved misbehaviour or incapacity, neither defined; 100 LS / 50 RS notice under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968; Speaker may refuse; 3-member committee; double majority in each House, same session. Never completed.
  • Justice Varma — cash found 14 Mar 2025 · in-house committee reported 4 May 2025 · motion with 146 signatures admitted 12 Aug 2025 · resigned 10 Apr 2026 under Art 217(1)(a) · inquiry abated.
  • Jurisdictions: 131 original/exclusive (no UTs, no election disputes) · 32 writs · 132–134A appellate · 136 SLP (not armed-forces tribunals) · 143 advisory (President only; 143(1) may decline, 143(2) must report) · 137 review (30 days) → curative, from Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002).
  • 141 binding on all courts (not itself) · 142 complete justice · 144 act in aid · 145(3) minimum five judges for constitutional interpretation or an Art 143 reference · 146 CJI appoints staff.
  • Art 129 court of record + contempt; Constitution does not define civil/criminal contempt — the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 does, after the H.N. Sanyal Committee. Entry 77, Union List.
  • Salaries charged on the CFI, non-votable; cannot be reduced except under Art 360. Conduct not discussable except on a removal motion — Arts 121 and 211. Parliament may extend but not curtail jurisdiction.
  • Nov 2025 advisory opinion (2025 INSC 1333, 16th Presidential Reference): three options stand; no timelines; no deemed assent; actions not justiciable; Art 142 cannot supplant the Constitution; but prolonged unexplained inaction invites limited scrutiny.
  • Post-retirement bar: a retired SC judge may not plead before any court in India; a retired HC judge may plead in the SC and other High Courts.
