Q4 · Negative · you (d), key (c)

Two statements — (1) a removal motion cannot be rejected by the Speaker under the Judges (Inquiry) Act; (2) the Constitution defines "incapacity and proved misbehaviour". Which is/are INCORRECT?

  • (b) 2 only

Answer: (c) — both statements are incorrect

  • Both facts are in Note 05, Section 03, and both appear in the trap bank. You knew them.
  • This is a polarity error, not a knowledge error. The stem asked which are incorrect; you answered as though it asked which are correct. Answering (d) means "both statements are true" — which contradicts material you had drilled two days earlier.
Q7 · Statements · you (d), key (a)

(1) The original Constitution envisaged a Supreme Court with a Chief Justice and five puisne judges. (2) Rules of practice and procedure are made by the Supreme Court with the President's approval. (3) The Supreme Court's jurisdiction may be enlarged by the President in consultation with the CJI.

  • (a) 2 only
  • (b) 1 and 2
  • (c) 2 and 3
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (a) — 2 only
  • Statement 1 is wrong on the number: seven puisne judges, not five — Article 124(1). Statement 3 is wrong on the authority: Parliament enlarges jurisdiction under Article 138, not the President.
  • Statement 2 is correct — Article 145(1), rules made with the President's approval.
  • All three are in Note 05. The strength ladder opens Section 01 and the "seven" is in the revision tab. Article 138 is in the Section 06 articles table.
  • Two of the three failures were an authority word and a number — precisely the families the red-pen grid exists for.
Q24 · Statements · you (d), key (a)

(1) There is no provision in the Constitution for the impeachment of a judge of a High Court or the Supreme Court. (2) Parliament is not empowered to discuss the conduct of a judge, and that protection applies to his judicial duties as well as his private conduct.

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both
  • (d) Neither
Answer: (a) — 1 only
  • Statement 1 is correct and turns on vocabulary: the Constitution provides for removal, and reserves the word impeachment for the President under Article 61. Note 05 states this expressly.
  • Statement 2 is wrong on its last clause: the protection under Arts 121 and 211 extends to a judge's official conduct only, not his private conduct.
  • Partial gap. Arts 121/211 are in the note, but the official-versus-private limit was not. That distinction has now been added.
Q28 · Statements · you (d), key (a)

Statement 3 read: "By the writ of quo warranto, the court may strike off an order passed by any official of the government, local body or statutory body."

  • (a) 1 and 2 only
  • (b) 2 and 3 only
  • (c) 1 and 3 only
  • (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (a) — 1 and 2 only
  • Striking down an order already passed is certiorari. Quo warranto asks a person holding a public office by what authority he holds it.
  • Squarely in Note 06, Section 04 — the five-writ table gives each writ its literal meaning and its one distinguishing feature, and the trap bank has the same swap.
  • You spent 1:58 here. This was not a time problem; the swapped definition simply was not recognised as a swap.
Q29 · Statements · you (a), key (c)

(1) Courts below the High Court are referred to in the Constitution as subordinate courts. (2) The principal function of the District Court is to hear appeals from the subordinate courts.

  • (a) 1 only
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) Both 1 and 2
  • (d) Neither
Answer: (c) — both
  • Note 07, Section 01 states that the District Judge is the highest civil court in the district and hears appeals from subordinate judges.
  • You rejected statement 2, most likely because "principal function" felt like an overstatement. It is the standard textbook formulation.
  • 2:00 spent. With both statements individually familiar, this was a confidence failure rather than a knowledge failure.
Q35 · Statements · you (a), key (c)

The Rajya Sabha has no procedure for moving: (1) adjournment motion (2) censure motion (3) no-confidence motion.

  • (a) 1 and 3
  • (b) 2 only
  • (c) All of the above
  • (d) 2 and 3
Answer: (c) — all three
  • All three censure the government, and the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha alone. So none is available in the Rajya Sabha.
  • You had adjournment and no-confidence right and dropped censure. The unifying principle — anything that expresses want of confidence belongs to the House that can withdraw confidence — is the thing to hold, rather than three separate facts.
  • Covered in Topic 5 Note 03's motions family; the principle has now been stated explicitly there.
Q39 · Direct · you (a), key (d)

A theory under which judges hesitate to strike down laws unless obviously unconstitutional, limit their own power, and emphasise procedure established by law over due process. Which theory?

  • (a) Judicial Review
  • (b) Judicial Overreach
  • (c) Judicial Activism
  • (d) Judicial Restraint
Answer: (d) — Judicial Restraint
  • Note 08, Section 02 is a four-row table of exactly these four terms, and restraint is defined there as deference plus confinement to the dispute.
  • You answered "judicial review" — the baseline power, not the posture. Review is what the court may do; restraint, activism and overreach describe how energetically it does it.
  • Fix: whenever the stem describes an attitude ("hesitate", "limit", "should not"), it is asking about a posture on the ladder, never about the power itself.
Q41 · Direct · you (c), key (d)

Which power of the Supreme Court allows it to declare legislative and executive actions contrary to Fundamental Rights void?

  • (a) Original Jurisdiction
  • (b) Appellate Jurisdiction
  • (c) Writ Jurisdiction
  • (d) The power granted by Article 13
Answer: (d) — Article 13, judicial review
  • Note 08, Section 01 opens with Article 13 as the textual foundation of judicial review — the article that makes inconsistent laws void.
  • Writ jurisdiction is the remedy through which review is exercised; Article 13 is the power that voids the law. The question asks what makes the action void, not how a litigant reaches the court.
  • 2:03 spent — you were choosing between (c) and (d), which is the right shortlist. The tiebreak is that a writ runs against a person or authority; Article 13 operates on the law itself.
Q42 · How many · you (b), key (a)

(1) The President is bound to accept the Supreme Court's advice. (2) The Court may refuse to advise. (3) The jurisdiction is limited to matters arising after the Constitution came into effect.

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
Answer: (a) — only one (statement 2)
  • Statement 1 is wrong — the opinion is advisory, not binding. Statement 3 is wrong — Art 143(2) covers disputes arising from pre-Constitution treaties, so the jurisdiction expressly reaches backwards.
  • All three facts sit in Note 05, Section 04, and statement 3 is the exact point of the 143(1)-versus-143(2) contrast.
  • You counted two correct instead of one — the classic how-many failure, where one statement is judged on impression rather than tested. 2:13 spent.
Q47 · How many · you (b), key (a)

(1) SC/ST reservation in a State Assembly is based on population ratios. (2) Originally for ten years, extended until 2020. (3) An SC or ST member can only contest a reserved seat.

  • (a) Only one
  • (b) Only two
  • (c) All three
  • (d) None
