# Mains Answer Craft — the standing method for writing GS answers

This file governs how every Mains answer in this project is written, and how Lavan should write in the exam. It exists because Mains is not a knowledge test — it is a **communication test conducted under a stopwatch, marked by a tired human being**. Knowing more than the next candidate is worth nothing if the examiner cannot see it in ninety seconds.

Read Part 1 before anything else. It is the reason every other rule in this file exists.

---

## Part 1 — The examiner's reality (design for this, not for an ideal reader)

Four facts about how your answer is actually consumed. Every rule below follows from them.

1. **Your paper will not be read line by line.** An examiner handles hundreds of scripts. Most of your answer is *scanned*; some paragraphs get read properly — and you cannot know which. Design so that a **skim still delivers the argument**.
2. **Marks are awarded for what the examiner can find, not for what you know.** A brilliant point buried in the middle of an unbroken paragraph is, for scoring purposes, invisible. Structure is not decoration; it is retrieval.
3. **Presentation is rewarded disproportionately — but only functional presentation.** Clear spacing, visible sub-headings, legible hand, a small diagram: these reduce the examiner's effort and get repaid. Multiple pen colours, ornamental boxes, elaborate underlining: minutes spent for almost nothing. Keep it clean, never pretty.
4. **The realistic ceiling is lower than beginners assume.** Strong candidates take roughly **6–7 out of 10** on a good answer; a GS paper around 50% is a very good score. The target is therefore *never* a perfect answer — it is a **consistently above-average answer in seven and a half minutes, twenty times in a row**. Chasing perfection on Q1 and running out of time by Q17 is the single most common way to lose a rank.

**The governing sentence of this whole file:** write so that a hurried examiner, skimming, cannot miss the fact that you (a) answered the exact question asked, (b) knew specific things, and (c) balanced the argument.

## Part 2 — The scoring model: where marks actually come from

For a 15-mark / 250-word GS-II answer, marks distribute roughly like this. Use it to allocate effort.

| Component | Weight | What earns it |
|---|---|---|
| **Directive compliance** | Highest single lever | Did you do what the tail word demanded — *examine* vs *critically examine* vs *discuss* vs *evaluate*? Getting this wrong caps the answer no matter how good the content. |
| **Structure & visibility** | Very high | Intro–Body–Conclusion, sub-headings, short blocks. Determines whether the rest is even seen. |
| **Value addition** | The 4/10 → 7/10 difference | Articles with clause numbers, named judgments, committee/commission reports, schemes, data, constitutional bodies. Specificity is the currency. |
| **Multi-dimensionality** | High | Two to four *distinct* angles rather than one angle repeated. Political / legal / federal / administrative / social / economic / ethical. |
| **Balance** | High (essential where "critically" appears) | Both sides argued fairly before a verdict. A one-sided answer to a "critically" question is a capped answer. |
| **Conclusion quality** | Underrated | Forward-looking, constructive, non-alarmist. Examiners remember the last three lines. |
| **Diagram / flow / table** | Bonus, cheap | Communicates in 15 seconds what costs 60 words, and visibly breaks the text. |

**The specificity rule, stated once:** *"The Supreme Court has held that federalism is a basic feature"* is an average line. *"In S.R. Bommai (1994), a nine-judge bench held federalism to be part of the basic structure and made Article 356 proclamations judicially reviewable"* is a scoring line. Same knowledge. Different marks.

## Part 3 — The anatomy of a 250-word answer

Total ≈ 250 words, ≈ 7–8 minutes. Proportions are a guide, not a straitjacket.

**1. Introduction — 2 to 3 lines, ~30 words. Never more.**
Pick *one* of these four openings, whichever the question invites:
- **Constitutional anchor** (default for Polity): open with the Article. *"Article 200 gives the Governor four options on a Bill passed by the State legislature…"*
- **Definition**: define the core term precisely, if the question turns on a concept.
- **Context/current hook**: a live development that frames the question.
- **Data or a report finding**: where the question is empirical.

Do **not** write "India is a vast and diverse country…". Generic openings signal a generic answer and waste 20 of your 250 words.

**2. Body — ~180 words, the entire scoring zone.**
- Break into **2 to 4 sub-headed blocks**, each 3–5 lines. Sub-headings should mirror the question's own words wherever possible — that is how you *prove* directive compliance to a skimming eye.
- **One idea per bullet**, each carrying at least one specific: an Article, a case, a committee, a scheme, a number.
- Where the directive demands balance, the sub-headings should physically show it — e.g. *"Arguments in favour" / "Concerns"*, or *"Merits" / "Limitations"*.
- Prefer **short paragraphs or bullets over long prose**, but never a bare list of one-word points — each bullet needs a reason or an example attached.

**3. Conclusion — 2 to 3 lines, ~35 words.**
Never a summary of what you just said. A conclusion must add a *direction*. Reliable closes:
- **Way forward** — a concrete, implementable suggestion (best default).
- **Balanced verdict** — where the question asked for judgement.
- **Committee/constitutional aspiration** — close on an unimplemented recommendation or a constitutional value.
Keep it **optimistic and constructive**. Examiners consistently reward a solution-oriented ending and penalise despair.

## Part 4 — Directive words: the highest-leverage table in this file

The tail word is an *instruction about structure*. Obeying it is worth more than any single fact.

| Directive | What is demanded | Structure it forces |
|---|---|---|
| **Discuss** | A reasoned debate across perspectives, ending in a position | Multiple viewpoints → arguments both ways → conclusion |
| **Discuss critically** | As above, with explicit judgement | Both sides + weighed verdict |
| **Examine** | Break the subject into parts and inspect each | Deconstruct into components → analyse each → overall assessment |
| **Critically examine** | Examine + judgement on merits and demerits | Components → strengths → weaknesses → balanced verdict |
| **Analyse** | Break down and show how parts relate and why they matter | Components + significance + inter-relations |
| **Critically analyse** | Analyse + critique the evidence and alternatives | Components → positives → negatives → weighed conclusion |
| **Evaluate** | Deliver a decision from weighed pros and cons | Criteria → evidence both ways → clear verdict |
| **Elucidate / Explain** | Make clear with detail and illustration | Definition → mechanism → examples. *Not* a debate |
| **Comment** | Express an informed opinion, backed by reasons | Facts → your reasoned view → balanced close |
| **Substantiate / Justify** | Support a claim with evidence | Claim → several pieces of hard evidence → affirmation |
| **To what extent / How far** | A *degree* judgement — never a yes/no | The extent it is true → the extent it is not → net verdict with the degree stated |
| **Critically** (as a prefix) | A fair judgement is compulsory | You may not take a single stance or ignore contrary facts |

**Two rules that cost marks constantly:**
- **"Critically" is never optional.** If it appears and your answer is one-sided, the answer is capped regardless of content.
- **"To what extent" must produce a degree, not a verdict.** End with words like "largely true, but limited by…" — not "yes" or "no".

## Part 5 — The GS-II Polity question archetypes, and the attack for each

Six shapes cover almost every Polity Mains question. Recognise the shape in the first ten seconds, and the skeleton is already decided.

**A. "Do we need this institution?" / Should X be reformed?**
*(e.g. do we need the office of Governor? Should the Speaker's role change?)*
→ **Constituent Assembly's original rationale → how practice diverged → judicial correction → unimplemented reform proposal → verdict.**
Value ammunition: CA debate arguments, the commission that recommended reform (Sarkaria, Punchhi, ARC), the case that narrowed the power.

**B. "Institution X vs Institution Y" / comparative**
*(Parliamentary vs Presidential; India vs UK; LS vs RS)*
→ **A crisp comparison table or parallel sub-headings, then a reasoned "why India chose as it did".**
Never write two disconnected descriptions — the marks are in the *contrast axis*.

**C. Judgment-driven / "in light of recent Supreme Court decisions"**
→ **Constitutional provision → the older position → the judgment that changed it → what remains unsettled.**
This is where the recent-judgment layer of the notes pays. Name bench strength where notable (9-judge, 7-judge).

**D. Federalism / Centre–State friction**
→ **Constitutional design → the specific friction points (legislative, administrative, fiscal) → judicial arbitration → cooperative-federalism way forward.**
Always cover *all three* limbs — legislative, administrative, fiscal — even briefly. That is the multi-dimensionality mark.

**E. Rights / DPSP / social-justice questions**
→ **Article + its judicial expansion → the competing right or limitation → the doctrine that reconciles them (proportionality, reasonable classification, basic structure) → live example.**

**F. Governance / process questions** (Budget, committees, privileges, anti-defection)
→ **Purpose of the mechanism → how it works → why it underperforms → reform.**
The "why it underperforms" block is where most candidates stop short and lose marks.

## Part 6 — The value-addition arsenal for GS-II

Keep these categories in mind while writing; aim for **at least four specifics** in a 250-word answer, spread across categories.

1. **Articles, with clause precision.** Art 75(1A) is the 15% cap; 75(5) is the six-month rule. Clause-level accuracy signals genuine study.
2. **Landmark judgments with the holding, not just the name.** Kesavananda (1973, 7:6), S.R. Bommai (1994), Minerva Mills (1980), Puttaswamy (2017, 9 judges), NJAC (2015), Shreya Singhal (2015), Sita Soren (2024, 7 judges), In re Article 370 (2023).
3. **Commissions & committees with their signature recommendation.** Sarkaria, Punchhi, 2nd ARC, Swaran Singh, Verma, Kovind Committee, Law Commission reports (e.g. the 267th on hate speech).
4. **Constituent Assembly debates.** Ambedkar's replies, Ayyangar on the Rajya Sabha as a revising chamber, K.T. Shah's warnings on the Governor. Highest-value and least-used material.
5. **Schemes and statutes mapped to provisions.** Janani Suraksha Yojana→Art 39, ISA→Art 51, RTE→Art 21-A/46, SMILE→Art 41.
6. **Live legislative and judicial developments.** 129th Amendment Bill (simultaneous elections), the 2025 assent-timeline saga, pending references before larger benches.
7. **Doctrines by name.** Basic structure, proportionality, reasonable classification, colourable legislation, pith and substance, repugnancy, eclipse, severability.
8. **A quotable line, sparingly.** One, at most, and only if it fits: *primus inter pares*; Austin's "conscience of the Constitution"; Ambedkar on constitutional morality having "to be cultivated".

### 6A — The case-lineage rule (mandatory for any judgment question)

If the question names a judgment, an Act struck down, or a doctrine, **open the body with the doctrinal arc in one line** before analysing the verdict. A judgment quoted in isolation reads like recall; a judgment placed in its lineage reads like understanding — and it is the single cheapest way to look like a serious student, because it costs about 25 words.

The arc has a fixed shape: *earlier position → what changed it → the case in question → what remains unsettled.*

- **Judicial appointments:** Art 124 says "consultation" → S.P. Gupta / First Judges Case (1981) read it as executive primacy → Second (1993) and Third (1998) Judges Cases converted it to "concurrence", creating the collegium → NJAC / **Fourth Judges Case** (2015) struck down the 99th Amendment → the Memorandum of Procedure is still unfinalised.
- **Amending power:** Shankari Prasad (1951) → Sajjan Singh (1965) → Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda (1973) → Indira Gandhi (1975) → Minerva Mills (1980) → Waman Rao (1981).
- **Article 356:** State of Rajasthan (1977) → S.R. Bommai (1994) → Rameshwar Prasad (2006) → Nabam Rebia (2016).
- **Privacy / personal liberty:** A.K. Gopalan (1950) → Kharak Singh (1962) → Maneka Gandhi (1978) → Puttaswamy (2017).
- **Reservation:** Champakam Dorairajan (1951) → Indra Sawhney (1992) → Nagaraj (2006) → Jarnail Singh (2018) → Janhit Abhiyan / EWS (2022).

Also **name the case by its familiar label** — "the Fourth Judges Case", "the Bommai principle" — alongside the full citation. Examiners recognise labels faster than full case names.

And **cover every Article the question's wording opens.** "Higher judiciary" means Art 124 *and* Art 217; "judicial independence" invites Art 50. Reading the question for the Articles it silently includes is worth more than adding a fifth judgment.

### 6B — The word-budget principle: value addition is *substitution*, never accumulation

This is the rule that governs all the advice above, and it must be applied to any review — including a review by another AI, a test-series evaluator, or this file itself.

**Every answer has a fixed budget: ~250 words and ~7½ minutes.** Any suggestion to "add a timeline", "add two diagrams", "add five more Articles", "expand the introduction" is only valid if it also says **what to remove**. Advice that adds without subtracting is not improvement — it is a request to overshoot the word limit, run out of time on later questions, or thin the argument until nothing is developed. All three lose more marks than the addition gains.

So the working method is:

1. Write the answer to the budget.
2. When reviewing, list what the answer would *gain* from each proposed addition.
3. For each, identify the **weakest existing 20–25 words** and trade them out. Typical cheap cuts: exhaustive membership/composition lists, generic comparative claims ("every major democracy..."), restatements of a criticism already made, and any sentence that explains rather than asserts-with-evidence.
4. If nothing is weak enough to cut, **the addition is not worth it.** Say so.
5. Record the trade explicitly, so the reasoning is reusable: *cut X, bought Y.*

A worked example lives in the M11 (NJAC, 2017) answer in `06 Basic Structure Doctrine.html` — the "why this was rewritten" box shows the exact swap: the NJAC composition list, a generic comparative line and a repeated criticism were cut to pay for the Fourth Judges Case label, the four-case lineage, Articles 217 and 50, the ratification fact, and two reform commissions. Same length, more marks.

**Corollary for judging feedback:** a reviewer who scores an answer harshly but recommends changes that cannot fit in the budget has diagnosed correctly and prescribed wrongly. Take the diagnosis, discard the prescription, and run the substitution yourself. Also discount reviewers who invent a marking rubric UPSC does not publish, or who treat 10/15 as a failure — per Part 1, that band is where toppers actually sit.

### 6C — The tolerance band, stated honestly

Sub-headings are part of the word count. A dense answer with four sub-headings and eight citations will not land on exactly 250. The working band is:

| Budget | Acceptable | Warning | Rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 words (15 marks) | 250–275 | 276–300 | above 300 |
| 200 words (12 marks) | 200–220 | 221–240 | above 240 |
| 150 words (10 marks) | 150–170 | 171–185 | above 185 |

**Ten-markers are where overshoot does the real damage.** A 150-word question answered in 230 words has consumed the time of a 15-marker while earning 10 marks — and in a 20-question paper that trade, repeated, is what leaves the last two questions unattempted. When trimming a 10-marker, shorten the sub-headings too: "Collective" beats "Collective responsibility" and costs nothing.

**Audit result for this project's 29 answers (July 2026).** Before the substitution pass, the mean overshoot was **+22%**, with six answers above +40%. After two passes: mean **+10%**, worst case +23%, none in the rewrite band. The content did not shrink — twelve new citations were added across the set. Only the padding went.

## Part 7 — Time, words and the exam-hall protocol

- **20 questions, 3 hours, ~150 marks.** That is **~9 minutes per question including thinking**; practically, **7–8 minutes writing** for a 250-word answer and **4–5 minutes** for a 150-word one.
- **Spend the first 30–45 seconds** identifying the directive and sketching 3 sub-headings in the margin. This is not lost time — it prevents the mid-answer collapse that costs far more.
- **Attempt every question.** An unattempted question scores zero; a mediocre attempt scores 3–5. Incompleteness is the most expensive error in Mains, exactly as skipping the tail of the Prelims paper was in your Week 2 test — the same failure pattern in a different exam.
- **Word discipline:** overshooting is not rewarded and steals time from later answers. If you are consistently overshooting, you are explaining instead of asserting-with-evidence.
- **If short of time:** write the skeleton — intro, sub-headings with one line each, conclusion. A visible structure with thin content beats half an answer that stops mid-sentence.

## Part 8 — Presentation rules (functional only)

- Sub-headings **underlined once**, in the same pen. Nothing else underlined except, at most, two or three genuinely critical terms per answer.
- **Leave a blank line between blocks.** White space is what makes the skim work.
- **One diagram, flowchart or small table** where the content is naturally sequential, comparative or multi-part. It saves words, earns marks, and breaks the page. Do not force one where the content is not structural.
- Legible hand beats beautiful hand. **Never** use multiple colours or draw boxes around everything.
- Number your points; do not let a block run beyond five lines.

## Part 9 — The failure modes to audit yourself against

1. **Answering the topic, not the question.** The most common and most expensive error. If the question says "critically examine the Governor's discretionary powers", an essay about the Governor's office scores poorly however good it is.
2. **Ignoring the directive.** Especially dropping the "critically".
3. **Generic openings and generic conclusions.** "India is a diverse country" / "Thus, much remains to be done."
4. **One-dimensional answers.** Four bullets all making the same point in different words.
5. **Assertion without evidence.** "This has led to misuse" — by whom, when, decided in which case?
6. **A wall of text.** Structurally invisible, therefore unmarked.
7. **Running out of time.** Perfection on the first five, silence on the last three.
8. **Pessimistic conclusions.** End constructively; the examiner is looking for a future administrator, not a critic.

## Part 10 — The self-evaluation rubric (score your own answer out of 10)

Apply honestly after every written answer. Anything under 6 should be rewritten once.

| # | Check | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Did I obey the exact directive (including "critically" / "to what extent")? | 2 |
| 2 | Is there a visible Intro–Body–Conclusion with sub-headings? | 1 |
| 3 | Did I open with an Article, definition, or sharp context — not filler? | 1 |
| 4 | At least four specifics (Article / case / committee / scheme / data)? | 2 |
| 5 | At least two genuinely distinct dimensions? | 1 |
| 6 | Is the answer balanced where balance was demanded? | 1 |
| 7 | Forward-looking, constructive conclusion? | 1 |
| 8 | Within word limit and finished in time? | 1 |

## Part 11 — How answers are written *in this project*

Every model answer produced for the notes must:
- State the **directive** and what it demands, before the answer begins.
- Show the **skeleton** (intro line, sub-headings, conclusion direction) so the structure is learnable separately from the content.
- Be written to **250 words for 15 marks / 150 for 10**, within the Part 6C tolerance band — not longer, because an unrealistic model answer teaches nothing about exam conditions.
- Open the body with the **doctrinal arc** if the question touches any row of the Part 6D index.
- Carry **at least four value-additions**, each tied back to a section of the note it belongs to, so revision is traceable.
- Include a **diagram or table suggestion** where the content is naturally structural.
- Carry a **budget-check box** stating the actual word count against the budget, and naming what was cut and what was bought. This is not decoration: seeing the trade is how the substitution habit transfers to your own writing.
- End with a **"what would cost you marks here"** note naming the specific trap for that question.

All 29 answers in this project were rebuilt to this standard in July 2026. Each one now displays its own budget check, so you can see at a glance whether a model answer is honest about exam conditions or quietly cheating on length.
