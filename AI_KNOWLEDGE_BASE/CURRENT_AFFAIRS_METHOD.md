# Current Affairs — Method

This file replaces `CURRENT_AFFAIRS_STRATEGY.md` and `UPSC_2027_MASTER_PROMPT.md`, both archived on 9 August 2026. It is built from what UPSC has actually asked, not from an idea of what thorough notes should look like.

Read it alongside `TEST_INTELLIGENCE.md` (question DNA) and `MAINS_ANSWER_CRAFT.md` (answer construction).

---

## Part 1 — What the exam actually does with current affairs

### The size of the prize

Current-affairs-linked questions in Prelims GS Paper I, by year:

| 2013 | 2014 | 2015 | 2016 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 29 | 20 | 23 | 28 | 26 | 18 | 24 | 25 | 26 | 24 | 24 | 26 |

Mean ≈ **24 of 100**, never below 18. In 2025 the directly-attributable count was around **13**, with more woven into Economy, Environment and Science questions.

So the realistic planning figure is **one question in five**. Large enough that neglecting it is fatal; small enough that spending half your day on it is also fatal. That balance is the whole design problem.

### The window

**Twelve months before the exam is the core; eighteen months is the safe outer edge.** Events older than that appear only when they have become static (a scheme now three years old is Polity/Economy, not current affairs).

### What the questions actually look like — this is the part that changes everything

The eight current-affairs questions identified from **Prelims 2025** asked about:

- **BIMSTEC** — number of member States, the year and name of the founding declaration, which States were founding members, which subsector India leads
- **Gandhi Peace Prize** — who sits on the selection jury
- **GAGAN** — what it is designed to provide, whether benefits extend beyond aviation
- **AI Action Summit, Paris 2025** — who co-chaired, which earlier summits it built on, who signed the declaration
- **International Years** — which year is designated for which theme
- **BRICS** — which country chaired the 16th Summit and where, whether Indonesia is a full member, the summit theme
- **Kho Kho World Cup** — host city, and the **scoreline** in the finals
- **Chess Olympiad** — who won and against whom; who holds the youngest-Grandmaster record

Look at what is common to all of these. **None asks for analysis.** Not one asks why BIMSTEC matters, what India should do about AI governance, or whether BRICS expansion is desirable. Every single one asks a **verifiable identity fact about a named entity**: founded when, members how many, hosted where, theme what, India's role which.

This is the single most important finding in this file, and it invalidates the previous system's design. A thirteen-layer analytical gauntlet applied to a daily news item produces exactly the wrong artefact: pages of argument where the exam wanted a date and a member count.

### The corollary — and the honest limit

If UPSC can ask the **scoreline of a Kho Kho final**, no coverage strategy reaches everything. Chasing completeness is how aspirants lose their whole day to current affairs.

The workable target is different: for every entity that enters the news in a significant way, know its **identity facts cold**. You will not get the Kho Kho scoreline. You will get BIMSTEC's founding declaration, BRICS' chair and theme, and the composition of the Gandhi Peace Prize jury — and those are the ones that recur.

**Prediction discipline, same as everywhere else in this system:** we predict at the level of *which entities and which fact-types*, never at the level of specific questions. Any note claiming to "guess what the examiner will ask" is overclaiming.

## Part 2 — Why the old system failed, recorded so it is not repeated

The archived approach asked for **ten topics a day, each through a thirteen-layer gauntlet**. The one note produced under it (4 August 2026) ran 1,770 words across 5 topics — about 350 words per topic — with layers present as headings and largely empty underneath. "Future Angle" appeared once in the document; the ten-statement generator was essentially absent.

That is not an execution failure. It is what the specification forces:

1. **The arithmetic is impossible.** Ten topics × thirteen layers done properly is a 15,000-word daily document. Nobody writes that daily, and more importantly nobody *revises* it. A current-affairs note has to survive to be re-read three or four times over twelve months, or it did no work at all.
2. **Layer-by-label generates filler.** "Statement 3: half correct. Statement 4: very tricky." Starting from the *form* of a statement rather than from a *fact* produces text that looks like practice questions and teaches nothing.
3. **It mixed two different products.** Prelims needs verifiable identity facts; Mains needs arguments, data and examples. A single document trying to be both is too shallow for one and too cluttered for the other.
4. **Daily granularity fights the exam.** UPSC asks about entities twelve months later. Filing by date makes an entity's facts unfindable — BIMSTEC's details are scattered across whichever days it appeared.
5. **No revision mechanism.** Notes were produced but never scheduled for re-reading. Production is not preparation.

## Part 3 — The design

### Two products, kept separate

| | **Prelims Fact Cards** | **Mains Material** |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | One card per **entity** | One entry per **theme** |
| Content | Verifiable identity facts | Argument, data, examples, way forward |
| Length | 60–110 words | 150–250 words |
| Test | Could this be a statement in an MCQ? | Could this be a paragraph in an answer? |
| Revised | Four times before the exam | Twice, plus before any Mains test |

Anything that fits neither is not written down. That rule is what keeps the volume survivable.

### Weekly files, a monthly hub, one dashboard card

```
UPSC-CSE/Current Affairs/2026/2026-08 August/
    Week 1 (Sun 02 - Sat 08 Aug).html
    Week 2 (Sun 09 - Sat 15 Aug).html
    Week 3 (Sun 16 - Sat 22 Aug).html
    Week 4 (Sun 23 - Sat 29 Aug).html
    Week 5 (Sun 30 Aug - Sat 05 Sep).html
    2026-08 August — Month Hub.html      ← the dashboard card points here
```

### The week runs Sunday to Saturday

This is not a formatting preference. **The weekly test is Sunday morning.** A week that closes on Saturday is read on Saturday evening and examined the next morning — nothing in the file is more than seven days old when it is tested. A Monday-to-Sunday week would place the test in the *middle* of the period it covers, which defeats the purpose.

| | |
|---|---|
| Week covers | **Sunday → Saturday** |
| File built | Saturday |
| Read | Saturday evening |
| Tested | Sunday morning |

**The month-boundary rule:** a week belongs to the month containing its **Sunday**. So Saturday 1 August 2026 sits in the week beginning Sunday 26 July and is filed under *July*; the week beginning Sunday 30 August is filed under *August* even though it ends in September. One rule, no ambiguity, and no week counted twice or lost between two months. Most months therefore hold four weeks; some hold five.

**The week is the working unit; the month is the container.** Three reasons, and they compound:

1. **It matches the test cycle.** Weekly UGSSP tests are what actually measure progress. A week's current affairs read on Saturday and tested on Sunday is material you can be examined on now, not in twelve months.
2. **It makes reading a block, not a bulk.** Six to ten topics is a twenty-five minute sitting. A whole month in one file is the thing you postpone.
3. **It fixes the sourcing problem.** Reconstructing a month at the end forces reliance on search summaries — which is exactly how unverified figures got into the first file. A week is close enough to check against PIB properly.

**The month hub** carries: links to its four weekly files, a consolidated entity index across the month, cross-week themes that no single week could show, and the month's full question set. It is what gets revised on the second and later passes; the weekly files are what get read the first time.

**Dashboard: one card per month**, opening the hub. Weeks are reached from inside the hub. This keeps the Current Affairs page to roughly twelve cards a year no matter how many weekly files exist beneath.

### Filed by entity, never by day

Within any file, the unit is the **entity**, not the date. UPSC asks about BIMSTEC twelve months later, not about a Tuesday.

### The topic card — one entry serving both papers

The July file separated Prelims fact cards from Mains material into different sections. At **weekly** scale, with six to ten topics, that separation costs more than it saves: you end up flipping between two places for the same entity. So the weekly card is **integrated but clearly zoned** — the factual half stays crisp, the argument half stays argument, and both sit under one heading.

**Zone 1 — Prelims (the facts).** Terse. Every line should be capable of becoming a statement in an MCQ.

- **WHAT** — what it is, one line
- **WHO** — ministry, body, composition, who chairs, who decides
- **WHEN** — launched, signed, approved, reference date; the instrument's name if it has one
- **WHERE** — headquarters, host city, States covered
- **HOW MUCH** — the outlay, the rank, the count; the single figure most likely to be asked
- **CONFUSABLE WITH** — the near-neighbour it gets swapped for

**Zone 2 — Static hook.** The Article, Schedule entry, or institutional category a linkage question will ride on. This is Part 5's requirement, applied per card rather than in a separate map.

**Zone 3 — Mains (the argument).** Prose, but disciplined.

- **WHY it matters** — the significance in two lines, not the news trigger
- **HOW it works** — the mechanism, where a mechanism is the point
- **PROS** — two or three, each a claim with evidence
- **CONS** — two or three; a card with only pros is propaganda, not preparation
- **WAY FORWARD** — one line, specific enough to be liftable into an answer

**The test for each zone:** could Zone 1 become an MCQ statement? Could Zone 3 become a paragraph in a 250-word answer? If a line does neither, cut it.

### Scope

**Per week:** six to ten topic cards, and six to ten questions. Readable in twenty-five minutes.

**Per month hub:** the consolidated index, three to five cross-week themes, and fifteen to twenty questions drawn across the weeks.

Selection rule, in priority order:

1. Anything with a **name, a founding year and a membership** — organisations, summits, indices, awards, exercises, missions. This category has supplied the largest share of recent CA questions.
2. **Government schemes and Bills** with their implementing ministry and the Article or Act they rest on.
3. **Reports and indices** — publisher, what it measures, India's rank *and* whether the rank moved.
4. **Judgments** — already covered by the Polity notes' recent-judgment layer; cross-reference rather than duplicate.
5. **Appointments and firsts** — only where the office is constitutional or the record is likely to recur.

Explicitly **out of scope**: routine political news, sports results other than the tournament's identity facts, and anything whose only content is that it happened.

### Subject balance — the defect the July file exposed

The first file under this method ran **6 legislation cards, 8 schemes, 2 international, 1 report** — and **nothing** on environment, science and technology, or the economy proper. That happened because the richest available source (PRS) is legislation-shaped, and I let the source choose the syllabus.

The exam's distribution is different. Economy carries roughly 18 questions, environment 13–15%, and science and technology a further significant block. So every week is checked against a floor:

| Bucket | Per week |
|---|---|
| Economy — RBI, budget, indices, trade, banking | at least 2 |
| Environment — species, conventions, climate, biodiversity | at least 1 |
| Science & Technology — space, health, biotech, digital | at least 1 |
| Polity, schemes and legislation | 2 to 4 |
| International — summits, organisations, agreements | 1 to 2 |
| Reports and indices | 1 |

If a week cannot fill the environment or sci-tech slot from that week's news, the slot is filled from a **standing backlog** of entities that recur — conventions, missions, indices — rather than left empty. An empty slot compounds across twelve months into a blind spot.

### Sources, in order of reliability

1. **PIB** — for schemes, cabinet decisions, official figures. Primary source; a scheme's own release beats any summary of it.
2. **PRS Legislative Research** — Monthly Policy Review and Bill summaries. Authoritative for legislation: Bill stages, clause-level changes, penalties, what an amendment actually does.
3. **The Hindu** and **Indian Express** — for context and for Mains argument.
4. The organisation's **own website** — for membership, founding instrument, headquarters. This is where BIMSTEC-type questions are actually answerable.
5. **Yojana** and **Kurukshetra** — for scheme depth and rural/development themes.

**PIB is not optional, and this had to be learnt the hard way.** The first file built under this method (July 2026) used PRS thoroughly and never opened a PIB release — everything on the scheme side came from search-engine summaries. That is how a wrong outlay figure gets into a card and then gets revised four times. PIB is the *primary* source for anything the Cabinet approves; a summary of a press release is not the press release.

**The PIB workflow, to be followed each month:**

- Go to **pib.gov.in → All Press Releases**, filter by **Cabinet / Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs** and by month. The listing page is JavaScript-driven, so a plain fetch returns only the current day — use a **site-restricted search** (`allowed_domains: pib.gov.in`) with the scheme name to reach the release page directly.
- **Record the PRID and the release date** on the card. `PRID 2284784, 15 July 2026` is a citation; "PIB" alone is not.
- Where a release gives a detail no summary carried — Semicon 2.0's Design pillar citing **105 startups** already developing chips — that detail is exactly the kind UPSC builds a question from. Summaries drop it; the release does not.

### Verification, non-negotiable

Carried over from the PYQ-integrity rule that governs the rest of this system:

- **No invented facts.** Every year, member count, rank and figure is checked against a source before it goes in the note.
- **Two tiers, marked on the card.** *Primary-verified* (PIB release or PRS) versus *search-summary only*. Anything in the second tier carries a visible **⚠ verify** flag and must not be committed to memory until checked. A month file may ship with second-tier cards — awareness has value — but never silently.
- **Where a figure is contested or changing** (member counts after an accession, ranks between editions), say so in the card rather than picking one.
- **Date every card** with when the fact was verified, because current affairs go stale.
- A card with an unverified number is worse than no card — it will be revised four times and learned wrong.

## Part 4 — Revision, which is the actual point

A note that is written and never re-read has cost time and returned nothing. The schedule is part of the method, not an afterthought:

| When | What | Which file |
|---|---|---|
| **Saturday evening**, before Sunday's test | Read fully, attempt the questions | The **weekly** file |
| End of the month | Read the cross-week themes, attempt the consolidated questions | The **month hub** |
| One month later | Zone 1 only, cover the answers | Hub |
| Three months later | Zone 1, tested against the entity index | Hub |
| Final eight weeks before Prelims | Zone 1 across all months, two months per sitting | Hubs only |

The weekly file is read **once, on time**. Everything after that runs on the hub — which is why the hub must consolidate rather than merely link.

The Mains material runs on a lighter cycle: revisit before any Mains test, and mine it for examples when writing answers.

**The month file must be revisable in under 30 minutes by the third pass.** If it cannot be, it is too long — which is the length constraint that keeps everything else honest.

## Part 5 — Static linkage: the half of the method Part 1 understates

Part 1 established that CA questions ask for verifiable identity facts. True — but incomplete, and the gap matters.

**UPSC rarely asks a current-affairs fact naked. It asks the fact riding on a static hook.** A candidate who read the newspaper but not the Constitution gets these wrong. So does a candidate who knows the Constitution but not the news. The question is engineered to require both, which is exactly what makes it a good discriminator — and why it is the shape UPSC keeps returning to.

The evidence is in this project's own test data. Week 3, **Q19** asked what happens when the Budget fails to pass the Lok Sabha — pure static Polity, triggered by a budget-season framing. **Q30** needed the RPA's constituency rule. **Q38** needed Article 163's discretionary sphere. None was answerable from news alone.

**So every fact card must carry its static anchor, and every month file carries a Static Linkage Map** — a table pairing each CA item with the Article, Schedule entry or institutional category a question will ride on.

**The three hooks that carry most linkage questions:**

1. **Which List?** Every scheme and Bill sits somewhere in the Seventh Schedule. The highest-value case is a **State List subject funded by the Union** — agriculture (Entry 14), sports (Entry 33), health, education. The answer is always **Article 282**, the power to make grants for any public purpose even outside legislative competence. That single Article explains every Central Sector and Centrally Sponsored Scheme, and it is a live federalism argument the Punchhi Commission engaged with.
2. **Constitutional, statutory, executive or corporate?** UPSC is constitutional (Arts 315–323). The Bar Council is statutory (Advocates Act, 1961). NITI Aayog and the NTA are executive creations. The NCGTC is a company. Questions exploit every boundary between these four.
3. **Which Article does it amend or rest on?** A Bill raising the Supreme Court's strength is Article 124(1), not the collegium's 124(2). An ordinance-replacing Bill drags in Article 123's six-week rule. A treaty headline drags in Article 253.

**Recurring linkage pairs worth pre-loading**, because they resurface every year: any treaty or summit → **Art 253** · any scheme on a State subject → **Art 282** · any Bill replacing an ordinance → **Art 123/213** · any Bill's House of origin → **Arts 110 and 117(1)** · any new body → the constitutional/statutory/executive test · any Concurrent List subject → **Art 254** repugnancy.

## Part 6 — Practice questions

Ten statements per topic, generated by label, is the thing that failed. Instead:

- **Fifteen to twenty MCQs per month, in two banks.** Roughly **half must be linkage questions** — a CA fact riding on a static provision — and the rest straight fact-recall from the cards. A month file with only recall questions is under-built, because it trains for the easier half of the paper.
- Formats follow `TEST_INTELLIGENCE.md` Part 1, weighted toward **Statements** and **How many**.
- Every question must be answerable **from a card or the linkage map in that month's file**. If it is not, either the question is out of scope or a card is missing a field.
- Presentation follows Part 4B: full option layout, answers hidden until a choice is committed, verdicts naming the option letter, explanations as bullets.
- Exceptions surfaced by CA questions are added to `99 Exceptions Ledger.html`, same as static ones.

## Part 7 — The note file, in order

1. **Header** — month, verification date, count of cards and themes
2. **Entity index** — every entity in the file as a jump list, so a fact can be found in seconds twelve months later
3. **Prelims Fact Cards**, grouped: Legislation · Schemes & Cabinet · International & Reports
4. **Static Linkage Map** — every item paired with the Article, Schedule entry or institutional category a question will ride on
5. **Mains Material** — four to six themes, each with the argument, two or three data points, and a way-forward line
6. **Practice questions** — fifteen to twenty in two banks, linkage first, then fact-recall, click-to-reveal
7. **30-second recall** — the month on one screen: names, years, numbers only

Same design system as the Polity notes, so it reads identically and needs no new habits.

---

## Part 8 — What this method deliberately does not do

- **It does not chase completeness.** Roughly 24 questions come from current affairs; you cannot cover the ground that produces all 24, and trying costs more than it returns.
- **It does not predict specific questions.** Entities and fact-types, never questions.
- **It does not produce a daily artefact.** Daily reading still happens — that is a habit, not a file. The written output is monthly.
- **It does not analyse where the exam wants facts.** Analysis goes in the Mains section, where it earns marks.
